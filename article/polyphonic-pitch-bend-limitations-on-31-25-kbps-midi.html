Polyphonic Pitch Bend Limitations on 31.25 kbps MIDI

This article examines the technical bottlenecks that occur when transmitting high-density polyphonic pitch bend data over legacy MIDI 1.0 hardware connections. It details how the strict 31.25 kbps baud rate limit creates severe throughput constraints, resulting in audible latency, timing jitter, control stepping, and dropped messages when handling complex, multi-channel performance data like MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE).

The Bandwidth Bottleneck of 31.25 kbps

The original MIDI specification defines an asynchronous serial protocol operating at a fixed rate of 31.25 kilobaud (31,250 bits per second). Each MIDI byte is framed by one start bit and one stop bit, requiring a total of 10 bits of transmission time per byte. Consequently, a standard 5-pin DIN MIDI line has a hard physical ceiling of:

\[\frac{31,250 \text{ bits/sec}}{10 \text{ bits/byte}} = 3,125 \text{ bytes per second}\]

Data Footprint of Polyphonic Pitch Bend

A standard MIDI Pitch Bend message consists of 3 bytes: one status byte indicating the command and channel, followed by two 7-bit data bytes providing 14-bit resolution (16,384 values).

When performing polyphonic pitch bends—such as with MPE controllers where each note is allocated its own MIDI channel—the data overhead scales linearly with the number of active voices:

  • Single Pitch Bend Message: 3 bytes (approximately 0.96 ms of transmission time)
  • Single Voice at 100 Hz update rate: \(3 \times 100 = 300 \text{ bytes/sec}\)
  • Five-Note Chord at 100 Hz update rate: \(5 \times 300 = 1,500 \text{ bytes/sec}\) (nearly 50% of the total bandwidth)
  • Five-Note Chord at 250 Hz update rate: \(5 \times 750 = 3,750 \text{ bytes/sec}\) (exceeds the line's maximum physical capacity)

Modern continuous expression sensors frequently scan at rates between 200 Hz and 1,000 Hz to ensure natural, responsive tracking. Sending unthrottled streams across multiple voices simultaneously will instantly saturate a 31.25 kbps line.

Technical Consequences of Line Saturation

When the rate of incoming performance data exceeds the 3,125-byte-per-second threshold, several critical failure modes occur:

1. Severe Latency and Timing Jitter

MIDI operates on a first-in, first-out (FIFO) queue. When pitch bend messages flood the serial buffer, time-critical messages like Note-On, Note-Off, and synchronization clocks (MIDI Clock) get trapped behind dense streams of pitch data. This introduces variable latency (jitter) of tens to hundreds of milliseconds, disrupting the rhythmic precision of a performance.

2. Zipper Noise and Reduced Resolution

To prevent buffer overflow, hardware controllers or interfaces must aggressively filter or "thin" the continuous pitch bend stream. Reducing the transmission rate forces the receiving synthesizer to interpolate larger gaps between values. If the synthesizer's internal smoothing is inadequate, the continuous glide degenerates into audible, stair-stepped increments known as zipper noise.

3. Buffer Overflows and Stuck Notes

Most vintage synthesizers and hardware interfaces contain small internal hardware receive buffers (often 64 to 256 bytes). A sudden burst of polyphonic pitch changes can easily overflow these input queues, causing the receiving device to drop packets. If a dropped packet happens to be a Note-Off command, the synthesizer will sustain the affected voice indefinitely, causing a "stuck note."

4. Channel Stripping and Stealing

Because MIDI 1.0 does not natively support per-note pitch bend within a single channel, polyphonic pitch bend relies on assigning separate channels to individual notes (MPE). When pitch bends are combined with simultaneous expressive controls like Aftertouch (Channel Pressure or Polyphonic Key Pressure) and CC74 (Brightness), the total data footprint triples, making it virtually impossible to transmit smooth multi-finger expressive gestures over standard 5-pin DIN cables without severe data reduction.
