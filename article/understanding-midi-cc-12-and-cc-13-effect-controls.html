Understanding MIDI CC 12 and CC 13 Effect Controls

MIDI Continuous Controller (CC) messages allow musicians and producers to manipulate parameters dynamically in both hardware synthesizers and software instruments. This article provides an overview of MIDI CC 12 (Effect Control 1) and MIDI CC 13 (Effect Control 2), detailing their defined specifications, how they differ from standard effect sends, and how to effectively map and automate them in music production.

In the standard MIDI 1.0 specification established by the MIDI Manufacturers Association (MMA), continuous controllers range from values 0 to 127. While some CC numbers are dedicated to fixed physical elements—such as CC 1 for the Modulation Wheel or CC 7 for Channel Volume—CC 12 and CC 13 are designated specifically as Effect Control 1 and Effect Control 2.

The primary function of CC 12 and CC 13 is to alter specific, internal parameters of an effect algorithm rather than just controlling the output volume or dry/wet mix. Standard effect depth messages (such as CC 91 for Reverb Send and CC 93 for Chorus Send) generally determine how much of a sound is sent to an effect bus. In contrast, CC 12 and CC 13 are designed to modulate the active behavior of the effect itself.

Common parameters assigned to CC 12 (Effect Control 1) and CC 13 (Effect Control 2) include:

  • Delay Parameters: Modulating delay time, feedback percentage, or ping-pong width.
  • Modulation Effects: Controlling the rate, depth, or resonance of a phaser, flanger, or chorus.
  • Distortion and Drive: Adjusting input gain, clipping thresholds, or tone shaping on an overdrive circuit.
  • Filter Modules: Controlling cutoff frequency or resonance when the filter functions as an insert effect rather than an instrument's main voice filter.

Because MIDI CC 12 and CC 13 are defined as generic effect controls, modern hardware synthesizers and virtual studio technology (VST) plugins do not enforce a single, rigid assignment. Sound designers and producers frequently map these controllers to custom macro knobs. This allows hardware faders, rotary knobs, or DAW automation lanes to manipulate complex effect chains in real time during a performance or mix session.

When higher precision is required, both CC 12 and CC 13 can be paired with their corresponding Least Significant Byte (LSB) controllers—CC 44 and CC 45, respectively. This 14-bit pairing expands the resolution from the standard 128 discrete steps (0–127) to 16,384 steps, eliminating audible "zipper noise" or stepping artifacts during smooth parameter sweeps.
