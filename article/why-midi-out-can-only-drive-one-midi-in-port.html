Why MIDI Out Can Only Drive One MIDI In Port

The MIDI 1.0 specification mandates that a single MIDI Out port must connect to only one MIDI In port because of the electrical design of the interface, specifically its use of a dedicated 5mA current loop and optoisolators. Unlike voltage-based protocols that can support multiple receivers in parallel, MIDI relies on a precisely budgeted electrical current to trigger an optical switch inside the receiver. Splitting a single output passively starves the circuit of necessary current, degrading the digital signal and causing data corruption, timing errors, and stuck notes.

The 5mA Current Loop Standard

Standard MIDI hardware does not transmit signals using traditional logic-level voltages. Instead, it uses a 5-milliamp (mA) current loop. The transmitter (MIDI Out) acts as a current source, passing current through the cable when transmitting a logical "0" (space) and cutting the current when transmitting a logical "1" (mark).

This current-driven approach was chosen in the early 1980s to prioritize noise immunity and eliminate ground loops over long cable runs in stage and studio environments. The standard specifies 220-ohm resistors at both the transmitter and receiver ends, strictly calculated to establish the required current under a standard 5-volt supply.

Optocoupler Activation Requirements

Every compliant MIDI In port contains an optocoupler—an internal infrared LED paired with a phototransistor. This design guarantees galvanic isolation between connected devices, preventing ground hum and electrical interference.

For the receiver to detect an incoming signal, the current loop must deliver enough power to illuminate the optocoupler's internal LED quickly and completely. The component values specified by the MIDI Manufacturers Association are tuned to deliver approximately 5mA through that single LED.

Why Passive Splitting Fails

If a passive "Y-cable" is used to send one MIDI Out into two MIDI Ins, the electrical loads are placed in parallel or series:

  • Current Starvation: In a parallel split, the available current is divided between the two receiving optocouplers. Rather than receiving 5mA, each receiver receives roughly 2.5mA or less, which is frequently insufficient to saturate the optocoupler's internal phototransistor.
  • Rise and Fall Time Degradation: Reduced current severely slows the switching speed of the optocoupler. MIDI transmits serial data at a fixed baud rate of 31.25 kbps. Slow transitions warp the square-wave digital signal, resulting in jitter, framing errors, and missing bytes.
  • Voltage Drop: In series configurations, the forward voltage drop across multiple optocoupler LEDs exceeds the voltage headroom provided by the 5V transmitter, preventing the circuit from functioning at all.

The Active Alternative: MIDI Thru

To drive multiple instruments from a single data source without violating electrical constraints, the specification defines the MIDI Thru port.

A MIDI Thru port is not a passive tap; it is an active, buffered repeater. It takes the digital signal received at the MIDI In port, processes it through internal logic buffers, and drives a brand-new 5mA current loop transmitter dedicated to the next device. For setups requiring star topologies rather than daisy chains, dedicated, powered MIDI Thru boxes (active splitters) must be used to ensure each receiving device gets its own fully powered, isolated current loop.
