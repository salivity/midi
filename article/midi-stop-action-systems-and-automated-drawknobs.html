MIDI Stop Action Systems and Automated Drawknobs

A MIDI stop action system on an electronic organ bridges digital performance data with physical organ controls, allowing stops to be read, manipulated, and physically moved via MIDI messages. This system combines electronic sensing, microcontrollers, and electromechanical actuators to ensure that the physical state of the console's drawknobs or tilt tabs matches the internal registration of the organ or virtual pipe organ software. Through dedicated MIDI commands, external sequencers, software, or internal combination actions can physically reposition these controls in real time, providing seamless visual feedback and physical synchronization during a performance.

What is a MIDI Stop Action System?

In pipe and electronic organs, stops control which pipe ranks or digital audio voices are active. While modern electronic organs generate sound digitally, many traditionalists and concert organists prefer the mechanical tactile experience of authentic physical drawknobs or tab switches.

A MIDI Stop Action System is the hardware and firmware infrastructure that manages these physical controls electronically. Instead of purely mechanical linkages, the system uses digital sensors to detect when an organist moves a knob by hand, converts that physical movement into a standard MIDI command, and uses electromagnetic actuators to physically move the knob when commanded by an internal preset system, computer software (such as Hauptwerk or GrandOrgue), or an external MIDI sequencer.

The Mechanical Hardware: Stop Action Magnets (SAMs)

At the heart of an automated drawknob is a Stop Action Magnet (SAM) unit. Each drawknob is attached to a dual-action mechanism containing:

  • Solenoids or Coils: Most modern units utilize two independent electromagnetic coils (or a reversible magnetic armature with bi-polar drive). One coil pulls the drawknob "On" (outward toward the player), and the other pushes it "Off" (inward toward the console).
  • Sensors/Contacts: Reed switches, optical sensors, or Hall effect sensors monitor the physical position of the knob shaft. This allows the system to determine whether a stop has been engaged or disengaged manually without mechanical friction or wear.
  • Driver Boards: Because MIDI operates on low-voltage, low-current signals (typically 3.3V or 5V logic), it cannot directly power the heavy electromagnetic coils. Dedicated driver boards equipped with power MOSFETs or Darlington transistors interface between the logic circuits and the 12V to 24V DC power supply required to fire the magnets.

How Drawknobs are Automated via MIDI Commands

Automating a drawknob requires a bidirectional flow of MIDI data: reading the physical state and commanding physical motion.

1. Mapping MIDI Messages to Stops

Stop action systems are configured to associate each physical stop with a distinct MIDI message. Depending on the manufacturer and the organ software:

  • Note On/Note Off: Many systems assign each stop to a specific MIDI note number on a dedicated MIDI channel. Sending a Note On event (with velocity > 0) commands the stop to turn on, while Note Off (or Note On with velocity 0) commands it to turn off.
  • Control Change (CC): Alternatively, continuous controller messages can be assigned to individual stops, where CC value 127 engages the stop and CC value 0 disengages it.
  • System Exclusive (SysEx): Some commercial organ builders use proprietary SysEx strings to update multiple stops simultaneously in combination actions.

2. Signal Decoding and Current Pulse Delivery

When a MIDI command reaches the stop action controller:

  1. Decoding: The onboard microcontroller reads the incoming MIDI stream and identifies the command directed to a specific stop index.
  2. Current Pulsing: To avoid burning out the electromagnetic coils, the driver board delivers a brief, high-current pulse—typically lasting only 50 to 150 milliseconds—to the appropriate coil.
  3. Physical Actuation: The resulting magnetic field rapidly pulls or pushes the armature, mechanically throwing the drawknob into the corresponding position.

3. Loop Prevention and State Synchronization

A critical feature of automated drawknobs is loop prevention. When an incoming MIDI command fires the magnet to pull a knob outward, the knob’s internal position sensor will detect that motion. Without intelligent logic, the sensor would interpret this as a manual action and transmit a redundant MIDI message back into the network, creating an infinite feedback loop.

To solve this, the stop action system’s firmware temporarily suppresses its own MIDI output for that specific stop during and immediately following an automated actuation, ensuring the console stays synchronized without data flooding.
