Syncing Roland TB-303 with MIDI Using Sync Converters

Early electronic music producers faced a major integration challenge in the 1980s and 1990s because the Roland TB-303 Bass Line relied on Roland’s proprietary DIN Sync protocol rather than the emerging MIDI standard. To integrate the 303 into modern sequencing setups, artists relied on specialized hardware sync converters to bridge the two technologies. These devices translated digital MIDI timing data into analog electrical clock pulses, allowing the TB-303’s internal sequencer to lock into step with modern drum machines, computers, and master sequencers.

The Protocol Divide: DIN Sync vs. MIDI

Released in 1981, the Roland TB-303 predated the official adoption of the MIDI standard in 1983. Instead of digital serial communication, Roland equipped the 303 with DIN Sync (specifically DIN Sync24). While DIN Sync used the same 5-pin DIN connector later adopted by MIDI, the two protocols were completely incompatible:

  • DIN Sync24: An analog synchronization standard that uses discrete electrical voltages. It sends a continuous stream of square-wave clock pulses at a rate of 24 pulses per quarter note (PPQN) across Pin 3, along with a constant +5V direct current signal on Pin 1 to signal "Start/Run" (dropping to 0V for "Stop"). Pin 2 serves as the electrical ground.
  • MIDI: A digital serial communications protocol running at 31.25 kbaud. It transmits timing information via hex-encoded System Real-Time messages, including Timing Clock (0xF8), Start (0xFA), and Stop (0xFC).

Plugging a standard MIDI cable directly between a MIDI output and the TB-303's DIN Sync input did not work and could potentially damage the circuitry due to voltage mismatches.

The Role of Hardware Sync Converters

To solve this incompatibility, artists turned to dedicated bidirectional or MIDI-to-DIN converters. Popular period units included the Roland SBX-80, Roland MSQ-700, Korg KMS-30, and later, compact standalone converters from companies like Philip Rees (such as the MDS) and Doepfer (the MSY2).

The converter acted as a real-time translator:

  1. Digital Reception: The converter monitored the MIDI In port for incoming MIDI Real-Time Clock bytes (0xF8). Because MIDI clock also operates at an internal resolution of 24 pulses per quarter note, the timing resolution mapped 1:1 with Sync24.
  2. Voltage Generation: Inside the converter, a microprocessor or logic circuit detected each MIDI clock byte and instantly generated a corresponding +5V analog square wave pulse sent out through Pin 3 of its DIN Sync jack.
  3. Transport Control: When the master sequencer transmitted a MIDI Start command (0xFA), the converter pulled Pin 1 high to +5V, telling the TB-303 to initiate playback. A MIDI Stop command (0xFC) dropped Pin 1 back to 0V, immediately halting the 303.

Studio Implementation and Workflow

Setting up the TB-303 within an early electronic music studio required a specific hardware routing order:

  1. Master Clock: A central sequencer—such as an Atari ST running Cubase or Notator, an Akai MPC, or a Roland TR-909—was designated as the master MIDI clock.
  2. Signal Routing: A MIDI cable ran from the master sequencer's MIDI Out into the converter's MIDI In.
  3. DIN Connection: A standard 5-pin DIN cable connected the converter's DIN Sync Out to the TB-303’s Sync In jack.
  4. Machine Configuration: The TB-303 was switched to "Pattern Play" mode. In this mode, inserting a plug into the Sync In jack automatically disabled the unit's internal tempo potentiometer and configured the internal sequencer to advance only upon receiving external pulses.

Once linked, hitting "Play" on the master sequencer instantly started the TB-303 in perfect synchronization. This hybrid approach allowed acid house and techno pioneers to compose complex, multi-instrument arrangements on digital MIDI workstations while retaining the unique swing, accent, and slide behaviors native to the TB-303's onboard analog sequencer.
