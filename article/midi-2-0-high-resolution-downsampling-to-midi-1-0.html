MIDI 2.0 High-Resolution Downsampling to MIDI 1.0

This article examines what happens when high-resolution MIDI 2.0 data streams pass through a conversion downsampler into legacy MIDI 1.0 environments. It covers the mechanical and perceptual impacts of bit-depth truncation, data rate throttling, the translation of per-note expressions, and the resulting audio artifacts like parameter stepping.

Bit-Depth Truncation and Quantization

MIDI 2.0 introduces dramatically higher resolution across almost all data parameters. Velocity, pitch bend, and Continuous Controllers (CCs) expand from legacy ranges up to 16-bit or 32-bit values, yielding over 4 billion discrete steps compared to MIDI 1.0’s standard 7-bit (128 steps) or 14-bit (16,384 steps using CC pairs).

When a MIDI 2.0 signal reaches a downsampler:

  • Bit-Shifting Occurs: The converter typically takes the most significant bits (MSB) and discards the least significant bits (LSB). For example, a 32-bit CC value is scaled down to a 7-bit value by bit-shifting right by 25 bits.
  • Loss of Nuance: Micro-dynamics, subtle expressive pitch variations, and ultra-smooth controller curves are permanently flattened.
  • Zipper Noise: Parameter sweeps (such as filter cutoffs or volume fades) that were completely continuous in MIDI 2.0 can re-introduce audible stepping or "zipper noise" when quantized back to the 128 discrete values of standard MIDI 1.0.

Bandwidth Throttling and Decimation

Legacy MIDI 1.0 hardware connections (standard 5-pin DIN) are constrained to an asynchronous serial baud rate of 31.25 kbps. Because MIDI 2.0 can transmit dense, continuous streams of high-resolution messages, simply translating 32-bit values into equivalent MIDI 1.0 messages would immediately flood legacy buffers and saturate the transmission line.

To avoid buffer overflows, dropped notes, and severe latency spikes, downsamplers apply message decimation:

  • Time-Based Filtering: The downsampler drops intermediate updates, only sending a MIDI 1.0 message when a defined time interval has elapsed or when the value changes beyond a designated threshold.
  • Throughput Capping: The density of continuous controller information is intentionally lowered to fit within the restrictive bandwidth of the legacy protocol.

Handling of Per-Note Controllers

One of MIDI 2.0's defining capabilities is native per-note expression (per-note pitch bend, per-note timbre, and per-note pressure) operating over a single channel. Standard MIDI 1.0 architectures apply controller data to the entire channel rather than individual notes.

When downsampling per-note data to legacy MIDI 1.0:

  • MPE Mapping (Best Case): If the downsampler supports MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE), it redistributes individual notes and their associated controller curves across multiple legacy MIDI channels (Channels 2–16), reserving Channel 1 for global messages.
  • Flattening or Discarding (Worst Case): If converting to standard single-channel MIDI 1.0, per-note data must either be converted into channel-wide messages (causing one note's modulation to erroneously affect all other sustained notes on that channel) or stripped completely.

Metadata and Attribute Stripping

MIDI 2.0 Universal MIDI Packets (UMP) contain rich metadata, including note attributes (such as articulation flags, tuning standards, and acoustic modeling parameters) and bidirectional profile configurations. Because MIDI 1.0 devices have no native data fields to read or process these attributes, downsamplers strip this information entirely, preserving only the fundamental Note-On, Note-Off, and standard controller commands.
