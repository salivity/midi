Current Sinking in MIDI Transmitter Driver Circuits

This article explains the mechanics and electrical role of current sinking within an authorized MIDI transmitter driver circuit. By utilizing a 5 mA switched current-loop architecture, standard MIDI transmitters do not supply driving voltage to register a signal; instead, they complete a path to ground to actuate an optocoupler at the receiving end. Below is a detailed breakdown of how the driver interfaces with the transmission loop, how the switching logic functions, and why this topology is specified in the official MIDI standard.

The MIDI Current Loop Interface

The physical layer of the MIDI specification (MIDI 1.0 Electrical Specification) relies on a point-to-point, 5 mA current loop rather than a direct voltage-level signaling bus. In a standard 5-pin DIN MIDI Out port, connection is established through two signal pins:

  • Pin 4 (Current Source): Connected to the system supply voltage (traditionally +5V, or +3.3V in updated low-voltage specifications) via a current-limiting pull-up resistor.
  • Pin 5 (Current Sink): Connected to the output of an inverting logic buffer or open-collector driver through an identical current-limiting protection resistor.
  • Pin 2 (Shield): Connected to earth/chassis ground at the transmitter only, providing RF shielding without bridging device grounds.

The circuit at the MIDI In receiver consists solely of a reverse-protection diode and the infrared LED of an optoisolator placed across Pin 4 and Pin 5.

The Operation of Current Sinking

In electronic driver design, "current sinking" occurs when an active device (such as a bipolar junction transistor or MOSFET) provides a path to ground for current supplied by an external source. In an authorized MIDI transmitter, the sinking action is controlled by the Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter (UART) serial output.

1. Logic 0 (Active / Space State)

Serial MIDI communication is active-low. When the UART outputs a logic 0 (such as during a start bit or data bit):

  • The driver circuit—typically implemented using high-speed inverting gates (such as a 74HCT14) or an open-collector driver (such as a 7407 or discrete NPN/N-channel transistor)—switches its output on Pin 5 to a low-impedance state linked to ground.
  • This drops the voltage on Pin 5 near 0V, establishing a completed circuit loop between Pin 4 (+Vcc) and Pin 5 (Ground).
  • Current flows out of Pin 4, travels through the cable, forward-biases the receiver's optocoupler LED, returns through the cable into Pin 5, and sinks directly into ground via the transmitter’s active switching element.
  • The glowing LED turns on the phototransistor inside the receiver, reconstructing the logic 0 at the receiving UART.

2. Logic 1 (Idle / Mark State)

When the UART line is at idle, or transmitting a logic 1:

  • The driver gate or transistor switches off, presenting a high impedance (tri-state or high-voltage pull) on Pin 5.
  • With no functional path to ground, current cannot flow through the loop (\(I = 0\text{ mA}\)).
  • The optocoupler LED at the receiver remains dark, keeping the receiver's input pulled high to its local power rail, which registers as a logic 1.

Symmetrical Resistance and Impedance Matching

Official MIDI hardware design guidelines mandate matching series resistors on both the source pin (Pin 4) and the sink pin (Pin 5). In a classic 5V circuit, both resistors are typically \(220\ \Omega\).

When the driver sinks current, these resistors evenly distribute the voltage drop across the loop, accounting for the forward voltage drop of the receiver's optocoupler LED (roughly 1.2V to 1.7V) and ensuring that approximately 5 mA flows through the circuit. Sinking the current through a matched resistance on the return leg ensures symmetrical cable impedance, absorbs back-EMF, and prevents high-current short circuits if incorrect or faulty cables are connected to the DIN jack.

Why Current Sinking Is Specified

  1. Galvanic Isolation: Sinking the loop return through an isolated ground path inside the transmitter preserves the galvanic isolation boundary created by the receiver's optocoupler. Because the receiver does not connect to Pin 2, no ground loop is formed between the interconnected instruments, eliminating 50/60 Hz hum and interference.
  2. Noise Immunity: Current-mode sinking significantly reduces vulnerability to electrostatic discharge and capacitive coupling over long cable runs. Interference that might corrupt high-impedance voltage states does not readily generate the 5 mA necessary to trigger the optocoupler.
  3. Failsafe Disconnection: If a cable is unplugged, the broken sink path mimics an idle logic state (1), preventing the receiver from registering corrupted data or continuous false triggers.
