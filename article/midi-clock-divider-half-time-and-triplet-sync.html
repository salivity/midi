MIDI Clock Divider: Half-Time and Triplet Sync

This article explains how hardware MIDI clock dividers process standardized tempo pulses to produce modified rhythmic timings. It covers the baseline structure of MIDI clock signals, the internal counting and division mechanisms used by hardware units, and the exact mathematical processes required to convert standard tempo data into half-time and triplet sync outputs for electronic instruments.

The Foundation: The Standard MIDI Clock

Standard MIDI tempo synchronization is transmitted as a stream of real-time status bytes (0xF8). This stream operates at a fixed resolution of 24 Pulses Per Quarter Note (PPQN). Regardless of the tempo, a master MIDI device sends exactly 24 0xF8 bytes to represent the duration of one quarter note.

A hardware MIDI clock divider receives these bytes via a MIDI IN port through an optocoupler, routing the serial data into a microprocessor, microcontroller, or discrete logic circuit. The device does not alter the incoming tempo; instead, it uses internal counters to monitor the incoming 24 PPQN pulse stream and triggers secondary outputs only when specific pulse counts are reached.

How Half-Time Sync Signals Are Generated

To create a half-time rhythm, the hardware must halve the frequency of the pulse triggers relative to the standard master tempo.

  1. Pulse Counting: In standard time, an output trigger representing a quarter note fires every 24 incoming clock ticks. To produce a half-time quarter note (effectively a half note in standard time), the hardware's internal counter is programmed to accumulate twice the standard count—48 pulses—before generating an output trigger.
  2. Frequency Division: If the unit outputs an analog sync signal (such as a 5-volt gate pulse for modular synthesizers or drum machines), a digital flip-flop or software counter tracks the pulses. By setting the division factor to \(2\times\), the circuit delays every sync pulse by a factor of two, causing connected slave gear to step forward at half the master tempo.
  3. MIDI Regeneration: If the divider outputs MIDI rather than analog pulses, it intercepts the 24 PPQN stream and transmits a filtered or reconstructed clock stream. It can either suppress every other 0xF8 byte to slow down devices that accept lower PPQN resolutions, or dynamically scale and re-emit a full 24 PPQN stream stretched over twice the time period using an internal timer locked to the incoming rate.

How Triplet Sync Signals Are Generated

The 24 PPQN standard was historically chosen because the number 24 is cleanly divisible by both 2 and 3, which allows hardware dividers to derive integer-based triplet values without introducing timing drift or phase jitter.

To generate triplet divisions, the hardware divides standard note values into three equal parts by counting specific subsets of the 24-pulse cycle:

  • Eighth-Note Triplets (1/8T): A standard quarter note equals 24 pulses. To fit three notes into the space of a quarter note, the divider calculates \(24 \div 3 = 8\). The hardware counter resets and fires an output pulse every 8 incoming clock ticks.
  • Quarter-Note Triplets (1/4T): A standard half note equals 48 pulses. To fit three notes into the space of two quarter notes, the divider calculates \(48 \div 3 = 16\). The hardware fires an output pulse every 16 incoming ticks.
  • Sixteenth-Note Triplets (1/16T): A standard eighth note equals 12 pulses. Dividing this by three (\(12 \div 3 = 4\)) means the hardware triggers a sync pulse every 4 incoming clock ticks.

Output Formatting and Pulse Shaping

Once the divider's internal logic calculates the target pulse threshold (e.g., 8 ticks for an eighth-note triplet, or 48 ticks for half-time), the hardware formats the output for downstream gear:

  • Analog Trigger/Gate Outputs: For Eurorack and semi-modular synthesizers, a digital output pin goes high for a fixed duration (typically 5 to 10 milliseconds) to generate a distinct analog pulse, then returns to 0V until the next counter threshold is met.
  • DIN Sync / Clock Outputs: Devices that output variable pulse-per-quarter-note signals (such as 2 PPQN, 24 PPQN, or 48 PPQN) scale their counter algorithms so the outgoing pulses match the electrical requirements of the receiving hardware.

By relying on simple integer division of the baseline 24 PPQN data stream, the hardware produces perfectly quantized half-time and triplet rhythmic intervals without latency or cumulative timing errors.
