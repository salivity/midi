Why MIDI Cables Cannot Transmit Digital Audio

Standard MIDI 1.0 cables cannot transmit digital audio waveforms because the MIDI standard was designed exclusively as an event-based control protocol, not an audio streaming medium. While audio cables carry the actual sound vibrations or digitized acoustic waveforms, MIDI cables carry only numerical instructions detailing how a musical performance is executed. Due to extreme limitations in data transmission speed, specialized optoisolated hardware, and the absence of a continuous audio framing protocol, standard MIDI 1.0 hardware cannot support the heavy bandwidth demands of real-time digital audio.

Event Data vs. Audio Waveforms

Digital audio is a continuous representation of acoustic sound pressure waves. To capture and stream digital audio accurately using standard Pulse Code Modulation (PCM), an analog signal must be sampled tens of thousands of times per second. For example, standard CD-quality audio requires 44,100 samples per second per channel, with each sample represented by 16 or 24 bits of data.

In contrast, MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) transmits symbolic performance instructions rather than sound. When a key is struck on a MIDI controller, the instrument sends a tiny packet of binary data containing three pieces of information: a command byte (such as "Note On"), a data byte indicating pitch, and a data byte indicating velocity (how hard the key was hit). The MIDI cable functions like digital sheet music, relaying the instructions to an external sound module, synthesizer, or computer, which then generates the sound independently.

The Bandwidth Bottleneck

The physical specification of MIDI 1.0 defines a fixed transmission rate of exactly 31.25 kilobits per second (kbps). This transmission speed was established in 1983 to provide reliable, low-latency communication over budget-friendly hardware without overloading microprocessors of the era.

To understand why this bandwidth makes digital audio impossible:

  • Uncompressed Stereo Digital Audio (16-bit, 44.1 kHz): Requires approximately 1,411 kbps of continuous throughput.
  • Standard MIDI 1.0 Connection: Caps out at an absolute maximum of 31.25 kbps.

Standard uncompressed stereo digital audio requires roughly 45 times more bandwidth than a standard MIDI 1.0 line can physically supply. Even highly compressed, low-fidelity audio formats demand far more sustained bandwidth than a 31.25 kbps connection can maintain in real time.

Hardware and Electrical Constraints

The physical design of standard 5-pin DIN MIDI hardware is electrically incapable of sustaining high-speed digital audio signals. The standard specifies a 5-milliamp current loop driven through an optocoupler at the receiving end (the MIDI IN port).

Optocouplers convert electrical signals into light pulses and back into electrical signals. This design creates galvanic isolation, which deliberately breaks electrical ground connections to prevent ground loops, hum, and noise between different mains-powered instruments. However, the legacy optocouplers specified for standard MIDI 1.0 are slow, narrow-bandwidth components. Attempting to force high-frequency megabit data streams through these components results in immediate signal rounding, rise-time degradation, and total data corruption.

Absence of Clocking and Streaming Protocols

Digital audio requires strict, jitter-free timing synchronization, usually provided by word clocks or embedded clock signals (such as those used in S/PDIF, AES/EBU, or ADAT connections), to convert incoming digital numbers back into continuous analog voltages without distortion.

MIDI 1.0 is an asynchronous serial protocol. It transmits individual 8-bit bytes surrounded by start and stop bits whenever an action occurs. It possesses no packet structures, data buffers, sample headers, or continuous clock synchronization designed to reconstruct sequential audio waveforms. Because the architecture lacks both the physical bandwidth and the software protocols required for digital audio, standard MIDI 1.0 remains strictly limited to transmitting symbolic control information.
