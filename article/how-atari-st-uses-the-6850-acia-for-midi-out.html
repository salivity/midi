How Atari ST Uses the 6850 ACIA for MIDI Out

The Atari ST was the first mainstream personal computer to feature built-in Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) ports, establishing it as a legendary standard in music production. This article explores how the Atari ST generates MIDI Out signals using the onboard Motorola 6850 Asynchronous Communications Interface Adapter (ACIA), covering the hardware clocking configuration, the role of the 6850 controller, and the discrete analog circuitry required to produce standard MIDI-compliant current loops.

The Motorola 6850 ACIA

The Motorola MC6850 ACIA is a dedicated peripheral chip designed to convert parallel data from the computer's CPU into asynchronous serial data (and vice versa). In the Atari ST, two 6850 chips reside on the motherboard: one handles keyboard and mouse communication, while the second is dedicated entirely to the MIDI In and MIDI Out ports.

The CPU communicates with the MIDI ACIA across the system data bus via memory-mapped input/output. When software transmits a MIDI message, the Motorola 68000 processor writes bytes to the 6850's transmit data register. The ACIA formats these bytes with standard asynchronous framing: one start bit, eight data bits, and one stop bit, with no parity.

Achieving the 31.25 kbaud MIDI Clock Rate

The MIDI 1.0 specification mandates a strict serial transmission rate of 31.25 kilobaud (kbaud) with a tolerance of ±1%. Standard computer communication ports of the era did not natively support this rate, but the ST achieves it through hardware frequency division:

  1. Clock Source: The master system oscillator feeds clock-divider logic within the ST's custom Glue/MMU chipset.
  2. ACIA Clock Input: The chipset outputs a dedicated 500 kHz clock signal routed directly to the Transmit Clock (\(TxCLK\)) and Receive Clock (\(RxCLK\)) pins of the MIDI 6850 ACIA.
  3. Internal Division: During system initialization, the Atari TOS operating system configures the 6850's internal control register to use a divide-by-16 prescaler mode.

Dividing the incoming 500 kHz clock by 16 yields exactly 31.25 kHz:

\[\frac{500\text{ kHz}}{16} = 31.25\text{ kbaud}\]

This hardware-level frequency matching allows the ACIA to transmit bit streams at the required rate without CPU-intensive software timing loops.

The MIDI Out Driver Circuitry

The serial data stream leaves the 6850 ACIA via its Transmit Data (\(TxD\)) pin as a standard TTL-level logic signal (0V to 5V). Because MIDI is designed as a 5-milliamp (mA) current loop interface rather than a direct voltage interface, the raw TTL output cannot drive external MIDI equipment directly.

To convert this TTL signal into a robust, noise-immune current loop, the ST routes the signal through a driver stage before sending it to the standard 5-pin DIN connector:

  • Inverter/Buffer Stage: The \(TxD\) pin connects to an internal open-collector inverter (part of a 74LS04 or 7406 logic package, depending on the motherboard revision). This inverter buffers the signal and provides the necessary current sinking capability to handle external cables.
  • Current-Limiting Resistors: Following the standard MIDI specification, the circuit incorporates two 220-ohm resistors. One resistor connects Pin 4 of the MIDI Out connector to the +5V supply rail. The other 220-ohm resistor connects Pin 5 of the connector to the output of the inverter.
  • Pin 2 (Shield): Pin 2 of the MIDI Out DIN socket is tied directly to the Atari ST’s chassis ground to provide cable shielding.

Signal Transmission Mechanics

When the line is idle, the ACIA holds the \(TxD\) line high (logic 1). The inverter inverts this signal to low, preventing current from circulating between Pin 4 and Pin 5.

When data transmission begins, the ACIA pulls \(TxD\) low (logic 0 for the start bit). The inverter flips this state, pulling Pin 5 toward ground. This creates a voltage potential across Pins 4 and 5, causing approximately 5 mA of current to flow through the cable into the optocoupler of the receiving MIDI device. This current-loop approach isolates external noise, minimizes signal degradation over long cables, and avoids ground loops between connected instruments.
