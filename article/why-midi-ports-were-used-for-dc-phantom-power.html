Why MIDI Ports Were Used for DC Phantom Power

In the early days of the MIDI specification, several manufacturers routed direct current (DC) phantom power through the unused pins of standard 5-pin DIN connectors to eliminate bulky external power supplies for remote equipment. While this practice solved an immediate convenience problem for stage musicians and studio engineers, it blatantly violated official MIDI standards, leading to widespread hardware incompatibilities, blown circuits, and fried components.

The MIDI 1.0 Pinout Architecture

When the MIDI 1.0 specification was released in 1983, the standard adopted a 5-pin 180-degree DIN connector. However, the protocol only required three pins to function:

  • Pin 4: +5V source (current loop driver)
  • Pin 5: MIDI Data line
  • Pin 2: Cable shield / Ground (connected only at transmitter ports to prevent ground loops)
  • Pins 1 and 3: Unassigned and reserved for future specification updates

Because pins 1 and 3 carried no electrical signals according to the official standard, they were left floating (unconnected) on compliant hardware.

The Problem of Remote Power

During the mid-to-late 1980s, rack-mount synthesizers, multi-effects units, and MIDI-controlled guitar preamps exploded in popularity. Musicians relied on floorboard MIDI foot controllers to switch patches and parameters from the front of the stage.

These foot controllers required DC power to run their internal microprocessors and LED displays. This forced musicians to run two separate lines: a MIDI cable carrying control data to the rack, and an AC/DC power adapter ("wall wart") cable carrying power from the wall back to the stage floor. This added clutter, increased setup times, and introduced tripping hazards on dark stages.

Repurposing Pins 1 and 3

Recognizing that Pins 1 and 3 were electronically idle, several manufacturers—particularly those in the guitar and live-sound industries like Rocktron, ADA, and various boutique builders—decided to repurpose them.

By wiring DC voltage (typically +9V or +12V) to Pin 1 and a dedicated ground return to Pin 3 at the rack receiver, power could be sent backwards down a single, standard 5-pin DIN cable directly to the foot controller. From an engineering and cost standpoint, this appeared to be an elegant, zero-cost solution that required no proprietary cables or bulky secondary power runs.

Why the Implementation Failed

Despite its practical appeal, hijacking Pins 1 and 3 was fundamentally non-compliant and introduced several severe technical flaws:

  • Direct Short Circuits: Some fully compliant MIDI device manufacturers tied Pins 1 and 3 to ground on their circuit boards or chassis to reduce radio-frequency interference (RFI). When a proprietary power-injecting device was connected to one of these units, the DC supply rail was shorted directly to ground, often melting traces, blowing internal fuses, or destroying power supplies.
  • Cable Discrepancies: Because the MIDI specification only mandated three functional pins, many cable manufacturers sold budget "MIDI cables" that only had three physical conductors wired inside (Pins 2, 4, and 5). Pins 1 and 3 were entirely omitted. When consumers plugged these cables into non-compliant powered setups, the devices mysteriously failed to turn on.
  • Lack of Industry Standardization: Manufacturers did not agree on voltage, polarity, or pin assignment. One brand might use Pin 1 for positive voltage and Pin 3 for ground, while another reversed the polarity or sent low-voltage AC power instead. Cross-connecting gear from different brands could instantly destroy delicate logic chips.

The Shift to 7-Pin Connectors

To eliminate these hazards without abandoning single-cable setups, the industry eventually adopted 7-pin DIN connectors. By keeping Pins 1 through 5 strictly compliant with standard MIDI data and adding dedicated Pins 6 and 7 specifically for phantom power, manufacturers could provide phantom power safely. Standard 5-pin MIDI cables could still plug directly into the center of a 7-pin female jack without shorting the external power lines, definitively resolving the issues caused by early non-compliant designs.
