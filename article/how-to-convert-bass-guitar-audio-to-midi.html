How to Convert Bass Guitar Audio to MIDI

Converting a monophonic bass guitar audio track into clean, editable MIDI notes involves preparing the raw audio, applying pitch and transient detection algorithms, and refining the resulting MIDI data. Audio editors and digital audio workstations (DAWs) use these processes to translate analog pitch and dynamics into digital note values, allowing producers to correct performance errors, double lines with virtual synthesizers, or replace the bass tone entirely.

1. Pre-Processing the Audio Track

Low-frequency instruments like the bass guitar present unique challenges for pitch detection due to sub-bass rumble, complex harmonic overtones, and fret buzz. Before conversion, editors pre-filter the audio:

  • High-Pass Filtering: Removing sub-audible frequencies below 30–40 Hz prevents the detection algorithm from mistaking rumble for low notes.
  • Low-Pass Filtering: Cutting high frequencies above 2 kHz to 4 kHz removes string squeaks, finger noise, and high harmonic overtones that can confuse pitch-tracking algorithms.
  • Dynamic Control: Applying gentle compression or limiting evens out the performance, giving the detector a more consistent signal level to read.

2. Transient and Pitch Detection

Once prepped, the DAW's detection engine analyzes the audio file using two primary mechanisms:

  • Fundamental Frequency Tracking: The software uses algorithms such as autocorrelation or spectral analysis to identify the fundamental frequency (\(F_0\)) of each note. Since a monophonic bass track plays one note at a time, the engine isolates this base frequency while ignoring the upper harmonics.
  • Transient Detection: Fast amplitude spikes at the start of each pluck mark the note's onset. The software uses these transients to determine exactly where a MIDI note should start (Note-On) and how hard it was struck, translating peak amplitude into MIDI velocity.

3. Audio-to-MIDI Translation

The software maps the detected acoustic parameters directly into the standard MIDI protocol:

  • Pitch to MIDI Note Number: The detected fundamental frequency (measured in Hertz) is mapped to the nearest standard MIDI note number (e.g., 41.2 Hz corresponds to low E, or MIDI note E1).
  • Amplitude to Velocity: The initial attack volume determines the velocity value (from 1 to 127).
  • Sustained Energy to Duration: The engine monitors when the signal drops below a specific amplitude threshold, generating the Note-Off command to set the duration of the MIDI event.

4. Cleaning and Editing the Generated MIDI

Automated conversion rarely produces a perfect result without human refinement. The final phase involves cleaning up common tracking artifacts:

  • Octave Correction: Bass notes can sometimes be misread as an octave higher or lower due to strong harmonic content. Editors quickly scan the piano roll to shift any misplaced notes back to the proper octave.
  • Removing Ghost Notes: Unintended noises—such as fingers lifting off the strings or light fret taps—often register as ultra-short, low-velocity MIDI notes. Selecting all notes below a specific velocity or duration threshold allows for quick deletion.
  • Quantization: Once the notes are clean, producers apply timing quantization to lock the performance to the project tempo grid or adjust note lengths for consistent sustain.
