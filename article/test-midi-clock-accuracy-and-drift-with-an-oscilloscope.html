Test MIDI Clock Accuracy and Drift with an Oscilloscope

This guide explains how an audio engineer can use a digital storage oscilloscope to measure the timing accuracy, jitter, and long-term drift of an external MIDI clock. By tapping into the physical transmission line, isolating the recurring 0xF8 MIDI Timing Clock bytes, and utilizing the oscilloscope’s measurement, persistence, and statistical functions, you can objectively quantify clock stability against mathematical standards.

1. Understanding the MIDI Clock Signal

The standard MIDI Timing Clock protocol relies on a single-byte Real-Time message: 0xF8 (binary 11111000). It transmits at a rate of 24 Pulses Per Quarter Note (PPQN) over a standard 31.25 kbaud asynchronous serial line (1 start bit, 8 data bits, 1 stop bit).

To calculate the expected interval (\(T\)) between clock pulses at any given tempo in Beats Per Minute (BPM):

\[T = \frac{60}{\text{BPM} \times 24}\]

Example: At 120 BPM, \(T = \frac{60}{2880} \approx 20.833 \text{ ms}\).

2. Physical Probe Setup

Because standard 5-pin DIN MIDI uses an opto-isolated 5 mA current loop rather than a ground-referenced voltage line, you must establish an appropriate test point:

  • Hardware Tap: Connect a breakout box or a modified 5-pin DIN cable. Pin 4 is the current source (+5V through a resistor), and Pin 5 is the data line.
  • Probing: The most reliable measurement point is behind the receiving optocoupler on a receiving device or MIDI splitter, where the signal has been converted to a standard 0–5V or 0–3.3V TTL/CMOS UART signal. Connect the oscilloscope probe’s ground clip to circuit ground and the probe tip to the optocoupler's output (UART RX line).
  • Direct Cable Probing: If probing an isolated cable without an active receiver, connect a 220-ohm pull-down resistor between Pin 4 and Pin 5 to simulate the optocoupler LED load, referencing your probe across the resistor.

3. Oscilloscope Configuration and Triggering

  1. Vertical Scale: Set to 1V/div or 2V/div to capture the full TTL logic levels (0V to 3.3V/5V).
  2. Horizontal Scale: Set to 2 ms/div or 5 ms/div to view multiple clock intervals.
  3. Trigger Setup:
    • Protocol Triggering (Recommended): If the oscilloscope has a UART/serial decode package, set the bus to 31,250 baud, 8-N-1, LSB first, and trigger specifically on the Data Byte 0xF8.
    • Edge Triggering: If protocol triggering is unavailable, set an Edge trigger to the falling edge (Start Bit). Ensure the MIDI line is idle of high-bandwidth continuous controller (CC) or pitch-bend data to avoid triggering on non-clock messages.

4. Measuring Accuracy (Nominal Frequency)

  1. Enable an automated measurement for Period or Time Between Triggers (\(\Delta T\)).
  2. Set the measurement gate to calculate the time between the start bit of one 0xF8 byte and the start bit of the subsequent 0xF8 byte.
  3. Compare the Mean measured period directly to your target mathematical interval (\(T\)).
  4. Systematic error—where the mean period consistently measures faster or slower than expected—indicates a master clock oscillator frequency error in the sending hardware.

5. Measuring Jitter (Short-Term Instability)

Jitter represents cycle-to-cycle timing variations caused by software interrupt latencies or buffer delays inside the transmitting device:

  1. Persistence Mode: Turn on Infinite Persistence or Color-Graded Persistence on the oscilloscope.
  2. Align the trigger on the first clock pulse. Observe the second clock pulse located one interval (\(T\)) to the right on the display.
  3. The width of the accumulated edge transitions reveals the peak-to-peak jitter. A tight, single trace indicates low jitter; a wide, blurred band indicates high timing variance.
  4. Statistical Analysis: Enable statistical tracking on the Period measurement. Record:
    • Standard Deviation (\(\sigma\)): Reflects the root-mean-square (RMS) jitter.
    • Peak-to-Peak (\(P\text{-}P\)): The total range between the minimum and maximum observed intervals over thousands of cycles. Low-jitter hardware typically maintains a \(P\text{-}P\) variance under \(100 \ \mu\text{s}\).

6. Measuring Long-Term Drift

Drift is the cumulative loss or gain of time over minutes or hours due to component thermal variation or inaccurate hardware crystal oscillators:

  1. Trend/Track Feature: Use the oscilloscope's measurement trend function (if equipped) to graph the calculated period over an extended capture period (e.g., 30 minutes).
  2. Dual-Channel Comparison:
    • Feed a stable, high-precision reference clock (such as an audio interface word-clock converted to pulses or a dedicated master sync generator) into Channel 1 and set it as the trigger source.
    • Feed the external MIDI clock into Channel 2.
    • Over time, observe the relative phase shift of the Channel 2 pulses. If the Channel 2 pulses progressively move left (leading) or right (lagging) relative to Channel 1, calculate drift by dividing the phase shift time by the total elapsed test time.
