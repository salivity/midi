Why MIDI Uses Pins 4 and 5 for Data Transmission

The standard 5-pin DIN connector used in MIDI technology relies on pins 4 and 5 to drive its 5mA current loop data transmission, while pin 2 serves as a ground shield and pins 1 and 3 remain unconnected. This article explains the technical, physical, and historical reasons behind this configuration, highlighting the non-linear layout of DIN connectors, signal shielding benefits, and design choices made for future compatibility and electrical isolation.

The Non-Linear DIN Pin Layout

The numbering scheme of a standard 180-degree 5-pin DIN connector (DIN 41524) is not sequential across its arc. From left to right when viewing the female jack, the pins are ordered 1, 4, 2, 5, and 3.

Pins 4 and 5 are physically positioned symmetrically on either side of pin 2, which occupies the exact center of the connector arc. When the MIDI 1.0 specification was drafted in the early 1980s, choosing pins 4 and 5 allowed the two active conductors to remain physically adjacent to the central reference pin, creating a clean, balanced physical pathway for the signal cable.

Shielding and the 5mA Current Loop

MIDI does not transmit data via standard voltage references; instead, it uses a 5mA balanced current loop to achieve high noise immunity and prevent ground loops:

  • Pin 4: Carries +5V (source) through a 220-ohm resistor.
  • Pin 5: Acts as the data return (sink), pulled down through another 220-ohm resistor by the transmitter's UART output to complete the circuit.
  • Pin 2: Connects to the cable shield at the MIDI Out and MIDI Thru ports, but is deliberately left floating at the MIDI In port to maintain optical isolation.

Because pin 2 is placed directly between pins 4 and 5, the cable shield connects physically between the active lines inside the plug. This layout minimizes crosstalk and balances the differential pair, reducing electromagnetic interference (EMI) radiated by the high-speed 31.25 kbaud serial signal.

Reservation of Pins 1 and 3

The designers of the MIDI specification deliberately left pins 1 and 3 unconnected. By isolating data transmission exclusively to the inner pins (4, 2, and 5), the standard left the outer pins (1 and 3) reserved for potential future expansions, such as two-way serial communication, synchronization clocks, or phantom power distribution. Although these pins were largely left standardless to avoid interoperability issues, reserving them prevented any active signal lines from being tied up on the outer edges of the connector.

Optoisolation Architecture

At the receiving end (MIDI In), pins 4 and 5 connect directly to the internal LED of an optoisolator (such as the Sharp PC900 or 6N138). Because pins 4 and 5 form a closed loop that powers this internal LED, no electrical connection exists between the sending and receiving hardware chassis. Utilizing a dedicated, isolated pair of adjacent pins made routing traces on printed circuit boards straightforward, isolating high-voltage mains and digital ground noise between connected musical instruments.
