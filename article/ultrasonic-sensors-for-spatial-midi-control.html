Ultrasonic Sensors for Spatial MIDI Control

Interactive sound artists frequently use ultrasonic distance sensors to bridge physical space and digital music production, turning physical movement into expressive musical data. By emitting high-frequency sound waves and calculating their time-of-flight, these sensors track the proximity of a performer’s hands or an audience member's body. Microcontrollers process these distance measurements and translate them into standard MIDI messages—most commonly Continuous Controller (CC) data—allowing physical gestures to modulate musical parameters like filter cutoffs, spatial panning, volume, and effects in real time.

The Physics of Distance Detection

At the hardware level, artists typically deploy sensors such as the HC-SR04. The sensor consists of an ultrasonic transmitter and receiver. The transmitter emits a burst of sound at approximately 40 kHz, well above human hearing. The sound wave travels through the air, reflects off a solid object (such as a hand or a person), and bounces back to the receiver.

The sensor measures the time elapsed between the transmission and the reception of the echo. A microcontroller—such as an Arduino, Teensy, or ESP32—calculates the physical distance using the speed of sound via the formula:

\[\text{Distance} = \frac{\text{Time} \times \text{Speed of Sound}}{2}\]

Signal Conditioning and Data Smoothing

Raw ultrasonic readings can be noisy due to atmospheric conditions, sensor latency, or imperfect reflective surfaces. Before converting distance data to musical control signals, the microcontroller must condition the input.

Artists implement software smoothing algorithms, such as:

  • Moving Averages: Averaging the last few readings to eliminate sudden erratic jumps.
  • Low-Pass Filtering: Smoothing the output trajectory so rapid, unintentional twitches do not cause audible artifacts in the sound engine.
  • Boundary Clamping: Setting minimum and maximum physical thresholds (for example, 5 cm to 100 cm) to define an active interaction zone.

Mapping Distance to MIDI Messages

Once a stable distance metric is calculated, it must be normalized and mapped to the MIDI protocol. Standard MIDI Continuous Controller (CC) messages use a 7-bit resolution, requiring values between 0 and 127.

The microcontroller runs a mapping function that scales the calibrated physical range to this 0–127 integer scale. For example, a hand positioned at 5 cm might output a CC value of 0, while moving the hand to 100 cm scales up to 127. The microcontroller then transmits this data over USB-MIDI or traditional 5-pin DIN connections directly to a Digital Audio Workstation (DAW) or hardware synthesizer.

Generating Multi-Dimensional Spatial Sound

While a single sensor measures one-dimensional depth (the Z-axis), artists frequently combine multiple ultrasonic sensors to construct multi-dimensional spatial systems:

  • Two-Axis Control (X/Y): Placing two sensors orthogonally allows an artist to control two parameters simultaneously, such as stereo panning along the X-axis and filter resonance along the Y-axis.
  • 3D Audio Panning: In ambisonic or surround sound environments, distance values can be mapped to 3D spatial coordinates (azimuth, elevation, and distance). Moving closer to a specific sensor pulls an audio source toward that speaker's physical position in the room.
  • Proximity-Based Wet/Dry Control: Artists often map distance directly to spatial depth effects. Moving away from a sensor can increase the wet/dry mix of a large reverb and reduce high frequencies, psychoacoustically simulating an object moving into the distance.

By converting the physics of acoustic reflection into digital control streams, ultrasonic sensors provide an intuitive, touchless interface that allows artists to sculpt sound directly through spatial choreography.
