Microtonal Chord Glissandos in MIDI 2.0 Explained

MIDI 2.0 fundamentally transforms electronic music expression by introducing native per-note pitch bend capabilities, allowing musicians to execute polyphonic microtonal glissandos with unprecedented ease. Previously restricted by global channel architectures, composers and performers can now glide individual voices within a chord in opposite directions, across micro-intervals, and at varying speeds on a single MIDI channel. This article examines how the protocol removes legacy routing workarounds, enhances pitch resolution, and streamlines the performance of complex polyphonic microtonality.

The Limitation of MIDI 1.0

In the original MIDI 1.0 specification, pitch bend is a channel-wide control change message. When a pitch wheel or ribbon controller is moved, every active note on that channel shifts uniformly.

To perform a polyphonic glissando—such as bending one note of a triad while the other two stay static, or sliding voices in contrary motion—users had to adopt complex workarounds:

  • Multichannel Splitting: Assigning each note of a chord to a separate MIDI channel.
  • Track Duplication: Creating multiple DAW tracks hosting identical synthesizer patches.
  • MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE): A legacy adaptation that dynamically distributes individual notes across 15 separate MIDI channels to isolate pitch messages.

While MPE made per-note expression playable, it remained an indirect patch on an outdated framework, introducing setup friction, voice-allocation bottlenecks, and cumbersome DAW editing.

Native Per-Note Control in MIDI 2.0

MIDI 2.0 addresses this limitation at the architectural level through the Universal MIDI Packet (UMP). Within UMP, pitch bend is no longer confined to the channel level. The protocol provides dedicated Per-Note Pitch Bend messages directly addressed to a specific note number and its unique note-attribute identity.

Because the pitch data is tied to the individual note event rather than the transport channel, an entire polyphonic cluster can exist on a single channel without crosstalk:

  • Voice Independence: Note C4 can glide down 50 cents, E4 can remain fixed, and G4 can glide up a quarter-tone simultaneously.
  • Contrary Motion: Chordal lines can converge, diverge, or execute complex microtonal clusters without colliding control data.
  • No Channel Cycling: There is no need to cycle through 16 channels to keep notes isolated, preventing voice-stealing bugs common in dense arrangements.

32-Bit Microtonal Precision

Beyond voice routing, microtonal glissandos require extreme pitch precision to prevent audible "stepping" or zipper noise. MIDI 1.0 pitch bend uses a 14-bit integer, providing 16,384 discrete steps. When stretched over wide bend ranges or extremely slow microtonal glides, these steps can become perceptually audible.

MIDI 2.0 upgrades pitch bend to 32-bit resolution, providing over 4 billion discrete values. This yields functionally continuous pitch variation:

  • Smooth, imperceptible transitions across unconventional microtonal tuning systems (such as 19-EDO, 24-EDO, or 31-EDO).
  • Ultra-slow portamento inside chord structures that retains an organic, acoustic-like continuity.
  • Direct support for tuning units where pitch can be addressed in precise fractions of a semitone or specific frequency ratios.

Streamlined DAW Editing and Performance

By centralizing multi-voice expression into single-track events, MIDI 2.0 dramatically simplifies the workflow for producers and performers:

  1. Clean Piano Roll Display: Instead of scattering a three-note chord across three separate tracks or viewing fractured multichannel lane automation, modern DAWs display chordal pitch envelopes directly over their respective note bars within a single view.
  2. Simplified Sound Engines: Software synthesizers receive pitch instructions mapped directly to the designated synth voice instance, reducing the processing overhead associated with hosting multiple multitimbral sound generators.
  3. Controller Ergonomics: Modern polyphonic aftertouch and continuous-pitch controllers communicate directly with synths using bidirectional MIDI-CI (Capability Inquiry), automatically configuring the instrument for per-note pitch response without manual CC-mapping.

Through dedicated per-note addressing and ultra-high resolution, MIDI 2.0 turns microtonal chord glissandos from a technical engineering hurdle into a natural, intuitive performance technique.
