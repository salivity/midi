MIDI Polyphonic Expression MPE Explained

MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) is an official specification ratified by the MIDI Association that enables electronic musical instruments to transmit multidimensional, per-note control data. Unlike standard MIDI, which applies modulation, pitch bend, and expression globally across all sounding notes on a single channel, MPE allows musicians to independently modulate the pitch, timbre, and dynamics of individual notes played simultaneously. This article outlines the primary function of MPE, how it routes data across multiple channels, and its role in modern digital music performance.

The Core Function of MPE

The primary function of the MPE specification is to provide acoustic-like articulation to electronic instruments. On traditional acoustic instruments, such as a violin or guitar, each vibrating string can be bent, vibrated, or altered in tone independently of the others. Prior to MPE, standard MIDI synthesizers could not replicate this behavior polyphonically; applying pitch bend or aftertouch affected every active note at the same time.

MPE solves this limitation by fundamentally altering how MIDI channels are assigned, giving compatible controllers the ability to send continuous, per-note expressive parameters directly to compatible synthesizers and digital audio workstations (DAWs).

How MPE Works

MPE achieves polyphonic independence by dynamically distributing polyphony across an array of MIDI channels rather than confining it to one.

  • Channel Allocation: In an MPE configuration, typically one channel (often Channel 1) is designated as the "Master" or "Global" channel for overall controls like program changes, sustain pedal input, and master volume.
  • Per-Note Channels: The remaining channels (typically Channels 2 through 16) are designated as "Member" channels. Whenever a new key or pad is struck, the controller automatically assigns that single note to its own unique Member channel.
  • Continuous Control Routing: Because the note occupies its own channel for the duration of its sustain, all channel-wide MIDI messages—such as Pitch Bend, Channel Pressure (aftertouch), and Control Change (CC) messages—apply exclusively to that individual note. Once the note is released, the channel becomes available for the next note.

Standard Expressive Dimensions

The MPE specification standardizes five core dimensions of touch, allowing hardware and software from different manufacturers to communicate consistently:

  1. Strike (Note-On Velocity): Measures the initial speed or force with which a note is played.
  2. Press (Continuous Aftertouch): Tracks the downward pressure exerted on a key or pad after the initial strike, allowing for dynamic swells and note-level volume shaping.
  3. Glide (Horizontal Pitch Bend): Measures left-to-right finger movement, enabling per-note vibrato, microtonal adjustments, and large pitch glides without detuning other held chords.
  4. Slide (Vertical Control): Measures upward and downward finger movement along the surface of a key, mapped by default to MIDI CC 74 to control timbral parameters like filter cutoff.
  5. Lift (Release Velocity): Measures the speed at which a finger leaves the playing surface, commonly used to control envelope release times or mechanical release noises.

Standardization and Interoperability

Ratified by the MIDI Association in January 2018, MPE established a uniform standard across the music technology industry. Prior to ratification, manufacturers implemented proprietary or fragmented workarounds to achieve polyphonic expression, often causing software incompatibilities and routing conflicts. The standardized MPE profile provides defined configuration profiles (such as MPE Configuration Messages or MCM), ensuring that MPE-capable controllers, DAWs, and synthesizers automatically negotiate channel counts and pitch bend ranges seamlessly.
