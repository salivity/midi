What Is MIDI Pad Velocity Thresholding?

MIDI pad velocity thresholding is a configuration setting that establishes the minimum force required for a controller pad to register a strike and output a MIDI note. In digital music production, overly sensitive pads often detect subtle vibrations, finger rests, or structural reverberations, resulting in quiet, unintended triggers known as ghost notes. By raising the baseline sensitivity floor, velocity thresholding ensures that only deliberate, decisive physical impacts trigger sound, effectively cleaning up your performance data.

Understanding Velocity and Thresholding

In the MIDI protocol, velocity represents the force or speed with which a key or pad is struck, quantified on a scale from 0 to 127. Velocity determines attributes like sample volume, filter cutoff, or sample layer selection.

Sensors inside MIDI controllers—typically piezoelectric sensors or force-sensitive resistors (FSRs)—generate an electrical voltage proportional to the force applied. Velocity thresholding sets a cut-off point at the bottom of this voltage spectrum. If a physical strike generates a signal below this designated threshold value, the controller's internal processor discards the data instead of transmitting a MIDI Note-On message.

Why Unintended Ghost Notes Occur

Ghost notes in MIDI tracking refer to spurious, low-velocity notes that clutter recorded sequences. These unwanted events typically stem from three physical issues:

  1. Chassis Crosstalk: Striking one pad forcefully sends mechanical vibrations through the controller’s frame, vibrating adjacent sensors enough to produce low-velocity notes.
  2. Pad Rebound and Double-Triggering: After striking a pad, the physical rebound of the rubber or the musician's finger can cause a secondary micro-tap.
  3. Resting Weight: Drummers and beatmakers often rest their fingers lightly on pads between strikes. Highly sensitive pads can interpret this light pressure as an intentional performance event.

How Thresholding Eliminates Ghost Notes

Velocity thresholding operates essentially as a digital noise gate for physical force. It solves the ghost note issue through direct mechanical filtering:

  • Suppressing Structural Vibrations: Because crosstalk vibrations produce significantly lower voltage than direct hits, a properly calibrated threshold allows the controller to ignore the physical shockwaves traveling through the plastic or metal casing.
  • Eliminating Rebound Artifacts: Pad bounce inherently dissipates energy rapidly. While the primary strike hits with high velocity, the follow-up micro-bounce registers at an extremely low level that falls below the threshold floor.
  • Ignoring Surface Grazes: Setting the threshold slightly above zero ensures that casual contact or moving fingers between pads won't inadvertently fire off a sound.

Finding the Right Balance

Calibrating velocity thresholding requires balancing responsiveness and noise rejection. If the threshold is set too low, the pad becomes hyper-sensitive, letting crosstalk and double-triggers seep into the performance. If set too high, the pad loses its dynamic range, ignoring subtle, intentional ghost notes often used in expressive drumming and finger-drumming techniques.

The ideal threshold is the lowest possible value that completely eliminates mechanical crosstalk and unintentional triggers while preserving the player's lightest intentional taps.
