How Analog Guitar Signals Generate MIDI CC

Converting an analog electric guitar signal into dynamic MIDI Control Change (CC) messages requires capturing the instrument's amplitude profile, converting that waveform into a direct-current (DC) voltage, and translating that voltage into digital data. An analog envelope follower extracts the dynamic contour of a performance by rectifying and smoothing the alternating-current (AC) output from the guitar pickups. A microcontroller then samples this extracted continuous voltage via an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) and maps it to a standard MIDI CC range (0–127), transmitting real-time performance dynamics to external synthesizers, software, or digital effects.

1. Preamplification and Buffering

The electrical signal produced by electric guitar pickups is a low-amplitude, high-impedance AC signal, typically measuring between tens to hundreds of millivolts. Before any dynamic processing can occur, the signal passes through a high-impedance buffer and preamplifier circuit. This stage prevents tone loading and boosts the signal to a usable operating level (often between 0V and 5V or line-level equivalents) while preserving the natural transient attacks and decaying sustain of the plucked strings.

2. Full-Wave Rectification

Because an audio waveform oscillates symmetrically above and below a central reference line, an envelope follower must polarize the wave to read its absolute amplitude. A precision rectifier circuit—usually constructed with operational amplifiers (op-amps) and diodes—inverts the negative half-cycles of the AC signal into positive half-cycles. Full-wave rectification is favored over half-wave rectification because it doubles the ripple frequency, reducing latency and making the signal easier to smooth without filtering out fast playing dynamics.

3. Filtering and Envelope Extraction

Once rectified, the signal consists of rapid, pulsating positive DC bursts. To obtain a steady representation of volume rather than raw frequency oscillations, the signal passes into a peak detector and a low-pass filter, typically built using an RC (resistor-capacitor) network.

This circuit establishes two critical timing parameters:

  • Attack Time: Dictates how fast the capacitor charges when the guitar is struck. A faster attack captures sudden percussive transients.
  • Release (Decay) Time: Governs how gradually the capacitor discharges through a resistor as the string's vibration fades.

The resulting output is a smooth, continuous DC control voltage that rises and falls in direct proportion to the volume of the guitar performance.

4. Analog-to-Digital Conversion (ADC)

The smoothed DC envelope is routed to the input pin of an onboard microcontroller equipped with an Analog-to-Digital Converter. The ADC repeatedly samples the continuous voltage at high speeds—commonly between 500 Hz and several kilohertz—to minimize tracking latency. The hardware translates the analog voltage into a digital integer based on its internal bit depth (for example, a 10-bit ADC provides values from 0 to 1023).

5. Digital Filtering and MIDI CC Mapping

Before transmitting data, the microcontroller's firmware processes the raw digital values to eliminate unwanted noise and MIDI buffer overloads:

  • Hysteresis and Noise Gating: Tiny fluctuations in the analog voltage (analog ripple) can cause rapid, unintended MIDI messages. Hysteresis algorithms ensure that a new value is only processed if the change crosses a defined threshold.
  • Scaling and Truncation: The digital integer is scaled down to a standard 7-bit MIDI data range (0 to 127), or occasionally a 14-bit high-resolution format.
  • Response Curves: Firmware often applies linear, logarithmic, or exponential response curves to match the perceptual dynamics of the player.

6. Real-Time MIDI Transmission

Finally, the microcontroller formats the mapped integer into a standard 3-byte MIDI Control Change message:

  1. Status Byte: Specifies the MIDI channel and indicates a CC command (e.g., 0xBn, where n is the channel).
  2. Controller Number: Dictates the destination parameter, such as CC 1 (Modulation), CC 7 (Volume), or CC 11 (Expression).
  3. Data Byte: The actual envelope value between 0 and 127.

These packets are transmitted across a 5-pin DIN connection, a TRS jack, or via USB-MIDI with sub-5-millisecond latency, allowing the physical volume and touch response of the guitar to manipulate software synth filters, effects parameters, or secondary sound layers in real time.
