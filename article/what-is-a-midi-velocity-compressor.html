What Is a MIDI Velocity Compressor?

A MIDI velocity compressor is a production utility that manipulates incoming MIDI note velocity data to reduce the dynamic gap between the softest and hardest played notes. Unlike traditional audio compressors that process recorded sound waves and decibel levels, a MIDI compressor works purely in the digital control domain before any sound is generated. By recalculating the velocity values of a performance, it creates a more consistent, polished, and controlled output while preserving the underlying timing and musical expression.

Understanding MIDI Velocity

In the MIDI protocol, velocity represents the force or speed with which a key, pad, or trigger is struck, measured on a standard scale from 0 to 127. In modern virtual instruments, velocity dictates not only volume but also timbre, tone, and sample layer selection. A hard strike often triggers a bright, aggressive sample, while a soft strike triggers a darker, gentler one.

When a performer plays unevenly, these velocity jumps cause abrupt, undesirable shifts in volume and tonal character.

How a MIDI Velocity Compressor Works

A MIDI velocity compressor narrows dynamic discrepancies by mathematically remapping input velocity values to a narrower output range. It typically utilizes parameters similar to standard audio dynamic processors:

  • Threshold: The velocity level above which compression begins. Any note struck with a velocity exceeding this number is subjected to reduction.
  • Ratio: The intensity of the compression. For example, with a 2:1 ratio, an input increase of 10 velocity units past the threshold results in only a 5-unit increase at the output.
  • Offset or Gain (Makeup Velocity): A fixed value added to all notes to compensate for lost velocity, effectively raising the floor of the softest notes.
  • Minimum and Maximum Limits: Hard caps that prevent any MIDI note from falling below or exceeding specified values (e.g., clamping all output between 60 and 110).

Narrowing Dynamic Discrepancies

When a keyboardist or finger-drummer delivers an erratic performance, weak strikes might register at a velocity of 40, while accented strikes peak at 120. A MIDI velocity compressor smooths this 80-value discrepancy.

By applying a threshold (such as 70) and a moderate ratio, the extreme peaks of 120 are pulled downward to a lower range, such as 95. Applying makeup gain or using a dual-ended compressor (which simultaneously boosts values below a low threshold) lifts the weak strikes of 40 up toward 65. The original wide variance of 80 units is effectively narrowed to an even 30-unit spread.

Advantages Over Audio Compression

Compressing the MIDI data prior to sound generation offers distinct advantages over standard audio compression:

  • Predictable Sample Triggering: Multi-sampled instruments, such as virtual acoustic pianos and realistic drum kits, switch sample layers based on incoming velocity. A MIDI compressor ensures that the instrument consistently triggers the intended tonal layer rather than jumping erratically between whisper-soft and aggressive samples.
  • Artifact-Free Processing: Audio compressors can introduce unwanted distortion, pumping, or room noise when pushed hard. MIDI processing alters only numerical data, leaving the acoustic envelope and transient shapes of the sound source completely intact.
  • Non-Destructive Editing: Applied in a digital audio workstation (DAW), MIDI compression can be adjusted in real time during playback, allowing full control over the performance's dynamic response without permanently altering the recorded MIDI notes.
