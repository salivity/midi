How Hybrid Piano Optical Sensors Measure Hammer Velocity

Hybrid grand pianos bridge the gap between traditional acoustic mechanics and digital sound production by using non-contact optical sensor systems to capture performance nuances. Instead of relying on mechanical switches that alter the weight and feel of the action, these instruments employ infrared light beams to track the exact speed of moving hammers. By calculating the travel time of the hammer just before it strikes a virtual string, the piano’s internal computer accurately determines velocity and translates it into standard MIDI data without impeding the player's physical touch.

The Core Components: Emitters, Detectors, and Shutters

The optical measurement system relies on three primary elements installed beneath or alongside the grand piano action:

  • Light Emitters: Miniature light-emitting diodes (typically infrared LEDs) project focused, continuous beams of light.
  • Photodetectors: Photodiodes or phototransistors sit opposite or adjacent to the emitters to receive the light signal and convert light intensity into an electrical voltage.
  • Graded Shutters (Flags): Lightweight fins, or vanes, are mounted directly onto each individual hammer shank. These shutters are carefully balanced so they do not alter the authentic regulation or inertia of the wooden acoustic action.

The Non-Contact Detection Process

As a pianist presses a key, the internal grand piano mechanism (the whippen, jack, and repetition lever) launches the hammer upward toward the point where an acoustic string would normally sit.

Because optical systems operate entirely through light interruption, the hammer never physically touches the sensor. As the hammer ascends, its attached shutter passes between the infrared LED and the photodetector, progressively breaking or reflecting the beam.

Manufacturers generally use one of two methods to read this motion:

  1. Dual-Point Optical Gates: The shutter interrupts two distinct light beams positioned vertically along the hammer's path. The system detects the exact moment the shutter breaks the first beam and logs the time it takes to reach and break the second beam.
  2. Continuous Optical Reflection/Absorption: The shutter features a gray-scale gradient or shaped profile that gradually diminishes the amount of light reaching the detector. The sensor continuously reads changes in voltage, monitoring the entire acceleration curve of the hammer in real time.

Calculating Velocity from Travel Time

Hammer velocity is governed by the basic formula:

\[\text{Velocity} = \frac{\text{Distance}}{\text{Time}}\]

Because the distance between the two detection points (or along the continuous gradient) is fixed and precisely calibrated, the system only needs to measure elapsed time (\(\Delta t\)).

High-speed microprocessors sample the sensors at frequencies often exceeding several kilohertz. A faster hammer movement yields a shorter time interval between detection points, signaling a hard strike (\(fortissimo\)), while a slower swing results in a longer time interval, indicating a soft touch (\(pianissimo\)).

Translating Velocity into MIDI Data

Once the processor determines the hammer's velocity:

  1. Velocity Curve Mapping: The raw speed value is processed through dynamic response curves tailored to replicate the dynamic response of a concert grand piano.
  2. MIDI Value Assignment: The calculated value is converted into a standard 7-bit MIDI velocity integer (ranging from 1 to 127) or a high-resolution 14-bit MIDI prefix/extension (up to 16,383 values) for MIDI 2.0-compatible systems.
  3. Note-On Generation: The system transmits a Note On event containing the note number (pitch) and its corresponding velocity parameter to the internal sound engine or an external digital audio workstation (DAW).

Because the measurement occurs at the hammer shank rather than the key itself, the optical system accurately accounts for advanced pianistic techniques, such as escapement releases, silent key presses, and rapid repetitions, delivering an authentic acoustic response entirely in the digital domain.
