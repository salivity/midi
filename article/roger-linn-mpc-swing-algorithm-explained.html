Roger Linn MPC Swing Algorithm Explained

Roger Linn revolutionized modern music production by introducing a swing algorithm to drum machines and MIDI sequencers like the LM-1, LinnDrum, and Akai MPC series. This algorithm transformed rigid, robotic digital timing into a natural, humanized groove by mathematically delaying alternate sixteenth notes. Rather than relying on random timing variations or true human inaccuracy, Linn’s technique applied a precise, repeatable mathematical formula across sub-divisions of musical time to emulate the traditional jazz and funk shuffle.

In standard 4/4 musical time with straight timing, each quarter note contains four sixteenth notes of equal length. Linn’s swing algorithm operates on pairs of sixteenth notes within each eighth-note interval. The first sixteenth note falls on the downbeat or the eighth-note beat (beats 1, 2, 3, 4 and the "&" subdivisions), while the second sixteenth note falls directly between them (the "e" and "a" subdivisions).

The algorithm leaves all odd-numbered sixteenth notes firmly anchored to their exact mathematical grid points. This ensures that the foundational tempo and downbeats of the track never drift or lose stability. Swing is introduced solely by delaying the onset of the even-numbered, alternate sixteenth notes.

Mathematically, Linn represented swing as a percentage of the total time span allocated to a pair of sixteenth notes (one eighth note). The calculation functions as follows:

  1. 50% Swing (Straight Time): The eighth-note interval is divided equally. The first sixteenth note occupies the first 50% of the total duration, and the second sixteenth note begins exactly at the 50% mark.
  2. 66.7% Swing (Triplet Feel): The first sixteenth note occupies two-thirds (66.7%) of the eighth-note duration, while the second sixteenth note is compressed into the final third (33.3%). This creates a standard, classic jazz eighth-note triplet shuffle.
  3. Variable Swing Range: Linn allowed users to dial in swing values ranging typically from 50% up to around 75%. At 54% to 58%, the delay creates a subtle, laid-back groove commonly associated with hip-hop and classic R&B, while values approaching 75% represent extreme dotted-sixteenth and thirty-second-note relationships.

To execute this inside hardware like the MPC60, the timing was translated into clock ticks based on Pulses Per Quarter Note (PPQN). The MPC60 runs at a resolution of 96 PPQN, meaning:

  • One quarter note = 96 ticks.
  • One eighth note (a pair of sixteenth notes) = 48 ticks.
  • One straight sixteenth note = 24 ticks.

Under Linn’s formula, the position of the delayed sixteenth note within any 48-tick pair is calculated by multiplying the pair's duration by the swing percentage:

Position = 48 ticks × Swing Percentage

At 50% swing, the second note triggers at tick 24 (48 × 0.50), resulting in no offset. At 54% swing, the calculation yields approximately 25.9 ticks, which rounds to tick 26. This adds a delay of two clock ticks (roughly 10 to 20 milliseconds, depending on the tempo). At a 66.7% triplet swing, the calculation places the second note at tick 32 (48 × 0.667), delaying it by 8 ticks.

By lengthening the first sixteenth note and proportionally shortening the time between the second sixteenth note and the next downbeat, the algorithm creates momentum. The mathematical simplicity of keeping the primary beats fixed while shifting only the off-beats produced a consistent, mechanical groove that felt unmistakably alive.
