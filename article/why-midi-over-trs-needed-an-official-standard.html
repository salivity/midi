Why MIDI Over TRS Needed an Official Standard

As electronic musical instruments became more compact, manufacturers shifted away from traditional 5-pin DIN connectors to 3.5mm TRS (Tip-Ring-Sleeve) jacks. However, an absence of an initial standard led to a hardware rift between two competing wiring schemes known as Type A and Type B. To eliminate widespread consumer confusion, restore universal interoperability, and protect equipment from signal incompatibilities, the MIDI Association stepped in during 2018 to release an official specification designating Type A as the universal standard.

The push toward 3.5mm TRS jacks was driven by hardware design. Modern synthesizers, sequencers, and guitar pedals grew increasingly compact, making the bulky, legacy 5-pin DIN jack difficult to integrate onto slim enclosures. Because a standard MIDI connection only utilizes three electrical lines (Source, Sink, and Shield), a standard three-conductor TRS audio jack was mechanically capable of carrying the serial MIDI signal using far less physical space.

Without a central guideline in place, major music technology brands implemented TRS connections independently. This led to two conflicting wiring conventions:

  • Type A: Wired the Tip to DIN Pin 5 (Sink/-), the Ring to DIN Pin 4 (Source/+), and the Sleeve to DIN Pin 2 (Shield). This configuration was adopted early on by brands like Korg, Make Noise, and IK Multimedia.
  • Type B: Reversed the data polarities, wiring the Tip to DIN Pin 4 (Source/+) and the Ring to DIN Pin 5 (Sink/-), while keeping the Sleeve connected to DIN Pin 2 (Shield). This variant was favored by companies such as Arturia, Novation, and 1010music.

Because both Type A and Type B shared the exact same physical 3.5mm connector, users frequently connected two devices using identical standard auxiliary audio cables, only for communication to fail completely. A Type A transmitter sending data through the Ring could not deliver that signal to a Type B receiver expecting the data stream on the Tip. This fragmentation forced musicians to buy custom, mismatched breakout adapters, track complicated brand-compatibility charts, and troubleshoot silent devices.

In 2018, the MIDI Manufacturers Association (MMA) resolved the conflict by publishing the official specification for MIDI over TRS. The organization formally designated Type A as the single, compliant standard for all future products. Type A was chosen primarily because its pin assignment mirrored the original DIN 5-pin mechanical structure more logically, matching the existing recommendations outlined in the updated MIDI 1.0 Electrical Specification.

The intervention of the MIDI Association stabilized the market. While manufacturers were permitted to support legacy Type B configurations through passive adapters, all newly developed MIDI equipment with 3.5mm jacks was expected to adhere to the official Type A specification. Today, the standardization ensures that musicians can connect modern, compact hardware from different brands using ordinary 3.5mm audio cables without fearing wiring conflicts or communication failures.
