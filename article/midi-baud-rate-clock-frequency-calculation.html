MIDI Baud Rate Clock Frequency Calculation

The standard 31.25 kilobaud (kbps) transmission speed of serial MIDI is directly derived from integer division of common microcomputer system clock frequencies. When the MIDI specification was drafted in the early 1980s, synthesizer designers needed an asynchronous transmission rate that was fast enough to minimize musical latency while remaining cheap and simple to generate using standard microprocessors and Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitters (UARTs). This article explains the exact hardware clock frequency formulas, the role of UART prescalers, and why 31,250 baud was chosen as the universal MIDI standard.

The Fundamental Frequency Formula

The transmission speed of a serial interface is determined by dividing a master clock frequency by an integer divisor using a counter or prescaler:

\[\text{Baud Rate} = \frac{f_{\text{clock}}}{N}\]

Where:

  • \(\text{Baud Rate}\) is the target data rate in bits per second (\(31,250 \text{ bps}\)).
  • \(f_{\text{clock}}\) is the input oscillator or system clock frequency fed to the timer/divider.
  • \(N\) is the total clock division ratio (integer).

Rearranging the formula to calculate the required clock divider for a given system clock yields:

\[N = \frac{f_{\text{clock}}}{31,250}\]

Standard Hardware Implementations

In the early 1980s, microprocessors such as the Motorola 6800 series, MOS 6502, and Zilog Z80 commonly operated with master crystals operating at 1 MHz, 2 MHz, 4 MHz, or 8 MHz. The target rate of 31.25 kbps was specifically chosen because it divides into every standard megahertz clock rate as an exact power of two, eliminating timing jitter and baud-rate error without requiring an auxiliary crystal oscillator.

  • 1.0 MHz Clock: \[N = \frac{1,000,000 \text{ Hz}}{31,250 \text{ Hz}} = 32 = 2^5\]

  • 2.0 MHz Clock: \[N = \frac{2,000,000 \text{ Hz}}{31,250 \text{ Hz}} = 64 = 2^6\]

  • 4.0 MHz Clock: \[N = \frac{4,000,000 \text{ Hz}}{31,250 \text{ Hz}} = 128 = 2^7\]

  • 8.0 MHz Clock: \[N = \frac{8,000,000 \text{ Hz}}{31,250 \text{ Hz}} = 256 = 2^8\]

Because each divider is an exact power of two (\(2^n\)), engineers implemented these dividers using basic binary ripple counters (such as a 74LS393 or 74LS161) without complex fractional baud-rate logic.

UART Clocking and the \(16\times\) Oversampling Factor

Asynchronous serial communication relies on receiver oversampling to locate the falling edge of the start bit and sample data in the center of each bit period. Most classic UARTs (such as the Motorola 6850 ACIA commonly found in vintage synthesizers) require a receiver/transmitter clock (\(f_{\text{UART}}\)) that is 16 times higher than the actual baud rate:

\[f_{\text{UART}} = \text{Baud Rate} \times 16\]

For MIDI:

\[f_{\text{UART}} = 31,250 \text{ Hz} \times 16 = 500,000 \text{ Hz} \text{ (500 kHz)}\]

Under this configuration, generating the required UART clock from a standard 1 MHz or 2 MHz bus clock required minimal hardware:

  • From a 1 MHz clock: \(\frac{1,000,000 \text{ Hz}}{2} = 500 \text{ kHz}\) (a single flip-flop divider).
  • From a 2 MHz clock: \(\frac{2,000,000 \text{ Hz}}{4} = 500 \text{ kHz}\) (a dual flip-flop or \(\div 4\) counter).

The internal UART logic then divided the 500 kHz clock by 16 to produce the precise 31,250 bits-per-second transmission rate.

Transmission Timing Characteristics

At 31.25 kbps, the duration of an individual bit period (\(T_{\text{bit}}\)) is exactly:

\[T_{\text{bit}} = \frac{1}{31,250 \text{ Hz}} = 32 \times 10^{-6} \text{ seconds} = 32 \ \mu\text{s}\]

Standard MIDI uses an asynchronous 10-bit framing format:

  • 1 Start bit (logic 0)
  • 8 Data bits (least significant bit first)
  • 1 Stop bit (logic 1)
  • No parity bit

Because each byte requires 10 serial bits, the time required to transmit a single byte is:

\[T_{\text{byte}} = 10 \times 32 \ \mu\text{s} = 320 \ \mu\text{s}\]

This yields a maximum throughput of 3,125 bytes per second. A standard 3-byte MIDI channel message (such as a Note-On command consisting of a status byte, note number, and velocity) requires:

\[T_{\text{message}} = 3 \times 320 \ \mu\text{s} = 960 \ \mu\text{s} \text{ (0.96 ms)}\]

By utilizing integer divisors of standard microcomputer clocks, the 31.25 kbps specification provided sub-millisecond message delivery, zero percent baud-rate error, and low manufacturing costs.
