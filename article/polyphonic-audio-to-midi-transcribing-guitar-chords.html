Polyphonic Audio to MIDI: Transcribing Guitar Chords

Polyphonic audio-to-MIDI transcription converts complex acoustic audio into discrete digital musical events by identifying multiple simultaneous notes within a single recording. When applied to an acoustic guitar chord, the system analyzes a dense mixture of overlapping sound waves, isolates the fundamental frequency of each vibrating string, and tracks the start, duration, and volume of every distinct pitch. This process relies on a combination of time-frequency spectral analysis, harmonic modeling, and deep learning algorithms to untangle shared frequencies and translate an organic performance into accurate MIDI data.

The Challenge of Acoustic Guitar Chords

An acoustic guitar chord is not simply a collection of pure sine waves; it is a complex acoustic event. When multiple strings are struck simultaneously, each string produces a fundamental frequency (the perceived pitch) along with a series of integer-multiple overtones known as harmonics or partials.

In a standard chord, these harmonics collide. For example, the higher harmonics of a low E string can land on the exact same frequencies as the fundamental or lower harmonics of the higher strings. Additionally, physical acoustic guitars introduce body resonance, sympathetic vibration from adjacent strings, and percussive pick attack transients, all of which flood the audio spectrum with competing energy.

Step 1: Time-Frequency Representation

The raw audio waveform represents changes in air pressure over time, which provides insufficient pitch information on its own. Transcription systems first convert this raw audio into a time-frequency representation, typically using the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) or the Constant-Q Transform (CQT).

The Constant-Q Transform is particularly suited for musical analysis because its frequency bins are logarithmically spaced, mirroring the human perception of pitch and the twelve-tone musical scale. This produces a spectrogram—a visual map plotting time along the x-axis, frequency along the y-axis, and energy amplitude through intensity.

Step 2: Onset Detection

To separate notes, the algorithm must determine the exact millisecond the chord was struck. Onset detection algorithms monitor sudden surges in high-frequency energy caused by the pick or fingers hitting the strings.

By analyzing the "spectral flux" (the rate of local change across frequency bands), the system identifies the transient attack of the chord. In arpeggiated or strummed chords, advanced onset detection can differentiate micro-delays between individual string plucks, registering each string contact as a distinct note start rather than a single homogeneous block.

Step 3: Multi-Pitch Estimation (MPE)

The core mechanism for isolating individual notes is Multi-Pitch Estimation. Systems use two primary frameworks to solve this:

Traditional Signal Processing: Spectral Unmixing

Classic transcription uses techniques like Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) or harmonic template matching. These systems know what an idealized guitar string spectrum looks like: a strong fundamental followed by decaying harmonic peaks.

The algorithm scans the frequency spectrum from the lowest frequencies upward. When it detects a prominent fundamental, it estimates the expected energy of that note's harmonic series and mathematically subtracts those theoretical overtones from the total spectrogram. This "spectral peeling" exposes the remaining fundamental frequencies that were previously masked by the louder, lower notes.

Deep Learning and Neural Networks

Modern state-of-the-art transcription relies heavily on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) or Transformers. Instead of hard-coded mathematical rules, these networks are trained on thousands of hours of synchronized guitar audio and MIDI data.

  • CNNs analyze the spectrogram as an image, learning to recognize the visual "fingerprint" of overlapping harmonic ladders unique to acoustic guitars.
  • RNNs and Transformers evaluate the temporal dynamics, understanding how guitar notes naturally decay and how strings behave together in conventional playing styles.

These models output pitch activation probabilities for all 88 standard musical pitches across every continuous time frame.

Step 4: Tracking Note Trajectories and Decay

Acoustic guitar strings decay non-linearly; higher harmonics fade much faster than the fundamental frequency. Polyphonic transcription engines track the temporal trajectory of each identified note frame by frame.

The algorithm distinguishes between a note that is ringing out naturally, a note that has been deliberately damped or muted, and a new note struck on the same string. Thresholding algorithms establish boundaries: when the energy probability of a specific fundamental drops below a predetermined floor, the system registers the end of that note.

Step 5: MIDI Message Generation

Once the frequencies, onsets, and offsets are confirmed, the system maps the physical data into standard MIDI commands:

  • Note-On Event: The identified fundamental frequency is mapped to its corresponding MIDI note number (e.g., 82.4 Hz maps to E2, MIDI note 40).
  • Velocity: The amplitude of the onset transient determines the MIDI velocity value (0–127), reflecting how hard the player strummed or plucked that individual string.
  • Note-Off Event: Triggered when the fundamental frequency decays past the detection threshold or is cut off by a subsequent action.

Through this multi-stage pipeline, the composite acoustic vibration of a guitar chord is successfully deconstructed into clean, independent digital musical notes.
