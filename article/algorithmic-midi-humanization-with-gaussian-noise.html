Algorithmic MIDI Humanization with Gaussian Noise

This article explains how digital audio workstations and MIDI plugins utilize Gaussian distribution noise to transform rigid, grid-aligned MIDI sequences into natural performances. By applying the mathematical principles of the normal distribution curve to note onset times and velocity values, an algorithmic humanizer introduces realistic micro-variations that mimic human biomechanical variance rather than chaotic, unnatural randomness.

The Limitation of Uniform Randomization

Standard randomization applies a uniform distribution where every value within a chosen range has an equal probability of occurring. When applied to MIDI timing or velocity, uniform noise sounds sloppy and erratic—often described as sounding like a "drunken musician." Human musicians do not make errors with uniform probability; instead, they aim for a target metric and deviate from it slightly most of the time, with larger deviations occurring rarely.

Why Gaussian Distribution Works

A Gaussian (or normal) distribution provides the characteristic "bell curve" defined by two core parameters: the mean (\(\mu\)) and the standard deviation (\(\sigma\)).

  • Mean (\(\mu\)): Represents the target value. For timing, the mean offset is typically zero (the exact grid position). For velocity, the mean is the original programmed velocity of the note.
  • Standard Deviation (\(\sigma\)): Represents the "humanize" strength or intensity parameter adjusted by the user.

Under a standard normal distribution, approximately 68.2% of generated values fall within one standard deviation (\(\pm1\sigma\)) of the mean, 95.4% fall within two standard deviations (\(\pm2\sigma\)), and 99.7% fall within three standard deviations (\(\pm3\sigma\)). This ensures that the majority of notes land extremely close to their intended timing and dynamics, while noticeable swings remain statistically rare, directly mimicking human motor control.

Applying Gaussian Noise to Timing

MIDI timing is calculated in ticks (pulses per quarter note, or PPQN) or in milliseconds. To humanize note timing:

  1. Calculate the Offset: The algorithm generates a random number \(Z\) from a standard normal distribution using methods like the Box-Muller transform: \[Z = \sqrt{-2 \ln(U_1)} \cos(2\pi U_2)\] (where \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) are independent random variables uniformly distributed between 0 and 1)
  2. Scale by Deviation: The random value \(Z\) is multiplied by the user-defined standard deviation \(\sigma_{\text{time}}\) (measured in ticks or milliseconds) to compute the timing delta: \[\Delta t = Z \times \sigma_{\text{time}}\]
  3. Shift Note On and Note Off: The calculated delta is added to the original note-on timestamp: \[t_{\text{new}} = t_{\text{original}} + \Delta t\] Most algorithms apply a corresponding or slightly independent shift to the note-off event to maintain realistic note durations.
  4. Boundary Checks: The algorithm clamps values to prevent chronological errors, such as a note shifting behind the playback start position or causing a note-off event to precede its corresponding note-on event.

Applying Gaussian Noise to Velocity

MIDI velocity values are integers strictly bound between 1 and 127 (with 0 reserved for note-off commands). Algorithmic humanization for dynamics follows a similar process:

  1. Calculate the Delta: Using the same Gaussian generator, a value is generated and scaled by the velocity standard deviation \(\sigma_{\text{vel}}\): \[\Delta v = Z \times \sigma_{\text{vel}}\]
  2. Apply and Round: The offset is added to the note's base velocity and rounded to the nearest integer: \[v_{\text{new}} = \text{round}(v_{\text{original}} + \Delta v)\]
  3. Clamping: The new value is restricted to valid MIDI boundaries: \[v_{\text{clamped}} = \max(1, \min(127, v_{\text{new}}))\]

Context-Aware Refinements

Advanced humanizers adjust \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) dynamically depending on musical context. For example, downbeats might have a lower \(\sigma_{\text{time}}\) (tighter timing) and higher mean velocity \(\mu_{\text{vel}}\), while syncopated ghost notes receive larger deviations. Furthermore, correlated noise (such as pink noise or 1/f noise) is sometimes layered over the Gaussian output to simulate the continuous drift of human tempo over extended musical phrases.
