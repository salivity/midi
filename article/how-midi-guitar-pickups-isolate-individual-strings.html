How MIDI Guitar Pickups Isolate Individual Strings

This article explores the technology behind specialized guitar-to-MIDI pickups, known as hexaphonic or divided pickups. It breaks down how individual electromagnetic and piezoelectric transducers capture each string independently, how physical and electronic designs minimize crosstalk, and how these isolated signals are routed as discrete channels to convert complex polyphonic guitar playing into accurate, monophonic MIDI data streams.

The Hexaphonic Architecture

Standard electric guitar pickups use a single continuous magnetic field and coil structure that sums the vibrations of all strings into a single monophonic electrical output. Because polyphonic pitch detection from a single summed audio source is computationally intensive and prone to pitch-tracking errors, MIDI systems require a divided or "hexaphonic" pickup system.

A hexaphonic pickup is essentially six miniaturized, independent pickups housed in a single casing or bridge assembly. By dedicating an isolated transducer to each individual string, the system avoids mixing the audio signals at the instrument stage.

Electromagnetic vs. Piezoelectric Isolation

Manufacturers primarily rely on two transducer technologies to achieve per-string isolation:

  • Electromagnetic Hex Pickups (e.g., Roland GK Series): These units sit near the bridge and feature six miniature pole pieces, each wound with its own micro-coil. The magnetic field of each pole is tightly focused with a narrow magnetic aperture. This narrow window ensures that the coil only responds to the vertical and horizontal excursions of the string suspended directly above it.
  • Piezoelectric Bridge Saddles (e.g., Graph Tech Ghost, RMC): Instead of sensing electromagnetic fluctuations, piezo systems integrate individual crystal sensors directly into the bridge saddles under each string. The physical contact point provides inherent mechanical isolation: the piezo crystal only measures the downward pressure and direct acoustic vibration of the string resting on that specific saddle.

Mitigating Acoustic and Electromagnetic Crosstalk

Achieving complete separation requires addressing "crosstalk"—the bleed of vibrational energy from one string into an adjacent string's sensor:

  1. Bridge Proximity: Magnetic hex pickups are placed as close to the bridge saddles as possible. Near the termination point, string excursion is smallest, which prevents a vibrating string from physically swaying into the magnetic field of an adjacent pole piece.
  2. Magnetic Shielding: Individual coils within magnetic hex units are often shielded with mu-metal or specialized magnetic barriers to stop flux leakage between neighboring coils.
  3. Mechanical Damping: In piezoelectric systems, saddles are structurally decoupled using non-resonant materials or rubberized dampeners within the bridge plate to prevent soundboard resonance from transferring through the bridge block.

Discrete Signal Routing

Once the six independent analog signals are generated, they cannot share a standard two-conductor guitar cable. The signals travel through a multi-conductor cable—traditionally a 13-pin DIN cable—where each string is assigned its own dedicated pin and ground return wire. This continuous physical isolation shields each channel from radio-frequency interference and capacitive coupling between adjacent wires.

Monophonic Pitch Tracking and MIDI Conversion

At the converter unit (or onboard digital processor), the six incoming signals arrive as distinct analog waveforms. Because each channel contains the vibration of only one string, the processor can treat each input as a simple monophonic source.

Monophonic pitch detection algorithms (such as zero-crossing detection, autocorrelation, or peak tracking) require significantly fewer processing cycles than polyphonic pitch extraction. The converter determines the fundamental frequency (\(f_0\)) of each isolated waveform in milliseconds, mapping the frequency directly to a MIDI note number and pitch-bend data. This individual-channel processing allows the system to accurately translate techniques like single-string bends, hammer-ons, and complex chord voicings without the pitch confusion inherent to mixed audio signals.
