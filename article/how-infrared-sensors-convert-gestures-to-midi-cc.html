How Infrared Sensors Convert Gestures to MIDI CC

Infrared hand-gesture sensors bridge physical movement and electronic music production by transforming the proximity of a performer's hand into continuous digital modulation. This process relies on a multi-stage pipeline: an infrared transceiver measures physical distance via light reflection or photon timing, an onboard microcontroller digitizes and filters the raw signal, a mapping algorithm translates those values into the standard MIDI protocol range, and a serial interface transmits the final Continuous Controller (MIDI CC) messages with low latency.

Optical Distance Detection

The conversion process begins with optical physics using either intensity-based triangulation sensors (such as Sharp GP-series sensors) or modern Time-of-Flight (ToF) sensors (such as the VL53L1X).

Intensity-based sensors project a beam of infrared light (typically around 850 to 940 nanometers) using an IR LED. When a performer places a hand in the beam's path, the light reflects back onto an integrated position-sensitive photodiode (PSD). The physical angle at which the light returns shifts depending on the hand's distance from the emitter, altering the resistance and output voltage across the PSD.

ToF sensors, in contrast, emit pulses from a microscopic vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (VCSEL) and calculate the precise time in picoseconds that photons take to bounce off the hand and return to a Single-Photon Avalanche Diode (SPAD) array. This provides an absolute millimeter measurement that is immune to variations in skin tone or fabric reflectivity.

Signal Digitization and Conditioning

Once the optical sensor captures distance, the physical measurement must be converted into digital data. In analog sensors, the output voltage passes into the Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) of a microcontroller (such as an ARM Cortex or ATmega). The ADC samples the voltage into a discrete numerical value, typically at a resolution of 10-bit (0–1023) or 12-bit (0–4095). Digital ToF sensors perform this conversion internally and stream raw distance metrics directly via an I2C or SPI communication bus.

Raw infrared signals inherently contain noise caused by ambient lighting, electrical fluctuations, and slight hand tremors. Microcontroller firmware applies digital signal processing (DSP) to stabilize the input before it generates control data:

  • Hysteresis and Deadbands: Ignores micro-movements beneath a set threshold to prevent unintended modulation when the hand is held still.
  • Low-Pass Filtering: Implements algorithms such as Exponential Moving Averages (EMA) or Kalman filters to smooth rapid fluctuations while retaining transient speed.
  • Boundary Clamping: Defines minimum and maximum operational ranges (for example, 5 cm to 40 cm). Distance readings beyond these limits are clipped to avoid sending erratic values when a hand enters or exits the sensor's field of view.

Scaling and Musical Curve Mapping

Standard MIDI Continuous Controller parameters require a 7-bit resolution, defining a range of integers between 0 and 127 (or 14-bit high-resolution values between 0 and 16383).

The microcontroller scales the filtered distance metric through mathematical transformation. Because human perception of musical parameters (such as filter cutoffs or volume) is non-linear, firmware often applies logarithmic or exponential transfer functions rather than simple linear interpolation. For example, moving a hand closer to the sensor can be set to exponentially open an audio filter from CC value 0 to 127, providing finer control at closer distances and broader sweeps further away.

MIDI Packet Encoding and Transmission

After calculating the target CC value, the processor packages the integer into a standard asynchronous MIDI byte stream. A standard MIDI CC message requires a three-byte packet:

  1. Status Byte (0xBn): Indicates a Control Change message, where n represents the designated MIDI channel (0–15).
  2. Controller Number (0x00–0x77): Identifies the target parameter (such as CC 1 for Modulation Wheel, CC 7 for Volume, or CC 74 for Filter Brightness).
  3. Data Byte (0x00–0x7F): Represents the mapped distance value from 0 to 127.

To prevent clogging the MIDI bus, the firmware employs change-detection algorithms that transmit packets only when the newly calculated CC value differs from the previously sent value.

Finally, the packet is sent downstream. In traditional hardware setups, it is transmitted through a UART serial line at 31,250 baud over standard 5-pin DIN or TRS cables. In modern setups, the microcontroller operates as a class-compliant USB-MIDI peripheral, sending packets inside high-speed USB endpoints directly to digital audio workstations (DAWs) or synthesizers with sub-millisecond latency.
