How Pad Controllers Sense MIDI Velocity and Pressure

Modern rubber pad controllers rely on Force-Sensing Resistor (FSR) technology to capture expressive musical performances with high dynamic fidelity. This article explores the physical and electrical mechanisms underlying these instruments, detailing how a single sensor layer can determine initial strike speed (MIDI velocity) through the rate of voltage change, while simultaneously tracking continuous, per-pad finger force (polyphonic pressure or aftertouch) through ongoing resistance monitoring.

The Force-Sensing Resistor (FSR)

The foundation of modern pad controllers—such as those found on modern MPCs, Ableton Push, and Maschine units—is the Force-Sensing Resistor (FSR).

An FSR assembly typically consists of:

  • A conductive grid: Interlocking (interdigitated) electrical traces printed on a flexible substrate or printed circuit board (PCB).
  • A semi-conductive layer: A polymer film embedded with electrically conductive nanoparticles (often carbon or metal).
  • An elastomeric pad: The external silicone or rubber pad that acts as a mechanical actuator.

When no pressure is applied, the conductive particles inside the polymer film are physically separated, resulting in an open circuit with near-infinite electrical resistance. When a musician strikes or presses the rubber pad, the elastomer compresses against the FSR. This physical compression forces the conductive particles closer together—a phenomenon governed by percolation theory—and establishes microscopic contact points with the underlying conductive traces. As applied force increases, electrical resistance decreases predictably and non-linearly.

Deriving MIDI Velocity

MIDI velocity represents the speed and intensity of an initial hit, mapped to a scale from 1 to 127. Because the rubber pad does not contain moving parts or multiple contact switches (like traditional piano-style dual-switch keybeds), velocity is calculated mathematically rather than measured directly.

  1. Transient Detection: When a pad is struck, an onboard microcontroller constantly scans the analog voltage output across the FSR matrix at sample rates often exceeding 1,000 Hz.
  2. Delta Measurement (\(\Delta V / \Delta t\)): Upon initial contact, the resistance drops sharply, causing a rapid spike in voltage. The microcontroller measures the rate of this voltage increase over a short time window (typically within the first 1 to 5 milliseconds of impact).
  3. Velocity Translation: A hard, fast strike generates a steep voltage curve in a very short window, whereas a soft strike generates a gradual curve. The firmware maps this slope through a user-selected velocity curve (linear, exponential, or logarithmic) to generate the final MIDI Note-On velocity value.

Capturing Polyphonic Pressure (Aftertouch)

Once the initial strike is registered and the velocity value is transmitted, the controller does not reset the sensor. Instead, it transitions to monitoring steady-state and fluctuating force to generate polyphonic pressure (per-note aftertouch).

Unlike standard channel aftertouch—which uses a single long sensor strip beneath an entire keyboard to affect all notes globally—modern pad grids isolate the FSR nodes beneath each rubber pad:

  1. Independent Electrical Paths: Each pad sits over an isolated sensor zone or an individually multiplexed intersection in a matrix circuit.
  2. Continuous Analog Sampling: While the finger remains on the pad, the microcontroller continuously samples the resistance across that specific node.
  3. Real-Time Mapping: Modulations in holding pressure cause real-time variations in resistance. The hardware converts these continuous analog voltage changes into a stream of 7-bit (0–127) or 14-bit (high-resolution) MIDI Polyphonic Pressure messages or MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) pressure data. Because each sensor is electrically discrete, pressing harder on one pad has no electrical effect on the pressure values generated by adjacent pads.

Mechanical Isolation and Crosstalk Elimination

For this mechanism to function accurately, mechanical isolation is critical. If striking one pad deforms the surrounding silicone enough to compress neighboring sensors, false velocity triggers or incorrect pressure data occur (known as mechanical crosstalk). Manufacturers prevent this by molding the rubber pad sheet with isolated elastomer wells, incorporating air gaps between individual sensors, and employing firmware algorithms that reject secondary shockwaves traveling across adjacent pads.
