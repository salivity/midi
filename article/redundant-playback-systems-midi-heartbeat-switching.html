Redundant Playback Systems: MIDI Heartbeat Switching

In modern concert touring, artists rely heavily on backing tracks, software synths, click tracks, and timecode for lighting and video automation. To ensure a performance continues uninterrupted if a primary computer crashes, touring professionals deploy dual-redundant playback rigs governed by automatic switchboxes using continuous "heartbeat" monitoring. This article outlines the architecture, signal flow, and failover mechanics required to configure a seamless, zero-downtime redundant playback system for live stages.

Dual-Redundant Rig Architecture

A standard touring redundant playback setup consists of two identical systems: Rig A (Primary) and Rig B (Secondary or Backup). Both machines run identical Digital Audio Workstations (DAWs)—typically Ableton Live, Pro Tools, or Apple MainStage—housing mirrored sessions with identical track layouts, plugin instances, and routing.

Both computers interface with dedicated audio and MIDI hardware. Their outputs route directly into an automatic failover switchbox, such as an iConnectivity PlayAUDIO series interface or a Radial SW8 with an automated switcher unit, before reaching the stage boxes and Front of House (FOH) console.

The Heartbeat Signal

The cornerstone of automated failover is the heartbeat: an uninterrupted signal generated by the primary DAW that informs the switchbox that the primary machine is running healthily. While some legacy systems use an ultrasonic or subsonic continuous audio tone, modern MIDI-capable failover systems frequently utilize a continuous stream of MIDI data.

To generate a MIDI heartbeat:

  1. Dedicated Track Creation: A dedicated MIDI track is created inside the DAW session, routed to a specific MIDI output port and channel reserved strictly for the switchbox.
  2. Continuous Output: A looping MIDI sequence plays continuous pitch, Control Change (CC), or MIDI Clock data for the entire duration of the project timeline.
  3. Session Interlock: Because the data is tied to the timeline, the heartbeat ceases not only if the computer crashes completely, but also if the software freezes, encounters a buffer underrun, or accidentally stops playing.

How the Automatic Switchbox Operates

The automatic switchbox constantly monitors the incoming heartbeat port from Rig A. The hardware contains internal logic circuits that measure the interval between incoming heartbeat pulses.

  • Normal Operation: As long as Rig A sends pulses within a predetermined time threshold (often set between 10 to 50 milliseconds), the switchbox keeps its internal relays routed to Rig A's audio and MIDI streams. Rig B runs completely in parallel, but its outputs remain muted by the switcher.
  • Failure Detection: If Rig A crashes, freezes, or experiences a software dropout, the heartbeat stream abruptly stops.
  • Instantaneous Failover: Once the switchbox detects that the heartbeat has lapsed past the allowed threshold, it activates mechanical or digital solid-state relays. The unit instantly cuts Rig A's outputs and opens Rig B's lines, redirecting the audio, click, and MIDI control streams to the PA and stage monitors.

Because the switch happens within milliseconds, the transition is imperceptible to the audience and prevents catastrophic dropouts in Front of House sound, artist in-ear monitors, or venue-wide automation.

Synchronization and Transport Control

For an automatic failover to sound seamless, Rig A and Rig B must play in precise synchronization. If Rig B is even a few milliseconds behind Rig A when a failover occurs, the audience will hear an audible glitch, flam, or rhythmic jump.

Touring engineers maintain synchronization through several methods:

  • Dual-Trigger Controllers: A master MIDI controller (like a dedicated foot pedal or control surface) sends identical, simultaneous "Play" and "Stop" commands to both computers via a passive MIDI splitter or specialized dual-USB MIDI interface.
  • Network MIDI or Timecode: In advanced setups, Rig A continuously generates MIDI Timecode (MTC) or Linear Timecode (LTC) to continuously synchronize the transport position of Rig B. However, hard-locking transport via timecode introduces a risk: if Rig A stutters, Rig B might stutter as well. Consequently, many playback engineers prefer independent, simultaneously triggered transports running from identical solid-state system drives.

Best Practices for Touring Deployment

To guarantee absolute reliability, engineering teams follow strict deployment standards:

  • Isolated Power Supplies: Primary and secondary machines, as well as their interfaces, must be powered by separate battery backups (uninterruptible power supplies, or UPS) to protect against venue power dips.
  • Strict Gain Matching: Levels between Rig A and Rig B must be calibrated to within a fraction of a decibel across every stem to prevent sudden volume spikes during a switch.
  • Pre-Show Failover Drills: During soundcheck, technicians intentionally crash the primary DAW or pull its USB connection while audio is playing to verify that the heartbeat switchbox triggers cleanly and Rig B carries the load without disruption.
