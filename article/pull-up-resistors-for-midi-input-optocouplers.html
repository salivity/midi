Pull-Up Resistors for MIDI Input Optocouplers

Connecting an open-collector optocoupler to a microcontroller's MIDI RX pin requires a pull-up resistor because the optocoupler’s internal phototransistor can only actively sink current to ground, not source current to supply a high voltage. Without a pull-up resistor tied to the microcontroller’s supply voltage, the input pin remains in an electrically floating, undefined state whenever the optocoupler turns off. The pull-up resistor establishes a definitive logic HIGH state, ensuring the microcontroller correctly reads the serial binary stream defined by the standard MIDI protocol.

How Open-Collector Optocouplers Work

In a typical MIDI receiver circuit, an optocoupler such as the 6N138 or 6N137 provides galvanic isolation between external audio equipment and the receiving microcontroller. The output stage of these optocouplers features an "open collector" (or open drain).

This configuration functions essentially as an electronic switch to Ground (GND):

  • Active State (Light Detected): Current flows through the input LED, turning the internal transistor ON. The transistor connects the output pin directly to GND, actively driving the signal to a logic LOW state.
  • Inactive State (No Light): Current stops flowing through the input LED, turning the internal transistor OFF. The connection to GND is broken, creating an open circuit (high impedance).

Because the output stage lacks a complementary transistor connected to positive voltage (\(V_{CC}\)), it cannot actively push the output line HIGH on its own.

The Role of the Pull-Up Resistor

Standard MIDI communication relies on asynchronous serial UART data transmitted at 31.25 kbaud. UART communication requires two discrete, stable voltage levels: a logic LOW (0) and a logic HIGH (1). Furthermore, the idle state of a UART line must be logic HIGH.

A pull-up resistor is placed between the microcontroller's power supply rail (\(V_{CC}\)) and the output pin of the optocoupler (which is connected directly to the microcontroller's RX pin).

The resistor functions in two states:

  1. When the optocoupler is OFF: The pull-up resistor weakly pulls the line voltage up to \(V_{CC}\). The microcontroller reads a stable, reliable logic HIGH (marking the idle state or a serial data bit of 1).
  2. When the optocoupler is ON: The optocoupler's transistor conducts and shunts the current from the pull-up resistor directly to GND. The voltage at the microcontroller's pin drops near 0V, resulting in a reliable logic LOW (a serial data bit of 0).

What Happens Without a Pull-Up Resistor

If the pull-up resistor is omitted:

  • Floating Inputs: When the optocoupler turns off, the microcontroller's high-impedance RX pin floats. Stray electromagnetic interference, capacitive coupling, and thermal noise will cause the pin's voltage to drift randomly between HIGH and LOW thresholds.
  • Corrupted Data and Framing Errors: The microcontroller UART will fail to detect valid start bits, framing boundaries, or standard 31.25 kbaud MIDI messages. Data will be completely corrupted or ignored entirely.

Internal vs. External Pull-Up Resistors

While modern microcontrollers feature built-in internal pull-up resistors, they are generally not suitable for MIDI optocouplers. Internal pull-ups typically range from 20 kΩ to 50 kΩ. Paired with the parasitic capacitance of the circuit board and the optocoupler's internal junction capacitance, this high resistance creates an excessive RC time constant.

A high RC time constant rounds off the sharp transitions of the 31.25 kHz square wave, causing the signal to rise too slowly from LOW to HIGH. This signal degradation results in severe timing jitter and bit read errors. A dedicated external pull-up resistor—typically valued between 270 Ω and 1 kΩ depending on the specific optocoupler and voltage level (3.3V or 5V)—is essential to achieve the sharp, fast rise times mandated by the MIDI specification.
