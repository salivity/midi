Why Is MIDI Channel 10 Reserved for Drums?

In the General MIDI (GM) standard, channel 10 is universally reserved for percussion rather than melodic instruments. This convention originated in the late 1980s with early multi-timbral hardware synthesizers—most notably the Roland MT-32—which permanently assigned its rhythm part to the tenth channel. When the MIDI Manufacturers Association formalized the General MIDI standard in 1991, they adopted this existing industry standard to maintain backward compatibility, ensuring that standard MIDI files would trigger drum kits consistently across different hardware and software synthesizers.

The Role of the Roland MT-32

When the original MIDI 1.0 specification was released in 1983, it established 16 discrete channels for transmitting musical data, but it did not define which instruments should correspond to which channels or patch numbers. A composer could assign a piano to channel 1 and drums to channel 2, but playing that file on a different synthesizer might map the drums to a flute or a trumpet.

In 1987, Roland introduced the MT-32 Multi-Timbral Sound Module. The MT-32 featured eight melodic channels (channels 2 through 9) and reserved channel 10 exclusively for a dedicated rhythm section. The unit became an enormous commercial success and was quickly adopted as the de facto audio standard for personal computer gaming by publishers like Sierra On-Line and LucasArts. Because thousands of game soundtracks and MIDI arrangements were explicitly programmed around the MT-32's channel layout, channel 10 became firmly established as the percussion standard long before an official specification existed.

The General MIDI 1 Standard (1991)

By the early 1990s, the lack of universal consistency across different manufacturers' synthesizers created widespread playback chaos. To solve this, the MIDI Manufacturers Association (MMA) and the Japan MIDI Standards Committee (JMSC) developed General MIDI Level 1 (GM1).

GM1 standardized a list of 128 melodic instrument patches and established consistent performance behaviors. Because Roland played a primary role in shaping the GM specification—basing it largely on their Sound Canvas (SC-55) hardware—and because so much software already targeted the MT-32, the committee officially mandated channel 10 as the designated percussion channel. This decision ensured backward compatibility for existing computer games and MIDI sequences.

How Drum Mapping on Channel 10 Works

Reserving channel 10 for percussion fundamentally alters how MIDI note data is interpreted:

  • Melodic Channels (1–9, 11–16): Note numbers represent pitch. Note 60 is Middle C, and playing different note numbers shifts the pitch of the currently selected patch (e.g., violin or piano).
  • Percussion Channel (10): Note numbers represent distinct instruments within a drum kit. Pitch is not shifted chromatically across the keyboard; instead, each key triggers a different percussion sound.

Under the standardized General MIDI Drum Map, key assignments are fixed between note numbers 35 and 81:

  • Note 35 and 36 trigger Acoustic and Electric Bass Drums.
  • Note 38 and 40 trigger Acoustic and Electric Snares.
  • Note 42 triggers the Closed Hi-Hat.
  • Note 46 triggers the Open Hi-Hat.

Selecting a new "program" or "patch" on channel 10 does not change the melodic instrument, but instead switches the entire drum kit (such as changing from a Standard Kit to a Jazz Kit, Electronic Kit, or Brush Kit), while retaining the same relative key layout.

Modern Legacy

Modern Digital Audio Workstations (DAWs) and virtual instruments (VSTs) allow users to assign percussion to any channel or track without restriction. However, the channel 10 convention remains deeply embedded in the architecture of standard MIDI files (.mid), legacy hardware modules, operating system synthesizers (like Microsoft GS Wavetable Synth), and karaoke players to ensure instant, predictable playback without manual reconfiguration.
