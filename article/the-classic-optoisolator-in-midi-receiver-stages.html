The Classic Optoisolator in MIDI Receiver Stages

This article explores the optoisolators that defined the hardware implementation of the MIDI standard, focusing primarily on the Sharp PC900 and the Hewlett-Packard 6N138. It details why galvanic isolation is critical to the MIDI specification, how these specific components solved signal integrity and ground loop challenges, and how their technical characteristics made them ubiquitous in vintage and modern electronic musical instruments.

The MIDI 1.0 specification, finalized in 1983, mandated optical isolation at every MIDI IN port to eliminate ground loops between interconnected synthesizers, sequencers, and audio gear. Ground loops introduce audible 50/60 Hz mains hum and can potentially damage equipment if significant voltage potentials exist between disparate electrical grounds. To achieve this isolation, MIDI utilizes a 5 mA current-loop configuration operating at a baud rate of 31.25 kbps.

In the original MIDI 1.0 Detailed Specification, the Sharp PC900 (often designated as PC900V) was explicitly recommended and quickly became the ubiquitous standard for first-generation MIDI receiver stages.

The PC900 rose to dominance because of its internal architecture. Unlike simple phototransistor couplers, the PC900 integrates an infrared LED optically coupled to a specialized receiver chip that includes a photodiode, an amplifier, and a built-in Schmitt trigger output stage. This architecture provides several distinct advantages:

  • Fast Switching Speeds: The PC900 provides propagation delay times well under 2 microseconds, ensuring that the 32-microsecond bit periods of the 31.25 kbps MIDI data stream do not suffer from severe pulse-width distortion.
  • Sharp Signal Edges: The integrated Schmitt trigger cleanly regenerates the logic levels, converting sluggish analog rise and fall times into sharp, digital square waves compatible with standard TTL or CMOS microcontrollers.
  • Minimal Component Count: Because the logic inverter and threshold circuitry are built into the 6-pin DIP package, synthesizer manufacturers could implement a compliant receiver with virtually no external pulse-shaping hardware.

As the Sharp PC900 faced supply constraints and eventual obsolescence, the 6N138 (originally developed by Hewlett-Packard) became the secondary ubiquitous optoisolator used across classic and modern MIDI hardware.

The 6N138 is a low-input-current, high-gain optocoupler featuring an integrated Darlington phototransistor output. Its low LED forward-current requirement (often operating reliably at under 1.6 mA) made it ideal for driving from low-power logic gates. However, because it utilizes a Darlington configuration, its turn-off time is inherently slower than that of the PC900 due to stored charge in the second transistor base. Designers routinely addressed this by adding a bypass resistor between base pin 7 and emitter pin 5, or by selecting a relatively stiff pull-up resistor (typically between 280 ohms and 470 ohms) on the open-collector output to meet the strict rise-time demands of the MIDI protocol.

While other alternatives like the Fairchild 6N137 and the Lite-On H11L1 later entered production designs, the Sharp PC900 remains the quintessential historic reference for MIDI input design, with the 6N138 serving as its most enduring, universally recognized companion.
