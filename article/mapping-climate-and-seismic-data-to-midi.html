Mapping Climate and Seismic Data to MIDI

Data sonification transforms complex scientific measurements into auditory experiences by converting numerical values into sound parameters. This article explains the technical process scientists use to sonify seismic wave activity and climate temperature records using the Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) protocol. By scaling real-world datasets into MIDI attributes such as pitch, velocity, and duration, researchers create accurate acoustic representations of physical phenomena to identify hidden patterns, monitor real-time events, and communicate environmental trends to the public.

1. Data Collection and Preprocessing

Before translating data into sound, raw scientific inputs must be cleaned, filtered, and normalized.

  • Seismic Data: Seismometers record ground displacement, velocity, or acceleration across three spatial axes at high sampling rates (often 20 to 100 Hz). The resulting time-series data contains rapid oscillations representing primary (P) waves, secondary (S) waves, and surface waves.
  • Climate Data: Climate datasets, such as global surface temperature anomalies or ice core records, operate on much longer timescales (monthly, yearly, or millennial increments) with smaller, gradual variance.

Because standard MIDI operates on discrete 7-bit values (integers ranging from 0 to 127), continuous scientific metrics must be scaled mathematically. Using linear interpolation or min-max normalization, scientists map the lowest observed value in a dataset to 0 and the highest to 127, or constrain them to a musically practical sub-range (such as 36 to 96, covering typical instrumental registers).

2. Parameter Mapping Strategies

Sonification relies on assigning specific scientific variables to corresponding MIDI message types.

Pitch (Note Number)

The MIDI note range (0–127, where 60 represents Middle C) is most commonly mapped to primary indicators of magnitude or state:

  • Climate: Global mean temperature anomalies are typically assigned to pitch. Baseline historical temperatures trigger lower pitches, while positive temperature anomalies trigger higher pitches, allowing listeners to perceive global warming as an upward melodic trajectory.
  • Seismology: Frequency content or dominant wave frequency can determine pitch. Alternatively, pitch can reflect the absolute vertical displacement of the ground. Microtonal shifts can be achieved by utilizing MIDI Pitch Bend messages alongside standard note numbers.

Velocity (Amplitude and Intensity)

MIDI velocity ranges from 0 (silent) to 127 (maximum strike force), governing loudness and timbre:

  • Seismic Waveforms: Ground acceleration or signal amplitude maps directly to MIDI velocity. Ambient background noise produces soft velocities, whereas the sudden onset of an S-wave triggers high-velocity notes, mirroring the physical impact of the earthquake.
  • Climate: Velocity can represent secondary variables, such as the rate of temperature change (acceleration) or the statistical uncertainty of a measurement.

Timing, Tempo, and Duration

Scientific time must be compressed or expanded to fit the human auditory range:

  • Time Compression: Climate records spanning centuries are compressed into minutes by defining a fixed temporal ratio (for example, one calendar year equals one sixteenth note, or one decade equals one beat at 120 BPM).
  • Time Scaling: High-frequency seismic events lasting only seconds can be played back at real-time speeds to create percussive bursts, or slowed down significantly to expose the distinct arrivals of P-waves and S-waves.

Continuous Controllers (MIDI CC)

For continuous variables that do not fit discrete note triggers, scientists use MIDI Continuous Controller (CC) messages:

  • CC 7 (Volume) and CC 11 (Expression): Modulate ongoing drone sounds to reflect sustained seismic rumbling or continuous sea-surface temperature changes.
  • CC 1 (Modulation) and CC 74 (Filter Cutoff): Alter sound brightness or distortion to represent atmospheric pressure, CO2 concentration, or secondary wave complexity.

3. Implementation and Synthesis

The transformation pipeline is usually programmed using computational environments such as Python (utilizing libraries like mido, pretty_midi, or scipy), MATLAB, or visual programming languages like Max/MSP and Pure Data.

The algorithm iterates through the ordered time-series array, calculates corresponding MIDI values, and writes a standard .mid file or transmits live MIDI events over a virtual bus. These MIDI signals are routed into software synthesizers or Digital Audio Workstations (DAWs), where specific timbres (such as low sine waves for deep crustal movements or orchestral instruments for climate shifts) are assigned to the output channels.

4. Analytical Advantages

Mapping data to MIDI provides distinct analytical benefits over visual representation alone. The human auditory system is exceptionally sensitive to rhythm, cadence, and transient changes, allowing researchers to perceive complex multi-variable relationships and subtle harmonic anomalies that might remain undetected in dense two-dimensional scatter plots.
