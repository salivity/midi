How to Calibrate MIDI Solenoid Piano Dynamics

Transforming a mechanical MIDI solenoid piano into an expressive instrument capable of replicating human touch requires systematic physical and digital calibration. This guide covers the essential engineering steps—including mechanical pre-regulation, solenoid force profiling, individual key offset mapping, and release velocity calibration—required to bridge the gap between raw binary MIDI data and the nuanced dynamics of an acoustic performance.

1. Mechanical Pre-Regulation and Physical Alignment

Before attempting any software or electrical calibration, the acoustic piano and the solenoid rail must be mechanically aligned.

  • Piano Action Regulation: Ensure the acoustic piano's action is fully regulated to standard factory specifications. Uniform key dip, let-off (escapement), drop, and hammer blow distance across all 88 keys provide a consistent mechanical baseline.
  • Rail Placement and Plunger Spacing: Mount the solenoid rail so that each plunger contacts the key or wippen dead-center. Plungers must move freely without lateral friction or binding. Set the rest gap (the distance between the tip of the plunger and the underside of the key) uniformly—typically between 1 to 2 millimeters—to allow the key to return fully without rattling.

2. Identifying Thresholds for Minimum and Maximum Force

Solenoids convert electrical pulses (typically Pulse Width Modulation or PWM) into magnetic force. Because bass keys have heavier hammers and dampers than treble keys, each key requires a unique minimum and maximum electrical threshold.

  • Deadband and Minimum Strike (Pianissimo): Determine the exact pulse width required to overcome static inertia and key weight. The minimum threshold is reached when the solenoid delivers just enough momentum to throw the hammer past escapement to produce an audible, clean pianissimo (\(pp\)) without choking or ghosting.
  • Saturation and Maximum Strike (Fortissimo): Establish the upper limit where increases in pulse width or voltage no longer produce a measurable increase in acoustic volume or hammer speed. Limiting the maximum drive prevents solenoid overheating, mechanical wear, and harsh string impacts.

3. Non-Linear Electromechanical Mapping

Solenoid magnetic force is naturally non-linear; as the ferromagnetic plunger moves deeper into the coil, the magnetic pull increases exponentially even under constant current.

  • PWM-to-Force Linearization: Implement an algorithm that compensates for this inverse-square magnetic behavior. Human fingers exert a push characterized by follow-through, whereas an uncalibrated solenoid delivers an aggressive snap.
  • Velocity Curve Profiling: Map standard MIDI velocities (1–127) onto a tailored power curve rather than a linear slope. Human perception of loudness (sones/phons) is logarithmic; translating MIDI values through an exponential power function ensures proportional acoustic steps between dynamic levels.

4. Per-Key Dynamic Profiling (Individual Offsets)

Friction, hammer mass, and wood elasticity vary across an 88-key piano action, meaning a uniform global velocity curve is insufficient.

  • Sensor-Based Feedback: Utilize an optical sensor rail (measuring time-of-flight of the hammer shanks just before striking the string) or an acoustic measurement microphone.
  • Automated Strike Sweep: Run calibration software that triggers each key through discrete MIDI velocity steps (e.g., values 16, 32, 64, 96, 127).
  • Per-Note Look-Up Tables (LUT): Record the resulting hammer velocity or decibel output for each strike. Generate an 88-column calibration matrix containing key-specific gain and offset values, ensuring that a MIDI velocity of 64 produces the exact same hammer velocity whether struck in the bass, middle, or treble register.

5. Calibrating Note-Off and Release Damping

Human expression depends as much on how a key is released as how it is struck. Abruptly cutting current to a solenoid causes the plunger to drop instantly, causing key bounce and abrupt damper cutoff.

  • Variable Decay Curves: Calibrate the decay rate of the solenoid drive current to reflect the incoming MIDI Note-Off velocity.
  • Controlled Descent: Implement a soft-release routine (controlled PWM ramp-down) that allows the damper felt to settle gently onto the vibrating string. This replicates the finger-cushioned release used by pianists for smooth legato transitions and controlled staccato articulation.

6. Pedal State Dynamic Compensation

Engaging the sustain (damper) pedal lifts all dampers off the strings simultaneously, which drastically reduces the physical resistance felt by the keys.

  • Pedal-Down Offset: Program an automated offset into the system's firmware that detects the position of the MIDI sustain pedal (CC64).
  • Energy Reduction: When the dampers are raised, reduce the solenoid strike energy by a calibrated percentage (typically 3% to 6%) across the keyboard. This prevents notes played with the pedal engaged from sounding noticeably louder or harsher than notes played with the pedal up.
