Why MIDI Synths Need an Inverter After the Optoisolator

In MIDI hardware design, the input stage relies on an optoisolator to provide galvanic isolation between connected devices, preventing ground loops and electrical interference. When an open-collector optoisolator outputs an inverted digital signal, the synthesizer must pass this signal through a logic inverter stage before routing it to the internal Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter (UART). This article explains the technical reasons for this requirement, detailing how the inverter restores standard UART polarity, sharpens degraded slew rates, and ensures stable data communication within the synthesizer.

Restoring Standard UART Polarity

MIDI communication relies on standard asynchronous serial data at a rate of 31.25 kbaud. A standard UART receiver expects the signal line to abide by standard Non-Return-to-Zero (NRZ) serial framing:

  • Idle State (Mark): Logic HIGH (binary 1)
  • Start Bit (Space): Logic LOW (binary 0)
  • Stop Bit: Logic HIGH (binary 1)

Depending on how the optoisolator's phototransistor and input LED are biased, an open-collector stage commonly inverts the incoming logic stream. When the sending device asserts a logic LOW to transmit a start bit, the current loop turns the optoisolator's internal LED on, switching on the phototransistor and pulling the open-collector output to ground (LOW). Conversely, when no current flows (idle), the phototransistor stays off, and a pull-up resistor pulls the collector to the supply rail (HIGH).

However, in circuit configurations where the optoisolator output produces an active-high inverted signal relative to the microprocessor’s expectations, the synthesizer's UART will misinterpret the line. If an idle state appears as LOW and a start bit appears as HIGH, the receiving UART cannot recognize framing boundaries, resulting in framing errors, false start bits, or total communication failure. Inserting a logic inverter stage flips the bitstream back to the correct polarity expected by the UART.

Correcting Asymmetric Rise and Fall Times

Open-collector optoisolator outputs suffer from an inherent physical asymmetry between their falling and rising edges:

  1. Active Pull-Down (Fast): When the phototransistor turns on, it directly shunts the output pin to ground with low impedance, producing a rapid, crisp falling edge.
  2. Passive Pull-Up (Slow): When the phototransistor turns off, it cannot actively drive the line high. Instead, an external pull-up resistor must charge the parasitic capacitance of the phototransistor junction, PCB traces, and receiving inputs. This produces an exponential RC charging curve, leading to a significantly slower rising edge.

This difference creates pulse-width distortion. Serial bits that spend more time rising than falling change the effective duration of high and low pulses, shrinking the valid sampling window in the center of each bit.

Waveform Squaring with Schmitt Trigger Inverters

To resolve the timing degradation caused by the passive pull-up, the inverter stage in a MIDI input circuit is typically implemented using a Schmitt trigger inverter (such as the 74HCT14 or 74LVC14).

A Schmitt trigger provides two distinct voltage thresholds: an upper threshold (\(V_{\text{T}+}\)) for rising signals and a lower threshold (\(V_{\text{T}-}\)) for falling signals. As the slow, exponential voltage ramp rises across the pull-up resistor, the Schmitt trigger snaps instantly to a solid digital state the moment the threshold is crossed. This restores steep, vertical edges to the digital waveform, virtually eliminating timing jitter and preventing false transitions caused by high-frequency electrical noise riding on the analog ramp.

Providing Clean Logic Levels and Fan-Out

The collector of an optoisolator is a high-impedance node that is sensitive to electrical noise and trace loading inside the synthesizer chassis. Routing this high-impedance signal across a circuit board directly to a microcontroller or digital signal processor can cause signal degradation.

The inverter stage acts as an active digital buffer with a low-impedance totem-pole (push-pull) output. This allows the signal to cleanly drive the input capacitance of the synthesizer’s CPU, secondary logic circuits, or a hardware "MIDI Thru" re-transmission buffer without altering pulse timing or loading down the optoisolator output.
