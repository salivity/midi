Why MIDI Delay Uses Polyphony and Audio Delay Does Not

When producing music with synthesizers, using a MIDI delay consumes available polyphony voices, while an audio delay does not use any. This fundamental difference occurs because of where each effect sits in the signal chain and what type of information it duplicates. A MIDI delay duplicates performance data (Note On and Note Off commands) before sound is generated, forcing the synthesizer's sound engine to allocate a new voice for every echo. In contrast, an audio delay captures and replays the final audio signal after it has already been synthesized, requiring zero additional processing from the synthesizer's voice architecture.

Understanding Synthesizer Polyphony

Polyphony refers to the maximum number of independent notes or "voices" a synthesizer can generate simultaneously. In both hardware and software synthesizers, each voice requires dedicated resources—such as oscillators, filters, envelope generators, and LFOs. If an instrument has an 8-voice polyphony limit, it can only synthesize eight distinct sound paths at any given moment. Once that limit is exceeded, older notes are cut off to make room for new ones through a process called voice stealing.

How MIDI Delay Operates

MIDI does not transmit sound; it transmits control data. When you play a key on a MIDI keyboard, it transmits messages such as Note On, pitch, velocity, and eventually Note Off.

A MIDI delay unit sits before the sound generation stage. When you play a single note:

  1. The MIDI delay receives the initial Note On event and sends it to the synthesizer engine.
  2. The delay processor generates subsequent Note On events at timed intervals, typically with decreasing velocity values to simulate decay.
  3. Every time the synthesizer engine receives one of these generated Note On messages, its voice-allocation algorithm treats it as a newly played key.

Consequently, if you play a 4-note chord into a MIDI delay set to repeat three times, the synthesizer must generate 16 distinct voices (\(4 \text{ original} + 12 \text{ repeats}\)). If your synthesizer only has 8-voice polyphony, the delay repeats will immediately trigger voice stealing, cutting off the sustain of earlier notes.

How Audio Delay Operates

An audio delay processor operates after the sound engine has created the audio signal. It receives audio samples (in digital systems) or continuous electrical voltages (in analog systems).

When using an audio delay:

  1. You trigger a note, and the synthesizer allocates a single voice to generate the sound.
  2. The resulting audio waveform exits the synth's voice architecture and enters the delay effect (either an onboard effect processor, an external pedal, or a DAW plugin).
  3. The delay records that audio signal into a temporary memory buffer (such as RAM or a bucket-brigade device) and plays it back after a designated time, reducing the volume on subsequent iterations.

Because the synthesis process has already occurred, the synthesizer's voice allocation engine is entirely uninvolved in generating the repeats. Playing a 4-note chord into an audio delay requires only 4 synthesizer voices, regardless of whether the delay repeats once or indefinitely.

Key Functional Differences

  • Signal Type: MIDI delay processes digital instructions; audio delay processes audio waveforms.
  • Position in Chain: MIDI delay is pre-synthesis; audio delay is post-synthesis.
  • Per-Echo Modulation: MIDI delay allows each echo to trigger fresh envelopes, filter sweeps, and velocity-dependent timbral changes per repeat. Audio delay simply plays back copies of the original, already-shaped sound.
  • Resource Cost: MIDI delay costs synth polyphony; audio delay costs general DSP or memory buffer space.
