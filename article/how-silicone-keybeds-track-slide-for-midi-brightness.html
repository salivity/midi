How Silicone Keybeds Track Slide for MIDI Brightness

Continuous silicone keybeds track longitudinal finger slide by pairing flexible surface elastomers with high-density sensor arrays that monitor positional shifts along the vertical axis of each key. By calculating the movement of a finger's pressure center in real time, the controller's internal processor translates physical motion into continuous control data. Through MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE), these coordinates are transmitted as dedicated per-note control change messages—typically MIDI CC 74—allowing independent modulation of acoustic brightness and timbre for every held note.

The Sensor Matrix Beneath the Silicone

Beneath the pliable, seamless silicone surface lies an advanced sensor grid. Most modern expressive keybeds utilize either a dense array of Force-Sensing Resistors (FSRs) or continuous capacitive sensor strips arranged along the length of each keywave.

When a player presses into the silicone, the material compresses and distributes mechanical force onto the sensor plane below. Because the silicone is pliable and continuous, it creates a footprint of contact rather than a single pinpoint. The underlying sensor layer detects this contact area across multiple sensing nodes simultaneously.

Detecting Longitudinal Motion

Longitudinal slide refers to the forward-and-backward movement along the long axis (Y-axis) of a key. Tracking this motion involves three primary steps:

  1. Continuous High-Speed Scanning: The hardware controller scans the sensor matrix at rates often exceeding 1,000 Hz to capture micro-movements without perceptible latency.
  2. Centroid Calculation: Instead of reading only raw electrical resistance or capacitance at discrete points, the firmware calculates the "center of force" or centroid of the contact patch. As the finger glides along the silicone, the highest concentration of pressure shifts across neighboring sensor elements.
  3. Interpolation Algorithms: Sophisticated mathematical interpolation estimates the exact position of the finger between discrete physical sensor nodes. This prevents stepping artifacts and yields a smooth, continuous stream of positional data.

Translation to Per-Note MIDI CC 74

Standard MIDI sends control change messages globally across a single channel, meaning a brightness shift would affect all sounding notes simultaneously. Continuous keybeds overcome this limitation using MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE).

When a note is triggered, the controller assigns that specific voice to its own dedicated MIDI channel within a defined zone. As the tracking algorithm detects movement along the Y-axis:

  • The firmware maps the calculated Y-coordinate to values ranging from 0 to 127 (or higher resolution 14-bit MIDI values).
  • These values are formatted as MIDI CC 74 messages, which represent "Slide" or "Timbre" in the MPE specification.
  • The message is sent exclusively through the MIDI channel assigned to that specific finger.

Synthesizer Response to Brightness Shifts

When the receiving MPE-compatible software or hardware synthesizer receives CC 74 data on a specific channel, it routes that data directly to the sound engine parameters assigned to that single voice. In most default synthesizer patches, CC 74 is routed to the cutoff frequency of a low-pass filter.

Moving the finger upward along the silicone keybed raises the filter cutoff, letting higher harmonic frequencies pass through to create a brighter timbre. Sliding the finger downward lowers the cutoff, darkening the sound. Because the sensing and transmission occur independently per key, one note can open up in brightness while adjacent held notes remain completely unaffected.
