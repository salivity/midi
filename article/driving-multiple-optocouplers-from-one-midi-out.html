Driving Multiple Optocouplers from One MIDI Out

Attempting to drive multiple optocoupler inputs in parallel from a single passive MIDI Out overloads the current-loop interface, leading to severe signal degradation and communication failure. Because the classic 5-pin DIN MIDI specification relies on a dedicated 5 milliamp (mA) current loop calibrated for a single receiver, splitting this passive loop across multiple receiving diodes causes current starvation, timing jitter, bit errors, and potential "current hogging." To reliably distribute one MIDI Out signal to multiple inputs, you must use an active, powered MIDI Thru or splitter box rather than a passive parallel tap.

Current Starvation

Standard 5V MIDI uses a current-loop architecture governed by two 220-ohm current-limiting resistors at the transmitter (MIDI Out) and a single optocoupler LED at the receiver (MIDI In). This network is balanced to push approximately 5mA through the optocoupler, which is the optimal operating forward current (\(I_F\)) to reliably drive the receiver’s internal phototransistor.

When you wire two or more optocoupler LEDs in parallel across the same MIDI Out lines (pins 4 and 5), the total available current is divided among the branches. With two receivers, each optocoupler receives only about 2.5mA. Most standard MIDI optocouplers (such as the 6N138 or PC900) require at least 5mA to switch cleanly at the standard 31.25 kbaud data rate. Operating below this threshold prevents the phototransistor from fully saturating into an "on" state.

Forward Voltage Mismatch and Current Hogging

Infrared LEDs inside optocouplers have slight component-to-component variations in their forward voltage drop (\(V_F\)), typically between 1.1V and 1.5V. When two diodes are connected in direct parallel without separate balancing resistors, the diode with the slightly lower forward voltage will conduct earlier and draw the majority of the current. This "current hogging" phenomenon means one receiving device may receive enough current to trigger intermittently, while the second device receives virtually none and fails entirely.

Slew Rate Degradation and Jitter

Optocouplers rely on sufficient drive current to quickly charge and discharge internal junction capacitances. Lowering the drive current dramatically slows the optocoupler's turn-on and turn-off response times:

  • Pulse-Width Distortion: Slower edge transitions round off square-wave pulses, causing high-to-low and low-to-high transitions to become asymmetrical.
  • Framing Errors: Because MIDI relies on asynchronous serial transmission with tight timing tolerances, edge delays translate directly into timing jitter, corrupted bytes, and UART framing errors.
  • Partial Data Loss: Fast, continuous data streams (such as pitch bend, aftertouch, or MIDI clock messages) will fail first, manifesting as dropped notes, hanging notes, or lost sync.

Increased Susceptibility to Cable Capacitance

Passive MIDI lines are already sensitive to the parasitic capacitance of long cable runs. Adding multiple parallel receiver cables multiplies total capacitance while halving the drive current. This combination functions as a severe low-pass filter, turning the sharp digital transitions into slow analog ramps that standard receiver hardware cannot decode.

Correct Implementation

Because of these electrical limitations, passive Y-splitters should never be used on MIDI outputs. Distributing a single MIDI transmitter to multiple destinations requires an active MIDI Thru circuit consisting of a single optocoupler input followed by dedicated digital logic buffers (such as a 74HCT14 or 74HC04) that drive separate, isolated 220-ohm resistor pairs for each output port.
