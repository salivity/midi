MIDI Voice Stealing Explained

When a sixteen-voice polyphonic synthesizer receives a seventeen-note chord, it encounters polyphony exhaustion, meaning the incoming MIDI data demands more simultaneous sound engines than the hardware or software possesses. To handle this limitation, the synthesizer activates a process known as voice stealing (or voice re-allocation). This article explains how the synthesizer chooses which note to silence, the algorithms behind that decision, and the audible impact on performance.

The Voice Allocation Bottleneck

Polyphony refers to the number of individual, independent voices a synthesizer can generate at once. Each voice requires its own set of oscillators, filters, envelope generators, and amplifiers. A sixteen-voice synthesizer can strictly calculate and output sixteen distinct audio streams concurrently.

When seventeen simultaneous MIDI Note-On messages arrive at the input, the synthesizer's internal voice-allocation manager attempts to assign each note to an open voice slot. Once sixteen voices are assigned, the synthesizer has no free resources left to generate the seventeenth note. Rather than ignoring the new note completely, the synthesizer must terminate one of the currently sounding voices and reassign its engine to play the new pitch.

How the Synthesizer Selects Which Voice to Steal

Synthesizers rely on pre-programmed priority algorithms to determine which of the active sixteen notes should be interrupted. The most common voice-stealing strategies include:

  • Oldest Note First (First-In, First-Out): The synthesizer tracks the chronological order in which keys were triggered. It terminates the note that has been sounding the longest, assuming that earlier notes are less musically critical than newly played ones.
  • Lowest Amplitude or Release Stage: The synth evaluates the envelope phases and current volumes of all active voices. It steals from the note closest to silence—such as a note already in its release phase—to make the cutoff as imperceptible as possible.
  • Lowest Pitch Priority: In performance contexts where bass lines are critical, synths often protect lower frequencies and instead steal notes from the middle or higher registers.
  • Highest Pitch Priority: Conversely, some patches protect the highest note, ensuring the melody line remains intact while sacrificing harmony notes in the middle.
  • Duplicate Note Preference: If the seventeenth note shares a pitch with one of the active sixteen notes, the synthesizer will invariably steal and retrigger that specific voice rather than cutting off a different pitch.

The Audible Result

When the chosen voice is stolen, the synthesizer abruptly resets its envelope generators to the attack phase of the new note. To prevent an audible, harsh digital click caused by an instantaneous jump to zero in the waveform, modern synthesizers apply a micro-fade (often between 1 and 5 milliseconds) to ramp down the old note before starting the seventeenth note. The listener hears fifteen sustained notes, one cleanly truncated note, and the immediate onset of the new seventeenth note.
