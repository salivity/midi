Polyphonic MIDI to CV Voice Stealing Algorithms

Polyphonic MIDI-to-CV converters bridge the gap between digital MIDI controllers and analog modular synthesizers by translating polyphonic data into multiple pairs of pitch Control Voltage (CV) and Gate signals. Because modular systems have a fixed, finite number of physical voice channels—typically two, four, or eight—these converters must actively manage situations where the number of played notes exceeds available physical outputs. Voice stealing algorithms govern which active analog voice is silenced and reassigned to articulate a new incoming MIDI note, directly impacting the musical phrasing, chord transitions, and natural decay behavior of the patch.

Oldest-Note (First-In, First-Out)

Oldest-Note priority reallocates the voice that has been sounding for the longest continuous duration. When a performer exceeds the polyphony threshold, the system identifies the earliest triggered gate and reassigns that channel to the incoming note. This is the most common default algorithm for standard polyphonic playing, such as piano-style chord progressions and pads. Because human ears naturally focus on recent harmonic changes, stealing the oldest voice minimizes perceived disruption to the overall harmony.

Round-Robin (Cyclic Allocation)

Round-Robin allocation steps sequentially through the physical voice outputs with each new Note-On event (e.g., Voice 1, Voice 2, Voice 3, Voice 4, and back to Voice 1). When all channels are engaged, voice stealing strictly follows this continuous rotation, overwriting the next physical channel in the cycle regardless of pitch or duration. This algorithm prevents uneven component wear and produces dynamic stereo or timbre variations when voices are panned across the stereo field or calibrated with slight analog variances. However, rapid arpeggios can cause premature cutting of long envelope release tails if the cycle advances faster than the release stage.

Pitch-Priority (Highest-Note and Lowest-Note)

Pitch-priority algorithms evaluate the incoming and active notes by musical frequency rather than timing:

  • Lowest-Note Stealing (High-Note Priority): The converter protects the highest notes—often the musical melody or top line of a chord voicing—and sacrifices the lowest active pitch to make room for new notes.
  • Highest-Note Stealing (Low-Note Priority): The converter shields the lowest pitch—typically the bass foundation of a chord—from being cut off, stealing intermediate or higher notes instead.

Some modules employ dynamic variants where an incoming note outside the current pitch range steals the voice closest to it, preserving the outer boundaries of the chord voicing.

Same-Note Reassignment

Rather than stealing an arbitrary active voice when a previously struck note is retriggered, this sub-algorithm reallocates the specific voice channel already assigned to that exact MIDI pitch. This prevents multiple physical channels from sounding the same pitch simultaneously, conserving polyphony for unique notes and allowing the envelope on that single voice to re-articulate naturally.

Lowest-Velocity and Amplitude-Aware Stealing

Advanced digital MIDI-to-CV interfaces monitor incoming velocity values or calculate simulated envelope stages. When polyphony is exhausted, the converter steals the channel with the lowest initial velocity, or the channel whose internal release timer indicates the amplitude envelope is closest to zero. This ensures that prominently struck notes and active swells remain audible while quiet or decaying notes are cut first.

Common Hardware Implementations

Modern polyphonic converters—such as the Expert Sleepers FH-2, Mutable Instruments Yarns (and its open-source derivatives), Intellijel Dixie-era voice allocators, and the Vermona qMI 2—frequently provide user-selectable allocation menus. These typically group the behaviors into categories like "Poly / Allocation Mode" (Rotate/Round-Robin, Reassign, or Reset) paired with explicit "Priority" settings (Last, Low, High) to accommodate different musical contexts like monophonic basslines, polyphonic pads, or layered multitimbral patches.
