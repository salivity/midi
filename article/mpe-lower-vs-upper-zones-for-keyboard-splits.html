MPE Lower vs Upper Zones for Keyboard Splits

MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) expands traditional MIDI by allowing multidimensional per-note control across separate channels. When dividing a single physical controller into distinct performance areas, MPE relies on a dual-zone architecture comprising a Lower Zone and an Upper Zone. This article explores how configuring Lower and Upper Zones facilitates keyboard splits, manages independent voice allocation, and prevents control data interference between hands.

The Architecture of MPE Zones

In standard MIDI, pitch bend, modulation, and aftertouch apply globally to all active notes on a given channel. MPE overcomes this limitation by assigning individual notes to dedicated "Member Channels," while sending common, global parameters across a designated "Master Channel."

To support keyboard splits without channel conflicts, the MPE specification standardizes two specific, non-overlapping zones:

  • Lower Zone: Uses MIDI Channel 1 as its Master Channel. Its Member Channels are allocated sequentially upward, starting from Channel 2 (e.g., Channels 2 through 8).
  • Upper Zone: Uses MIDI Channel 16 as its Master Channel. Its Member Channels are allocated sequentially downward, starting from Channel 15 (e.g., Channels 9 through 15).

How Zones Enable Keyboard Splitting

Dividing an MPE keyboard into two regions requires more than simply mapping note numbers; each side of the split must retain independent polyphonic expression and global settings.

1. Independent Sound Engine Addressing

By assigning the left side of the keyboard to the Lower Zone and the right side to the Upper Zone, the hardware can communicate simultaneously with two completely different virtual instruments or multi-timbral synth layers. A synth engine listening to the Lower Zone (Channel 1 + associated members) acts entirely independently of an engine configured to receive the Upper Zone (Channel 16 + associated members).

2. Isolation of Global Controls

Each zone retains its own Master Channel. If a performer uses a sustain pedal, mod wheel, or master pitch bend assigned to the Lower Zone (Channel 1), only the notes sounding within that zone are affected. The Upper Zone (Channel 16) remains isolated, preventing unwanted modulation or pitch changes on the right-hand part.

3. Dynamic Member Channel Distribution

Keyboard splits require setting the size of each zone based on polyphony needs. MPE hardware allows the user to define the number of active Member Channels per zone:

  • Example Configuration: A user can assign 7 Member Channels to the Lower Zone (Channels 2–8) for a bass or pad patch, and 7 Member Channels to the Upper Zone (Channels 9–15) for a lead or melodic patch.
  • Expression Integrity: Because the channel ranges never overlap, polyphonic gestures—such as per-note glide, pressure (aftertouch), and slide (CC74)—remain strictly confined to the zone in which the key was struck.

Practical Advantages in Performance

  • True Polyphonic Expression on Both Sides: Traditional splits often strip down expressive capabilities on secondary zones. MPE splits provide full multidimensional control across both hands simultaneously.
  • Plug-and-Play Standardization: Because the Master Channel rules (1 for Lower, 16 for Upper) are strictly defined by the MIDI Association, compatible digital audio workstations (DAWs) and software synthesizers can automatically route and parse split MPE data without complex manual mapping.
