1V/Octave vs Hz/V: Analog MIDI Pitch Scaling

When connecting digital gear to analog synthesizers using a MIDI-to-CV (Control Voltage) interface, pitch is controlled through voltage signals rather than digital data. The primary difference between the 1V/Octave (Volt per Octave) and Hz/V (Hertz per Volt) standards lies in the mathematical relationship between the voltage sent by the interface and the resulting audio frequency produced by the oscillator. Understanding this distinction is essential for ensuring that your MIDI notes track pitch accurately across the keyboard on different vintage and modern synthesizers.

The 1V/Octave Standard

The 1V/Octave standard is the most widely adopted pitch-scaling method in the analog synthesizer world, originally popularized by Moog and adopted as the universal standard for Eurorack modular systems, Roland, Sequential Circuits, and ARP.

In this system, pitch scaling is logarithmic:

  • Adding exactly 1.0 Volt doubles the frequency, raising the pitch by one octave.
  • Each semitone corresponds to an increase of 1/12th of a Volt (approximately 0.0833V).

Because human hearing perceives pitch logarithmically, 1V/Octave provides an intuitive relationship where every key step increases by a fixed, equal voltage increment. To achieve this, the analog oscillator (VCO) must incorporate an internal exponential converter circuit to translate linear voltage changes into exponential frequency changes.

The Hz/V Standard

The Hz/V standard, primarily used by vintage Korg (such as the MS-10 and MS-20) and Yamaha (CS-series) synthesizers, uses a linear relationship between voltage and frequency.

In this system:

  • Voltage is directly proportional to frequency: doubling the frequency requires doubling the voltage.
  • For example, if 1V produces a pitch of 100 Hz, 2V produces 200 Hz (one octave up), 4V produces 400 Hz (two octaves up), and 8V produces 800 Hz (three octaves up).

Because the voltage doubles with each octave, the intervals between semitones are not uniform in voltage; semitones in higher octaves require much larger voltage steps than semitones in lower octaves. The advantage of Hz/V is that the synthesizer's oscillator does not require a temperature-sensitive exponential converter circuit, often resulting in superior thermal pitch stability. The disadvantage is that covering a wide keyboard range requires exponentially higher voltages.

Practical Impact on MIDI Interfaces

A standard MIDI-to-CV converter reads digital MIDI note numbers (0–127) and converts them into an analog voltage:

  • Configured for 1V/Oct: The DAC outputs steady 0.0833V increments per MIDI semitone.
  • Configured for Hz/V: The DAC calculates an exponential voltage curve, outputting doubled voltages for every 12 MIDI notes.

If you send a 1V/Octave signal into a Hz/V synthesizer, the pitch will compress severely in higher registers, causing the synth to play flat and wildly out of tune. Conversely, sending a Hz/V signal into a 1V/Octave synthesizer will result in pitch intervals that expand exponentially, making standard musical scales unplayable. Selecting the correct output mode on your MIDI interface ensures that the control voltage properly matches the oscillator's internal circuitry for accurate musical tracking.
