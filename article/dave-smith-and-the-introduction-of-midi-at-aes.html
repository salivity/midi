Dave Smith and the Introduction of MIDI at AES

This article explores the foundational role Dave Smith played in introducing the concept of MIDI to the Audio Engineering Society (AES) in 1981. It details the presentation of the Universal Synthesizer Interface (USI), the industry challenges it addressed, and how Smith's public initiative galvanized competing electronic instrument manufacturers to collaborate on a universal standard that transformed modern music production.

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the electronic music industry faced a major interoperability crisis. Synthesizers built by different manufacturers relied on proprietary control voltage (CV) and gate standards, making it nearly impossible for musicians to connect and control multiple instruments together. Dave Smith, the founder of Sequential Circuits, recognized that a universal digital communication standard was essential for the industry to grow.

Smith took the initiative to propose an open, non-proprietary solution to the global audio engineering community. At the 70th Audio Engineering Society Convention held in New York City in October 1981, Smith and his colleague Chet Wood presented a technical paper titled "The Universal Synthesizer Interface" (USI). This paper marked the first public, formal proposal for a standardized digital protocol designed specifically to connect electronic musical instruments.

The USI proposal outlined a basic serial communication protocol that was inexpensive to implement, ensuring adoption would not significantly increase the cost of consumer instruments. Smith envisioned a system capable of transmitting pitch, note-on and note-off commands, and program changes over standard cables, eliminating the tangle of incompatible analog wiring that burdened musicians.

While the AES audience initially met the presentation with mixed reactions—ranging from skepticism to quiet interest—the paper served as the essential catalyst for industry-wide action. Recognizing that a standard could only succeed with unanimous support, Smith immediately pursued collaboration with international manufacturers.

Following the AES presentation, Smith met with key industry leaders, most notably Ikutaro Kakehashi, the founder of Roland, along with representatives from Yamaha, Korg, and Kawai. Together, they refined the initial USI specifications. They increased the transmission speed, switched to an optically isolated 5-pin DIN connector to prevent ground loops, and rebranded the standard as the Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI).

Dave Smith’s bold move to present the concept publicly at AES broke down commercial rivalries and forced the industry to modernize. By 1983, MIDI 1.0 was officially released to the public, fundamentally revolutionizing the way music was performed, recorded, and produced worldwide.
