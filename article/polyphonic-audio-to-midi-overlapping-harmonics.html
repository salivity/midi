Polyphonic Audio to MIDI: Overlapping Harmonics

Polyphonic audio-to-MIDI conversion remains one of the most complex challenges in digital signal processing and music information retrieval. While single-note (monophonic) detection is relatively straightforward, transcribing multiple simultaneous notes is confounded by acoustic interference. Algorithms frequently misidentify pitches, drop notes, or generate phantom triggers because simultaneous sound waves blend inextricably in the frequency domain. This article examines the core mathematical, physical, and algorithmic reasons why overlapping harmonics and sympathetic resonances evade accurate transcription.

The Harmonic Overlap and Energy Attribution Problem

Every musical note produced by an acoustic instrument consists of a fundamental frequency (\(f_0\)) and a series of integer-multiple overtones known as harmonics. When multiple notes are played simultaneously, their harmonic series inevitably intersect.

For example, when a musician plays a musical fifth, such as C3 (approx. 131 Hz) and G3 (approx. 196 Hz), the third harmonic of C3 (393 Hz) aligns almost exactly with the second harmonic of G3 (392 Hz). In a standard spectral analysis, such as a Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), these distinct overtones merge into a single energy peak.

Because audio signals undergo linear superposition, the algorithm cannot easily determine how much energy at a shared frequency belongs to the first note and how much belongs to the second. If the algorithm allocates too much energy to an overlapping harmonic, it may identify a "phantom fundamental"—inferring the presence of an octave or overtone note that was never played. Conversely, misallocating energy downward can cause the algorithm to miss lower-amplitude notes entirely.

The Impact of Sympathetic Resonance

Sympathetic resonance is a physical acoustic phenomenon where an unplayed element (such as an undamped string on a piano or acoustic guitar) begins to vibrate in response to external vibrations matching its natural frequency. When a pianist strikes a C major chord with the sustain pedal down, strings across the entire soundboard vibrate sympathetically with the harmonics of the struck notes.

To a transcription algorithm, sympathetic resonance looks identical to an intentional note event. The resonant strings produce their own fundamental frequencies and harmonic series, albeit at a lower amplitude and with different attack dynamics.

Standard polyphonic transcription algorithms rely heavily on transient onset detection—the initial spike in energy that accompanies a physical pluck, strike, or bow change. Sympathetic resonance lacks a sharp transient, possessing a slow, swell-like envelope. Algorithms struggle to categorize these spectral features: they must guess whether the energy represents a soft, legato note played by the musician or passive physical bleed caused by sympathetic resonance.

Time-Frequency Resolution Trade-Offs

Audio-to-MIDI algorithms are inherently bounded by the Gabor limit (the acoustic equivalent of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle). A digital system cannot achieve high frequency resolution and high time resolution simultaneously:

  • High Frequency Resolution: Necessary to distinguish closely spaced low-frequency notes (e.g., distinguishing low bass intervals). Achieving this requires longer time windows, which blurs the timing of note onsets and offsets.
  • High Time Resolution: Necessary to catch rapid arpeggios, percussive attacks, and precise note durations. Achieving this requires short time windows, which causes spectral leakage and blurs distinct harmonic peaks into wide, indistinct frequency bands.

This trade-off makes it difficult to detect the exact moment an overlapping harmonic changes amplitude, compounding the difficulty of separating overlapping voices in fast polyphonic passages.

Phase Cancellation and Timbral Modulation

In multi-instrument or dense polyphonic environments, overlapping sound waves interact constructively and destructively. Phase cancellation occurs when identical or close frequencies interfere, causing periodic drops in amplitude (known as acoustic beating).

Algorithms that rely on consistent harmonic templates—expecting an instrument to maintain a predictable distribution of energy across its overtones—fail when phase cancellation distorts the overtone profile. This distortion causes spectral modeling algorithms (such as Non-Negative Matrix Factorization) to misclassify or segment continuous notes into multiple fractured MIDI events.

Limitations of Machine Learning Solutions

Modern deep learning architectures (such as Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks and Transformers) have significantly reduced polyphonic transcription errors by learning contextual musical patterns rather than relying purely on spectral heuristics. However, these models still struggle with overlapping harmonics for distinct structural reasons:

  • Training Bias: Neural networks trained on synthesized datasets often fail on real-world recordings where room acoustics, mic placement, and physical resonances deviate from the training distribution.
  • Polyphonic Density: As the number of simultaneous voices increases, the number of harmonic collisions grows exponentially. Even state-of-the-art models exhibit a steep decline in precision when transcribing complex polyphony, as the internal neural representations cannot fully disentangle deeply intertwined spectral matrices.
