Potentiometer Jitter Suppression in MIDI Controllers

When building or programming hardware MIDI controllers, reading physical potentiometers requires converting analog voltages into discrete digital values. Without proper analog-to-digital converter (ADC) jitter suppression, electrical noise and microscopic voltage fluctuations cause unstable readings, triggering continuous streams of phantom MIDI messages. This article explores the root causes of potentiometer signal noise, the technical friction between high-resolution ADCs and standard MIDI protocols, and why implementing noise reduction and hysteresis algorithms is crucial for stable musical performance.

The Physics of Potentiometer Noise

A potentiometer functions as an adjustable voltage divider. When supplied with a reference voltage, turning the shaft shifts an internal wiper across a resistive track, outputting a continuous voltage proportional to its physical position. However, physical circuits are never electrically silent.

Microscopic mechanical vibrations, thermal noise inside the resistive element, contact resistance variations on the wiper, and high-frequency ripple from the microcontroller's power supply all introduce small, fluctuating voltage variations. Even when a knob is left completely untouched, the voltage entering the microcontroller's ADC pin fluctuates by a few millivolts continuously.

The ADC and MIDI Resolution Boundary Problem

Modern microcontrollers typically feature ADCs with resolutions of 10 bits (0–1023) or 12 bits (0–4095). In contrast, standard MIDI Continuous Controller (CC) messages are 7-bit values, spanning a range from 0 to 127.

To convert an ADC reading to a standard MIDI CC value, the firmware scales or bit-shifts the high-resolution input down to 128 discrete steps. While this reduces the overall precision, it creates a severe problem at the transition boundaries between steps. If an untouched potentiometer rests at an analog voltage right on the threshold between two MIDI values—such as 63 and 64—even a 1-bit fluctuation in the ADC output will cause the calculated MIDI value to rapidly oscillate between the two numbers.

Consequences of Unsuppressed Jitter

Failing to suppress this jitter produces significant performance and workflow issues:

  • MIDI Bus Congestion: A single jittering potentiometer can generate dozens or hundreds of redundant MIDI messages every second. On traditional 5-pin DIN MIDI connections operating at a fixed 31,250 baud rate, this data flood quickly consumes available bandwidth, introducing audible latency or dropping critical Note On/Off messages.
  • DAW and Plugin Overhead: Modern digital audio workstations (DAWs) log incoming automation data. A noisy controller will constantly write erratic automation curves, pollute undo histories, and place unnecessary processing loads on virtual instruments responding to real-time parameter changes.
  • Audible Glitches and Parameter Drift: In hardware or software synthesizers, rapidly fluctuating control values can cause audible clicking, zipper noise, or unintended phase and pitch modulation on sensitive targets like filter cutoffs or oscillator tuning.

How Jitter Suppression Solves the Problem

Jitter suppression techniques decouple raw analog fluctuations from final MIDI output generation. The primary strategies include:

  • Hysteresis (Deadbanding): Hysteresis requires a value to cross a defined threshold beyond the previous state before acknowledging a change. Once a MIDI value changes, the analog input must move past a buffer zone before the system accepts another increment or decrement, entirely preventing edge-boundary oscillations.
  • Digital Filtering: Algorithms like Exponential Moving Averages (EMA) or rolling boxcar filters smooth out momentary high-frequency spikes by blending consecutive ADC samples over time.
  • Activity Detection: Firmware can be designed to dynamically engage low-pass filtering when the control is stationary and disengage it during fast physical movement, ensuring responsive performance without sacrificing stability at rest.

By isolating deliberate physical inputs from environmental noise, jitter suppression ensures that MIDI messages are transmitted only when an intentional change occurs, preserving bandwidth and guaranteeing reliable control.
