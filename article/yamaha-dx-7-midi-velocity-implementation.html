Yamaha DX7 MIDI Velocity Implementation

This article explores how the 1983 Yamaha DX7 captured physical key dynamics and transmitted them through the early MIDI 1.0 protocol. It covers the dual-contact mechanical sensing mechanism, the calculation of key velocity, the formatting of standard MIDI Note On packets, and the internal design quirks that caused the DX7's notorious failure to transmit the full MIDI velocity range of 0 to 127.

Physical Keybed and Transit-Time Measurement

The Yamaha DX7 used a proprietary dynamic keybed assembly equipped with dual-contact rubber dome switches beneath each key. Rather than measuring the physical force or downward pressure of a key strike, the system measured velocity by timing the transit of the key between two distinct physical points.

When a performer pressed a key, the downward motion closed a primary electrical contact and, shortly after, a secondary contact. An internal scanning matrix driven by the DX7's central sub-processor (a variant of the Hitachi 6303 microprocessor) continuously polled these contacts. The hardware measured the elapsed time (\(\Delta t\)) between the opening of the upper contact and the closing of the lower contact. A rapid key strike resulted in a small \(\Delta t\), indicating high velocity, while a gentle press yielded a larger \(\Delta t\), indicating low velocity.

Translating Transit Time to Digital Expression

The measured time interval was converted into a digital value via internal lookup tables stored in the synthesizer's firmware ROM. In Frequency Modulation (FM) synthesis, velocity does not merely control volume; it modulates operator output levels. By mapping velocity to operator levels, the DX7 allowed a harder strike to increase the modulation index of modulator operators, resulting in brighter, harmonically rich timbres, or to increase carrier operator levels to raise the volume.

Simultaneously, this calculated velocity value was prepared for transmission over the synthesizer's 5-pin DIN MIDI Out port to interact with external hardware.

Transmission via MIDI 1.0

The DX7 was among the earliest commercial synthesizers to feature MIDI 1.0, which was standardized in 1983. In accordance with the specification, the DX7 encoded velocity within a standard three-byte Note On message:

  1. Status Byte (0x90 to 0x9F): Indicates a Note On command alongside the specific MIDI channel (1–16).
  2. Data Byte 1 (0x00 to 0x7F): Represents the MIDI note number (pitch), ranging from 0 to 127.
  3. Data Byte 2 (0x00 to 0x7F): Represents the velocity value, specifying how hard the key was struck.

When a key was released, the DX7 sent a Note On message with a velocity of zero (0x00), which the MIDI 1.0 standard treats as an efficient alternative to an explicit Note Off command (0x80).

The DX7 Velocity Range Limitation

Despite the MIDI 1.0 specification defining velocity on a scale from 0 to 127, the original generation of the DX7 rarely, if ever, transmitted a value of 127. Under normal playing conditions, the transmitted velocity capped out around 100, occasionally reaching roughly 110 to 118 with exceptionally forceful strikes.

This discrepancy occurred because Yamaha developed the DX7's firmware and hardware scanning routines concurrently with the finalization of the MIDI standard. Yamaha engineers tuned the keybed's velocity curve and the mathematical formulas within the firmware to maximize the dynamic range and nuance of the internal 6-operator FM engine rather than treating the external MIDI specification as a linear controller. Consequently, while the internal engine interpreted the maximum physical strike as full intensity, the corresponding value written to the MIDI output buffer never reached 0x7F (127).

This limitation became a well-known hurdle when using the DX7 as a master MIDI keyboard for external modules, samplers, and synthesizers, which expected incoming velocity to span the full 0–127 range to trigger maximum filter brightness or top-tier sample layers. Later revisions and third-party modifications, such as the Grey Matter Response E! expansion board, eventually updated the firmware to scale the output across the complete 0–127 range.
