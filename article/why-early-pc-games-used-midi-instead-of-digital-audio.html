Why Early PC Games Used MIDI Instead of Digital Audio

During the 1980s and early 1990s, personal computer games predominantly relied on MIDI (Musical Instrument Digital Interface) instructions and sound driver emulation rather than streaming pre-recorded digital audio tracks. This technical choice was driven by severe hardware limitations of the era, specifically regarding storage capacity, system memory, processor overhead, and bus bandwidth. By transmitting compact performance instructions to dedicated synthesizer hardware instead of reading massive waveform files, developers delivered rich, multi-instrumental soundtracks within the extreme resource constraints of contemporary PC systems.

Severe Storage Constraints

The most significant barrier to pre-recorded digital audio was physical storage. In the era preceding the widespread adoption of the CD-ROM, PC games were distributed on 3.5-inch or 5.25-inch floppy disks, which held between 360 kilobytes and 1.44 megabytes of data.

Uncompressed digital audio (PCM) sampled at CD quality (16-bit, 44.1 kHz, stereo) requires roughly 10 megabytes of storage per minute of audio. Even at telephone-quality mono (8-bit, 11 kHz), a minute of sound demands more than 600 kilobytes. In contrast, a MIDI file does not contain actual sound waves; it contains event data such as note pitch, duration, velocity, and instrument selection. An entire game soundtrack composed in MIDI often consumed less than 100 kilobytes, allowing intricate musical arrangements to fit easily onto a single installation disk alongside game code, art, and logic.

Memory and Bandwidth Limits

Early MS-DOS environments operated under severe memory ceilings, most notably the 640-kilobyte conventional memory limit. System RAM was at a premium and needed to hold the game executable, graphics tables, and active game states. Loading seconds of digitized audio into RAM was impractical.

Furthermore, reading digital audio in real-time from early mechanical hard drives or floppy drives saturated the system. The standard Industry Standard Architecture (ISA) bus had limited data throughput, and hard drive transfer speeds were slow. Streaming high-bitrate digitized audio continuously while simultaneously loading graphics, reading user input, and executing game logic introduced heavy input lag, frame-rate stutters, and audio dropouts.

CPU Overhead and the Absence of Compression

Modern streaming relies heavily on lossy digital compression algorithms such as MP3, Ogg Vorbis, and AAC. During the 1980s and early 1990s, these compression formats did not exist for commercial software. When codecs did begin to emerge in the mid-1990s, the host central processing units—such as the Intel 80286, 80386, and 80486—lacked the floating-point performance and clock cycles required to decompress high-fidelity audio streams in real time while concurrently calculating game physics and rendering raster graphics.

MIDI eliminated CPU burden entirely. The processor merely transmitted low-bandwidth serial instructions to the sound hardware, consuming a negligible fraction of clock cycles.

Sound Card Architecture and Hardware Synthesis

Instead of decoding audio via the main processor, the PC sound architecture relied on dedicated synthesis chips located on expansion cards. Game developers wrote their music to target these specific hardware decoders:

  • Frequency Modulation (FM) Synthesis: Cards like the AdLib and early Creative Sound Blaster used Yamaha chips (such as the YM3812/OPL2 and OPL3) to generate artificial instrument timbres through frequency modulation.
  • Wavetable Synthesis and External Modules: Higher-end hardware, such as the Roland MT-32 and Roland Sound Canvas, or wavetable cards like the Sound Blaster AWE32, stored pre-recorded instrument samples in onboard ROM chips.

When a game sent a MIDI note to an OPL chip or a Roland module, the sound card’s dedicated hardware synthesized or played back the tone directly from its own hardware banks. The PC did not stream audio; it merely conducted an autonomous digital synthesizer.

Dynamic Composition Flexibility

MIDI provided an interactive advantage that pre-recorded streaming audio could not easily match with early hardware. Because the music was generated live from data instructions, composers could alter tempos, loop sections indefinitely without audible seams, change instrument voices, or mute specific tracks based on player actions. These dynamic audio transitions required minimal code adjustments and zero buffer latency.

The shift toward recorded digital audio only became viable in the mid-1990s with the standardization of CD-ROM drives (enabling Red Book CD audio playback directly from the disc) and faster 32-bit processors capable of handling compressed digital sound formats. Until those hardware milestones were achieved, MIDI emulation remained the only viable technology for PC game music.
