Hardware Vocoders: How MIDI Controls Carrier Frequencies

A hardware vocoder uses MIDI keyboard input to determine the carrier signal's pitch and harmonic structure, replacing traditional static tones with dynamic, playable musical notes. By converting MIDI data into specific analog or digital oscillator frequencies, the vocoder generates a rich carrier signal that is subsequently shaped by the harmonic characteristics of a human voice (the modulator). This article explains the technical interaction between MIDI inputs, carrier oscillators, filter banks, and speech analysis in hardware vocoders.

The Vocoder Architecture: Modulator vs. Carrier

To understand the role of MIDI, it is necessary to separate the vocoder's two primary audio streams:

  • The Modulator: The dynamic audio input, typically human speech captured via a microphone. The vocoder analyzes this signal for spectral content and dynamic contours (formants and amplitude).
  • The Carrier: The synthesized sound source that supplies the raw harmonic material to be shaped. The carrier must be harmonically rich (such as sawtooth or pulse waves) so that there are ample frequencies across the audible spectrum for the filters to carve out.

Translating MIDI Input into Carrier Frequencies

A hardware vocoder contains an internal synthesizer or directly controls an external synthesizer via MIDI connections. When a musician presses a key on a MIDI keyboard, the vocoder processes the following data to establish the carrier frequencies:

  1. MIDI Note Numbers: Each key on the keyboard transmits a standard MIDI Note number (0 to 127). The vocoder's internal sound engine converts this digital value into a fundamental frequency (\(f\)) using standard tuning logic (where MIDI Note 69 is typically mapped to A440 Hz): \[f = 440 \times 2^{(n - 69) / 12}\]
  2. Oscillator Generation: The internal tone generator triggers one or more oscillators at the calculated fundamental frequency. If the vocoder is polyphonic, playing a chord triggers multiple carrier frequencies simultaneously, allowing speech to be harmonized.
  3. Continuous Controllers: Pitch bend, modulation wheels, and aftertouch alter the oscillator frequencies in real time, enabling vocal glides, vibrato, and microtonal inflections.

Speech Analysis and the Filter Bank

Once the MIDI input determines the frequencies generated by the synthesizer, the vocoder merges the carrier with the human voice through paired bandpass filter banks:

  1. Modulator Analysis: The human speech input is split across a series of bandpass filters tuned to consecutive frequency ranges. Each band features an envelope follower that measures the continuous amplitude (volume) of the voice within that specific slice of the spectrum.
  2. Carrier Synthesis: The MIDI-generated carrier signal is routed into an identical, parallel bank of bandpass filters.
  3. VCA Modulation: Voltage-controlled amplifiers (VCAs) on the carrier side are driven directly by the control voltages generated by the modulator's envelope followers.

The Resulting Sound

The carrier frequencies determined by the MIDI keyboard do not alter the analysis of the human speech. Instead, the MIDI input defines what notes are available to be heard, while the human voice defines which frequency bands open up. If a MIDI note produces harmonics that fall within the active frequency bands of the speaker's vocal formants, those frequencies pass through, creating the synthesized, singing-robot effect characterized by the vocoder.
