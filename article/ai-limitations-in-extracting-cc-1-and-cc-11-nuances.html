AI Limitations in Extracting CC 1 and CC 11 Nuances

While modern artificial intelligence has made substantial progress in Automatic Music Transcription (AMT) for pitch, onset, and velocity, it struggles significantly with continuous control performance data. Continuous Controllers such as MIDI CC 1 (Modulation, typically controlling dynamic crossfades and timbral intensity) and CC 11 (Expression, typically controlling volume shaping) represent fluid, subjective performance gestures rather than discrete events. Current AI models face fundamental bottlenecks in separating, quantifying, and accurately mapping these continuous expressive nuances from raw audio due to acoustic ambiguity, data scarcity, and architectural constraints.

The Ambiguity Between Amplitude and Timbre

In virtual orchestration and sample-based composition, CC 1 and CC 11 serve distinct but interdependent functions. CC 1 generally alters the harmonic profile by crossfading through distinct dynamic layers (such as moving from pianissimo to fortissimo to introduce higher harmonic overtones), while CC 11 acts as a secondary volume fader to attenuate or boost overall loudness.

When analyzing an audio waveform, an AI model cannot easily reverse-engineer this distinction:

  • Overlapping Spectral Profiles: A natural swell in an instrument's sound involves simultaneous changes in overall amplitude and harmonic saturation. The model cannot deterministically decipher whether a decrease in loudness was driven by an expression drop (CC 11), a softer playing technique (CC 1), or physical factors like microphone distance and room acoustics.
  • The Inverse Problem: Audio transcription is an ill-posed inverse problem. Multiple combinations of CC 1, CC 11, master fader levels, and equalizations can yield an identical acoustic output, leaving the model without sufficient mathematical constraints to isolate individual CC parameters.

Severe Lack of Aligned Training Data

Supervised deep learning requires vast amounts of precisely paired input (audio) and target labels (continuous control curves). For CC data, suitable datasets practically do not exist at scale:

  • The Acoustic vs. MIDI Disconnect: The gold standards in music transcription datasets (such as the MAESTRO dataset) focus primarily on the piano, an instrument governed by note-on velocities and sustain pedals (CC 64), not continuous expressive streams like CC 1 and CC 11.
  • Synthetic Limitations: Generating synthetic datasets using virtual instruments (VSTs) provides exact MIDI CC data, but the resulting audio lacks the micro-acoustic variations, natural room reflections, and dynamic phase relationships of real orchestral performances. Models trained on synthetic data generalize poorly to real-world acoustic recordings.
  • Human Performance Capture: Capturing human acoustic performances alongside sample-accurate MIDI CC tracking is physically impossible with traditional acoustic instruments, as continuous expression on a violin or flute is controlled via bow pressure, bow speed, or breath, none of which natively output standard MIDI data.

Polyphonic Masking and Source Separation Limits

Extracting individual CC curves from solo monophonic recordings is challenging, but doing so from polyphonic or multi-instrument mixes is currently beyond state-of-the-art architectures:

  • Spectral Bleed: In an ensemble, individual instruments share overlapping frequency bands. If a violin section and a horn section swell simultaneously, modern source separation models (like Demucs or Open-Unmix) introduce phase distortion and spectral leakage when isolating them, corrupting the delicate volume and timbre cues required to compute CC 1 and CC 11 accurately.
  • Context Blindness: Models cannot reliably tell whether a sudden drop in an instrument's presence is an intentional CC 11 fade by the performer or destructive phase interference caused by another instrument in the mix.

Resolution Trade-Offs in Neural Architectures

Standard audio processing architectures rely heavily on time-frequency representations like the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) or Mel-spectrograms. These representations inherently suffer from the Gabor limit (the trade-off between time resolution and frequency resolution).

  • Temporal Blurring: High frequency resolution is necessary to measure the subtle harmonic growth governed by CC 1. However, achieving high frequency resolution requires larger analysis windows, which blur fast expressive movements, dynamic vibrato, or rapid CC 11 swells.
  • Waveform-Level Inefficiencies: End-to-end raw waveform models (such as 1D CNNs or continuous-time diffusion models) avoid spectrogram trade-offs, but they struggle to track long-range expressive dependencies over several bars of music without requiring prohibitive computational power.

Because of these limitations, current AI models can approximate gross dynamic envelopes, but they remain unable to reliably decode the nuanced interplay of CC 1 and CC 11 embedded within complex audio.
