How MIDI CC 67 Soft Pedal Alters Piano Sound

MIDI Continuous Controller 67 (CC 67) controls the soft pedal—historically known as the una corda pedal—in digital and virtual acoustic pianos. When engaged, this message does not merely turn down the volume of an instrument; it commands the sound engine to reproduce the complex physical, tonal, and harmonic transformations that occur inside an acoustic piano when the soft pedal mechanism is physically depressed. Modern sampled and physically modeled pianos interpret CC 67 values to attenuate high frequencies, soften the attack transient, alter sympathetic resonance, and shift the overall dynamic response.

The Acoustic Mechanism Simulated by CC 67

To understand what CC 67 accomplishes digitally, it is essential to observe the physical behavior of acoustic pianos:

  • Grand Pianos (Una Corda): Depressing the pedal shifts the entire mechanical action (keys, levers, and hammers) slightly to the right. As a result, the hammers strike only two of the three strings in the middle and upper registers (or one string instead of two in the bichord range). Furthermore, the strings are struck by a softer, less compacted portion of the hammer felt that does not align with the standard string grooves.
  • Upright Pianos (Half-Blow): The pedal raises the hammer rail, moving the hammers closer to the strings before they are struck. This reduces the hammer's travel distance and kinetic energy, resulting primarily in a decrease in volume and slight softening of attack.

Advanced virtual instruments use CC 67 primarily to emulate the grand piano’s una corda mechanism, which causes far more dramatic tonal evolution than an upright's half-blow mechanism.

Sonic Alterations Generated by CC 67

When a MIDI instrument receives a CC 67 value (traditionally a switch where 0–63 is off and 64–127 is on, though modern instruments support continuous variable values), it introduces several distinct acoustic phenomena:

1. Attenuation of High-Frequency Harmonics

Because the hammer strikes the strings using softer, uncompressed felt, the collision transfers energy more slowly. This dampens the generation of high-order overtones. The resulting sound is fundamentally darker, rounder, and less metallic, stripping away the harshness of fortissimo notes and giving pianissimo passages a veiled, warm quality.

2. Modified Attack Transient

The initial "knock" or percussive transient of the hammer impacting the string is substantially weakened. In standard playing, compacted felt creates a sharp, immediate impulse. The soft pedal causes a slower mechanical compression on impact, lengthening the attack envelope and smoothing the initial strike sound.

3. Shift in Sympathetic Resonance

On a grand piano, the string left untouched by the shifted hammer is not muted by a damper; it remains open to oscillate sympathetically with the struck strings and the soundboard. Digital engines emulate this by introducing distinct sympathetic resonance algorithms or routing. The unstruck string vibrates slightly out of phase with the struck strings, creating subtle phase interactions, longer sustains in certain frequencies, and a wider, more ethereal spatial image.

4. Dynamic Mapping Modification

CC 67 alters the instrument's velocity curve. The same incoming MIDI Note-On velocity produces a lower amplitude and a gentler timbre than it would with the pedal disengaged. This allows the performer finer control over micro-dynamics at the lower end of the dynamic range.

Digital Implementation: Sampling vs. Physical Modeling

Virtual instruments interpret CC 67 through two primary sound-generation paradigms:

  • Dedicated Sample Switching: High-end sample libraries (such as those running in Kontakt, UVI, or proprietary engines) record a completely distinct set of sample layers specifically with the una corda pedal depressed. When CC 67 is received, the engine remaps the incoming MIDI notes to these dedicated sample pools, capturing the real-world acoustic response directly.
  • Physical Modeling and Real-Time DSP: Modeled instruments (such as Modartt Pianoteq) physically simulate the strike position and hammer hardness parameters in real time. CC 67 dynamically shifts the virtual strike point relative to string spacing, decreases virtual hammer felt stiffness, and reconfigures the inter-string resonance matrix. Continuous CC 67 input in these systems enables seamless "half-pedaling," smoothly interpolating between the standard strike and the full una corda position.
