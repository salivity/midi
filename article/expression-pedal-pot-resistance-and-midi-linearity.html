Expression Pedal Pot Resistance and MIDI Linearity

An expression pedal operates as a variable voltage divider that an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) inside a MIDI controller reads to generate continuous control data. In a theoretically ideal circuit, potentiometer resistance values like 10kΩ or 50kΩ do not change the linearity of translated MIDI values, because the voltage ratio remains identical. In real-world hardware, however, the interaction between the potentiometer’s total resistance and the receiving circuit’s input impedance introduces loading effects that can significantly warp a linear sweep into an uneven, non-linear MIDI response.

The Voltage Divider Principle

Expression pedals work by applying a reference voltage (typically +3.3V or +5V) across the outer lugs of a potentiometer while the wiper terminal reads the position. In an unloaded system, the output voltage is governed strictly by the ratio of the wiper’s position along the resistive track:

\[\text{V}_{\text{out}} = \text{V}_{\text{ref}} \times \left(\frac{\text{R}_{\text{wiper}}}{\text{R}_{\text{total}}}\right)\]

Assuming the potentiometer has a linear taper (type "B"), the output voltage increases proportionally to foot movement. The host controller’s ADC samples this voltage and digitizes it into standard MIDI values (typically 0 to 127 for 7-bit Control Change messages, or 0 to 16,383 for 14-bit high-resolution MIDI). Because this is a relative voltage ratio, a 10kΩ pot and a 50kΩ pot output identical theoretical voltages at 50% travel.

Input Impedance and the Loading Effect

The deviation from linearity occurs because the receiving MIDI device does not possess infinite input impedance. The sensing input pin of the microcontroller or ADC circuit places its own internal input impedance (\(R_{in}\)) in parallel with the potentiometer’s lower half (\(R_{lower}\)).

The equivalent resistance seen at the wiper is:

\[\text{R}_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{\text{R}_{\text{lower}} \times \text{R}_{\text{in}}}{\text{R}_{\text{lower}} + \text{R}_{\text{in}}}\]

When \(R_{in}\) is significantly larger than the total resistance of the pot (typically 10 to 100 times higher), the loading effect is negligible. However, many MIDI controllers, audio interfaces, and digital modelers feature input impedances ranging between 10kΩ and 100kΩ.

  • 10kΩ Potentiometer: Because 10kΩ is relatively low, its source impedance remains small throughout the sweep. Even when connected to a moderate input impedance (such as 50kΩ), the voltage sag at the midpoint is minimal, keeping the translated MIDI data linear.
  • 50kΩ Potentiometer: When a 50kΩ pot is set to 50% travel, the source impedance at the wiper peaks at 12.5kΩ (the two 25kΩ halves in parallel). If connected to an input with a 50kΩ or lower impedance, this creates a pronounced voltage sag. Instead of reading 50% of the reference voltage at the midpoint, the ADC reads a lower value, causing the MIDI curve to bunch up near the toe-down position.

ADC Sampling Capacitance and Slew Rate

Microcontroller ADCs sample voltage using an internal sample-and-hold capacitor. Charging this capacitor requires a stable current source:

  • A 10kΩ pot provides lower output impedance, allowing the ADC sampling capacitor to charge rapidly within the acquisition time window. This results in stable, instantaneous, and accurate numerical conversions.
  • A 50kΩ pot has a higher output impedance that slows the charging rate of the internal ADC capacitor. If the microcontroller samples too rapidly, the capacitor may not reach full voltage before the measurement is locked, producing artificial dead zones, jitter, and non-linear transitions between MIDI values.

Practical Impact on MIDI Performance

When a 50kΩ pedal is used with hardware optimized for 10kΩ inputs:

  1. Value Clustering: MIDI values advance slowly throughout the heel-to-mid range of travel, then accelerate rapidly toward value 127 in the final quarter of pedal throw.
  2. Reduced Usable Range: If pull-up or pull-down protection resistors are present on the controller's jack, a 50kΩ resistance can prevent the signal from reaching true 0V or full reference voltage, clipping the minimum or maximum MIDI output.
  3. Noise and Jitter: Higher impedance circuits are more susceptible to radio-frequency and electromagnetic interference along the expression cable, which manifests as fluctuating MIDI CC values ("MIDI jitter") that can degrade performance stability unless heavily filtered via software.
