Debugging MIDI Baud Rate Skew and Framing Errors

Troubleshooting non-functioning MIDI hardware often requires verifying serial communication at the physical and protocol layers. When a receiver fails to process incoming commands, clock discrepancies and rise-time degradation are primary suspects. This guide explains how an engineer uses a digital logic analyzer to isolate the root causes of framing errors and baud rate skew in 31.25 kbps MIDI circuits, from physical probing to timing measurement and protocol decoding.

Setting Up Probes and Signal Acquisition

MIDI uses an asynchronous serial protocol (8-N-1) running at a standard rate of 31,250 baud (32 µs per bit). To isolate whether an issue originates at the transmitter, the isolation interface, or the receiver:

  1. Attach the logic analyzer’s ground clip directly to the circuit under test's signal ground.
  2. Place Channel 0 on the microcontroller's UART TX line before the output driver.
  3. Place Channel 1 at the output of the receiving circuit’s optocoupler (the UART RX pin of the target receiver).
  4. Set the logic analyzer’s sample rate to at least 24 MHz (preferably 50 MHz or higher) to achieve sub-microsecond timing resolution capable of measuring narrow pulse-width variations.

Measuring Baud Rate Skew

Baud rate skew occurs when the transmitter or receiver clock drifts away from the nominal 31,250 Hz. To verify clock precision:

  1. Capture a transmission burst containing standard MIDI traffic.
  2. Locate the narrowest high or low pulse in the transmission, which represents a single bit. Nominal bit width must be exactly 32.0 microseconds.
  3. Place timing markers at the transition edges of a single bit to calculate the instantaneous baud rate (\(1 / \Delta t\)).
  4. For greater accuracy, place markers across an entire 10-bit frame (1 Start bit + 8 Data bits + 1 Stop bit) from the falling edge of the Start bit to the rising edge of the Stop bit. Nominal total frame duration is 320 µs.
  5. Calculate the percentage deviation: \[\text{Skew \%} = \frac{\Delta t_{\text{measured}} - 320\,\mu\text{s}}{320\,\mu\text{s}} \times 100\] If the skew exceeds \(\pm 1\%\) to \(\pm 1.5\%\), low-cost internal RC oscillators or inaccurate clock prescalers on the transmitting microcontroller are typically the culprit.

Inspecting Framing Errors

A framing error occurs when the receiving UART samples a logic LOW where it expects a logic HIGH Stop bit.

  1. Enable the analyzer's built-in Async Serial / UART decoder. Configure the settings to:
    • Bit Rate: 31,250 bps
    • Data Bits: 8
    • Stop Bits: 1
    • Parity: None
    • Bit Order: LSB First
  2. Look for decoded frames flagged with red warning indicators or explicit "Framing Error" markers.
  3. Zoom in on the flagged byte and observe the Stop bit boundary (320 µs after the initial falling edge of the Start bit).
  4. Determine the failure mode:
    • Accumulated Clock Drift: If the baud rate is running too fast or too slow, the receiver samples each successive bit slightly earlier or later. By the 10th bit (the Stop bit), the sample point shifts into the adjacent bit boundary, reading a LOW instead of a HIGH.
    • Asymmetric Slew and Optocoupler Delays: Compare Channel 0 (pre-driver) to Channel 1 (post-optocoupler). Slow turn-off times in sluggish optocouplers stretch LOW pulses and truncate HIGH pulses. If the optocoupler takes several microseconds to recover, the logic level will fail to cross the high threshold in time for the Stop bit sample window.

Isolating and Resolving Hardware Faults

Once the timing error is confirmed:

  • If the TX pin shows skew: Recalibrate the MCU clock, adjust UART baud divider registers to minimize integer division error, or switch from an internal RC oscillator to an external quartz crystal.
  • If TX is accurate but the optocoupler output is distorted: The receiver's optocoupler is either too slow for 31.25 kbps data or the pull-up resistor value on its open-collector output is too large. Reduce the pull-up resistor value (e.g., from 4.7 kΩ to 1 kΩ–2.2 kΩ) to sharpen rise times, or upgrade to a high-speed optocoupler designed for MIDI applications, such as a 6N138 or H11L1.
