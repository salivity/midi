How Analog Glide Works in MIDI to CV Converters

This article explains how MIDI pitch-to-voltage (CV) converters generate smooth pitch transitions—known as glide or portamento—using analog circuitry. While MIDI data consists of discrete digital note events, an analog glide circuit conditions the resulting direct-current output into a continuously sweeping control voltage. By employing post-DAC analog slew-rate limiters, voltage-controlled resistors, and active switching topologies, converters produce natural-sounding pitch slides without stepping artifacts.

The DAC Output and Step Transitions

In a MIDI-to-CV converter, incoming MIDI Note On messages are processed by a microcontroller, which determines the target pitch and commands a Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC) to output a corresponding steady direct-current voltage (typically adhering to the 1V/Octave or Hz/V standard). When portamento is handled purely in the analog domain, the microcontroller does not interpolate intermediate values. Instead, it updates the DAC output instantaneously, creating an abrupt voltage step between the old note and the new note. The task of smoothing this abrupt jump into a continuous transition is handed off entirely to the analog output stage.

The Slew Limiter Circuit

The core component responsible for analog portamento is a slew limiter (also called a lag generator or integrator). At its simplest, a slew limiter consists of a passive low-pass RC (resistor-capacitor) filter placed between the DAC output buffer and the final CV output driver.

When the DAC output changes instantly, the capacitor cannot charge or discharge instantaneously; it responds at a rate dictated by the circuit's time constant (\(\tau = R \times C\)). This slows the voltage transition, transforming a square voltage step into a continuous transition curve. A simple RC network produces an exponential glide curve, meaning the pitch changes rapidly at first and slows down as it approaches the destination note.

For linear glide—where the pitch changes at a constant rate regardless of the musical interval—an active integrator circuit built around an operational amplifier is used. In this configuration, the capacitor is charged via constant-current sources instead of simple resistors, forcing the output voltage to ramp at a steady linear rate (\(dV/dt = I/C\)).

Dynamic Control of Glide Time

To make glide musically useful, the glide rate must be adjustable via physical controls or MIDI messages (such as MIDI CC 5, Portamento Time). In the analog domain, this requires varying the resistance or charging current of the slew circuit electronically:

  • Operational Transconductance Amplifiers (OTAs): Converters often use an OTA (such as the LM13700) configured as a voltage-controlled resistor or transconductance stage feeding an integrating capacitor. The microcontroller outputs an analog control current (via a secondary DAC channel or filtered PWM) proportional to the desired MIDI glide time, regulating the current delivered to the capacitor and dynamically speeding up or slowing down the slew rate.
  • Digital Potentiometers: Some designs employ digitally controlled analog potentiometers (digipots) within the RC path, allowing the digital subsystem to alter the physical resistance directly via an I2C or SPI interface.
  • Vactrols and Light-Dependent Resistors: Earlier or vintage-style designs route the signal through a photocell illuminated by an LED, where the LED's brightness controls the resistance and therefore the glide duration.

Switching and Legato Implementation

Analog glide circuits require switching mechanisms to engage or bypass the slew behavior, typically controlled by MIDI CC 65 (Portamento On/Off) or firmware-level legato detection.

Analog switches, such as CMOS transmission gates (e.g., CD4066) or JFET switches, are placed across the timing resistance or between the dry DAC signal and the slewed path. When glide is disabled or when a non-legato note is struck, the switch closes to bypass the timing capacitor entirely, delivering the DAC's instantaneous step response to the output. When glide is triggered, the switch opens, routing the signal through the slew-limiting network to generate the smooth pitch transition.
