How MIDI Traffic Causes Audible Note Flamming

Audible note flamming occurs when excessive MIDI data saturates the standard 31.25 kbaud serial connection, forcing simultaneous note commands into a sequential queue that stretches over tens of milliseconds. Because the legacy MIDI 1.0 protocol transmits data serially at a fixed bit rate, dense bursts of controller messages, pitch bend, or polyphonic chords introduce serialization latency and jitter. This delay causes notes that were triggered at the exact same instant to reach sound modules at staggered intervals, transforming a tight, punchy transient into a loose, smeared "flam" effect.

The 31.25 kbaud Hardware Limitation

Standard 5-pin DIN MIDI operates at an asynchronous transmission rate of 31,250 baud (bits per second). Using standard serial framing, each MIDI byte consists of 10 bits: one start bit, eight data bits, and one stop bit. Consequently, a MIDI line can transmit a maximum of 3,125 bytes per second. At this fixed speed, sending a single byte takes exactly 320 microseconds (0.32 milliseconds).

A standard MIDI Note-On message requires three bytes:

  1. Status byte (Note-On and channel)
  2. Data byte 1 (Pitch/Note number)
  3. Data byte 2 (Velocity)

Transmitting one complete Note-On command requires 0.96 milliseconds. Even using "running status"—an optimization that omits the status byte for consecutive messages of the same type—each subsequent note still requires two bytes, or 0.64 milliseconds.

Serial Serialization and Chords

MIDI is strictly a serial communication channel, meaning it cannot transmit multiple messages in parallel. When a musician plays a dense ten-note chord or a sequencer triggers multiple layered tracks simultaneously, the messages must line up in a single-file queue.

Under ideal conditions with running status applied, a ten-note chord takes roughly 6.72 milliseconds to transmit. While 6 to 7 milliseconds is generally considered acceptable and often imperceptible to the human ear, this baseline latency rapidly degrades when non-note data enters the stream.

The Impact of Excessive Data Traffic

Modern digital audio workstations (DAWs), master keyboards, and controllers frequently generate continuous streams of non-note MIDI events. High-density data includes:

  • Continuous Controllers (CC): High-resolution automation curves for expression (CC11), modulation (CC1), filter cutoff (CC74), and volume (CC7).
  • Channel and Polyphonic Aftertouch: Constant pressure updates sent per key or per channel.
  • Pitch Bend: High-frequency, 14-bit data consisting of two data bytes per update.
  • MIDI Clock and Active Sensing: Real-time synchronization bytes interleaved directly into the data stream.

Each standard CC or Pitch Bend update consumes two to three bytes. When a sequencer sweeps a filter while simultaneously modulating volume and playing chords, the interface can easily output hundreds of bytes within a fraction of a second. If 50 continuous controller bytes are queued right before a group of notes, those CC messages will block the serial line for 16 milliseconds before the first note even begins transmitting.

Why Flamming Occurs

Flamming is an acoustic phenomenon borrowed from percussion terminology, where two drum hits meant to sound together land with a slight gap, producing a "flam" sound rather than a single transient attack.

The human auditory system is exceptionally sensitive to transient timing. While delays below 5 milliseconds are heard as phase shifts or unified hits, delays between 10 and 30 milliseconds are perceived as distinct, smeared attacks.

When heavy data traffic forces Note-On messages to space out across 15 to 40 milliseconds:

  1. Transient Smearing: The attack phases of layered instruments (such as a kick drum and a bass synth triggered on beat one) no longer align, stripping the mix of its impact.
  2. Asymmetrical Arrival Times: The first note in the serial queue sounds immediately, while the final note in the queue plays noticeably late.
  3. Phase Cancellation and Inconsistency: Because the volume of CC data fluctuates dynamically, the delay between notes changes from beat to beat. This timing variation (jitter) makes the flamming erratic and distracting during playback.

Preventing Serial Bottlenecks

To eliminate traffic-induced flamming on hardware MIDI setups, traffic must be actively managed:

  • Thinning MIDI CC Data: Most DAWs include a MIDI event filter or thinning function to reduce the density of continuous controller events without altering the audible curve.
  • Filtering Redundant Messages: Disabling unneeded data such as MIDI Time Code (MTC), Active Sensing, or polyphonic aftertouch preserves bandwidth.
  • Multiport Interfaces: Distributing complex arrangements across multiple physical MIDI Out ports bypasses the single-cable 31.25 kbaud limit by providing independent serial buses for different hardware synthesizers.
