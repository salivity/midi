Guitar to MIDI Latency on Low Strings Explained

Hardware guitar-to-MIDI converters suffer from noticeable latency when tracking bass and lower-register guitar strings. This article examines the primary cause of this delay: the fundamental physics of sound wave periods, which dictates that pitch-detection algorithms must wait longer to analyze low-frequency waveforms before converting them into digital MIDI notes.

The Physics of the Fundamental Period

The primary cause of latency on low-frequency vibrating strings is the mathematical relationship between frequency and time, expressed as \(T = 1 / f\), where \(T\) is the period in seconds and \(f\) is the frequency in Hertz (Hz).

Lower frequencies have longer wave cycles:

  • A standard guitar's high E string vibrates at approximately 329.6 Hz, yielding a single cycle period of roughly 3.03 milliseconds.
  • The low E string vibrates at approximately 82.4 Hz, yielding a single cycle period of roughly 12.13 milliseconds.
  • A standard bass low E string vibrates at 41.2 Hz, extending the period to 24.27 milliseconds per cycle.

Algorithm Detection Requirements

Hardware pitch-to-MIDI converters use pitch-detection methods such as autocorrelation, zero-crossing detection, or fast Fourier transforms (FFT). To accurately determine pitch and prevent false triggering, these algorithms cannot identify a note from a single instant of sound; they must observe at least one to two complete cycles of the waveform.

Because low strings produce cycles lasting over 12 milliseconds each, sampling 1.5 to 2 full cycles requires an unavoidable buffer of 18 to 24 milliseconds just to collect the necessary audio data. In contrast, the high E string requires only 4.5 to 6 milliseconds of audio data to complete the same analysis.

Harmonic Transients and Settling Time

Compounding the long cycle period is the complexity of a plucked string's initial transient. The moment a pick strikes a low string, the sound is dominated by inharmonic noise and high-amplitude upper harmonics rather than a clean fundamental sine wave.

Converters must wait for these chaotic pick transients to decay and the fundamental frequency to stabilize. If a converter triggers too quickly during this initial phase, it risks outputting an octave-up error or an erratic pitch. Waiting for the fundamental wave to stabilize adds additional cycles to the analysis window, increasing real-world tracking latency on low strings to 25–40 milliseconds before a MIDI Note-On message can be generated.
