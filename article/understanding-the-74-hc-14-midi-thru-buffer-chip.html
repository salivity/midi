Understanding the 74HC14 MIDI Thru Buffer Chip

A MIDI Thru hardware buffer chip, such as the 74HC14 hex Schmitt-trigger inverter, restores, cleans, and strengthens incoming MIDI data signals before relaying them to subsequent devices. This article explains the technical function of the 74HC14 buffer within MIDI circuitry, detailing how it prevents signal degradation, drives the current-loop interface, and ensures reliable communication across daisy-chained musical instruments.

The Purpose of a MIDI Thru Port

The MIDI Thru port is designed to create an exact duplicate of the data arriving at a device's MIDI In port. This allows musicians to daisy-chain multiple synthesizers, drum machines, and samplers using a single MIDI output source. Because the signal must pass through an optical isolator (optocoupler) at the MIDI In stage, the original electrical waveform becomes distorted. A dedicated hardware buffer is required to regenerate the signal so downstream equipment receives valid, readable data.

Signal Regeneration via Schmitt-Trigger Hysteresis

The optocoupler at the input stage introduces rise and fall time delays due to junction capacitance and parasitic circuit properties. Over cable runs and across multiple daisy-chained devices, these slow transitions round off the square-wave digital signal, resulting in timing jitter and dropped bytes.

The 74HC14 contains six inverting Schmitt-trigger gates. Unlike standard logic gates that switch at a fixed threshold voltage, a Schmitt trigger uses hysteresis, meaning it has separate switching thresholds for rising and falling edges:

  • Upper Threshold (\(V_{T+}\)): The input must rise above this voltage to be recognized as a logic HIGH.
  • Lower Threshold (\(V_{T-}\)): The input must fall below this voltage to be recognized as a logic LOW.

This hysteresis filters out electrical noise and instantly snaps slow, degraded transitions back into sharp, steep square waves, restoring the precise timing needed for the MIDI standard's 31.25 kbaud data rate.

Current-Loop Driving Capability

MIDI operates as a 5-volt, 5-milliampere current loop. The phototransistor inside an input optocoupler lacks the current capacity to drive a cable and trigger another optocoupler on a downstream device.

The 74HC14 solves this by acting as a low-impedance line driver. Each gate on a 74HC-family chip can sink or source adequate current to safely drive the standard 5mA loop through the required 220-ohm current-limiting resistors connected to pins 4 and 5 of the 5-pin DIN output connector.

Logic Inversion and Circuit Topology

The 74HC14 is an inverting gate, meaning a logic HIGH at the input yields a logic LOW at the output. Because the MIDI signal must maintain its original polarity, two inverters are typically placed in series:

  1. First Inverter: Cleans the signal from the optocoupler and inverts the logic.
  2. Second Inverter: Re-inverts the signal back to the original polarity, sharpens the edges a second time, and directly drives the MIDI Thru jack.

Unused gates on the hex chip are often paralleled to distribute current or utilized for the standard MIDI Out port of the device.

Electrical Isolation and Protection

A dedicated buffer creates an electrical safety barrier between external cabling and the host device's internal components. If a bad cable or a faulty connected instrument shorts the MIDI Thru lines to ground or high voltage, the inexpensive 74HC14 chip absorbs the damage, preventing catastrophic failure of the device’s main microcontroller.
