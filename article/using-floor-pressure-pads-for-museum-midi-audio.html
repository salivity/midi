Using Floor Pressure Pads for Museum MIDI Audio

Interactive museum exhibits often use floor pressure pads to transform visitor movement into dynamic soundscapes. By stepping on specific floor zones, visitors mechanically compress sensors that convert physical force into electrical signals. A microcontroller or hardware encoder translates these electrical changes into standard Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) messages, which are then transmitted to a central computer running audio software to trigger or modulate sound in real time.

The Sensor: Detecting Physical Pressure

The interaction begins at the floor level using specialized pressure-sensing technology. Installations typically deploy one of three types of sensors hidden beneath carpets, rubber mats, or raised flooring:

  • Momentary Contact Switches: Simple on/off mats that close a circuit when a visitor's weight exceeds a specific threshold. These function like oversized keyboard keys.
  • Force-Sensitive Resistors (FSRs): Sensors whose electrical resistance decreases as more force is applied. These allow the system to detect not just the presence of a visitor, but also their weight, impact speed, and pressure changes.
  • Piezoelectric Sensors: Transducers that generate a voltage spike upon sudden physical impact, making them ideal for detecting transient footsteps or tapping rhythms.

Signal Processing and MIDI Encoding

Raw electrical data from the floor pads cannot directly communicate with audio software. A hardware intermediary—typically a microcontroller like an Arduino, Teensy, or a dedicated commercial MIDI encoder—processes the signals.

  1. Analog-to-Digital Conversion: When an FSR or piezo sensor receives pressure, the microcontroller reads the varying voltage through an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC).
  2. Debouncing and Calibration: Software running on the microcontroller filters out electrical noise, vibration ripple, and erratic signals (a process known as debouncing) to prevent accidental double-triggers.
  3. MIDI Mapping: The microcontroller formats the clean data into standardized MIDI packets:
    • Note On / Note Off: Stepping on a switch triggers a Note On event (identifying the specific pad as a distinct musical pitch), while stepping off sends a Note Off event.
    • Velocity: For FSRs or piezos, the speed and intensity of the footstep are mapped to a MIDI velocity value ranging from 0 to 127.
    • Continuous Controller (CC): Sustained shifting of body weight can be translated into CC messages to modulate audio parameters like volume, filter cutoff, or pitch bend.

Transmission to the Audio Engine

Once the MIDI commands are generated, they must travel reliably across the exhibit space. For short distances, the microcontroller sends MIDI data directly over USB (USB-MIDI). In larger museum spaces where audio racks are located tens or hundreds of feet away in control rooms, installations use traditional 5-pin DIN cables, RS-485 balanced serial signals, or MIDI over Ethernet (RTP-MIDI) to avoid latency and data degradation.

Sound Generation and Spatialization

At the destination, the incoming MIDI data feeds into an audio workstation or interactive software platform, such as Max/MSP, Pure Data, Ableton Live, or TouchDesigner.

The software maps incoming signals to specific sound events. A single step might trigger a discrete sample, advance an algorithmic melody, or alter an ambient background drone. In advanced exhibits, systems use multi-channel audio interfaces to route the triggered sounds to localized directional speakers positioned directly above or around the specific pad being pressed, creating an immersive, spatially accurate auditory response to the visitor’s movement.
