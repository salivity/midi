Why MIDI 2.0 Eliminates Parameter Smoothing

The transition from MIDI 1.0 to MIDI 2.0 introduces 32-bit continuous parameter resolution, replacing the classic 7-bit limitation with over 4 billion discrete steps. This massive increase in numerical precision ensures that parameter changes occur with continuous, analog-like fidelity right out of the box. Because the difference between adjacent control values is now mathematically microscopic and imperceptible to human hearing, digital synthesis engines and digital audio workstations (DAWs) no longer need to implement analog-style parameter smoothing to disguise stepping artifacts.

The Problem in MIDI 1.0: Zipper Noise

MIDI 1.0 relied on a 7-bit resolution for the vast majority of its continuous controllers (CCs), restricting values to an integer range between 0 and 127. When a musician swept a critical audio parameter—such as a synthesizer's low-pass filter cutoff, resonance, or master volume—the jump between any two adjacent values was substantial.

These noticeable discrete jumps created an audible artifact known as "zipper noise." To mask this harsh stepping, software developers and hardware engineers had to insert DSP-based parameter smoothing. By applying slew-rate limiters, one-pole low-pass filters, or linear interpolation to control inputs, the system artificially ramped values from one step to the next to simulate smooth analog potentiometers.

While parameter smoothing eliminated zipper noise, it introduced compromises:

  • Latency: Interpolating between coarse steps created a perceptible lag between user input and sound alteration.
  • Transient Smearing: Rapid, intentional parameter modulations were smoothed out, softening sharp, percussive adjustments.
  • CPU Overhead: Constantly calculating smoothing curves for dozens of active modulation streams consumed unnecessary processing power.

The Scale of 32-Bit Resolution

MIDI 2.0 expands parameter resolution through Universal MIDI Packets (UMP), providing up to 32 bits of control precision for pitch bend, velocity, assignable controllers, and standard CCs.

  • MIDI 1.0 (7-bit): 128 discrete values
  • MIDI 2.0 (32-bit): 4,294,967,296 discrete values

With over 4.29 billion increments, a complete sweep of a parameter across its full dynamic range yields step sizes that are mathematically microscopic.

Why Parameter Smoothing Is Obsolete

Below the Threshold of Human Perception

Human ears cannot detect value changes of this scale. In an audio system, a change of a fraction of a decibel is near the limit of human perception, while the voltage-level difference between two steps in a 32-bit control path is hundreds of times smaller than the noise floor of the highest-grade analog converters. The discrete steps are so infinitesimal that the control curve is effectively a continuous line.

Finer than Digital Audio Precision

Modern audio engines calculate DSP routines using 32-bit floating-point or 64-bit double-precision architectures. A 32-bit control signal maps directly into the dynamic range of modern audio math without requiring artificial upsampling. The control data matches or exceeds the precision of the audio buffers it modifies, rendering algorithmic interpolation technically redundant.

Zero-Latency Control

Without smoothing filters to buffer and ramp incoming data, parameter changes are applied instantaneously to the audio buffer. This allows hardware synths and virtual instruments to respond immediately to sharp cuts, high-frequency LFO automation, and expressive performance gestures without the phase smear or sluggishness inherent to analog-style slew filtering.
