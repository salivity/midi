Using MIDI Silent Pilot Tones for Sequencer Failover

In mission-critical live music production, redundant playback rigs rely on automated switchers to ensure audio never stops if a computer crashes. A silent pilot tone transmitted over MIDI functions as a real-time digital heartbeat between a digital audio workstation (DAW) and an automated failover switcher. By continuously monitoring this continuous stream of non-audible data, the failover system can instantly detect when a primary computer has frozen or crashed, immediately routing playback to a synchronized backup system with zero latency or human intervention.

The Role of a MIDI Heartbeat

A silent pilot tone is essentially a repeating sequence of non-audible MIDI messages sent on an isolated channel or dedicated virtual port. Commonly, technicians program a continuous stream of low-overhead MIDI events—such as standard Note On/Off commands on an unused note, continuous pitch bend messages, or low-level Control Change (CC) data.

Because these messages are assigned to an unmapped channel or swallowed directly by the switching hardware, they generate no audible sound through the front-of-house PA system. Their sole purpose is to indicate to the hardware switcher that the primary sequencer's transport and playback engine are actively running.

Why Audio-Only Detection Falls Short

Traditional failover systems often rely on an audio pilot tone—such as an inaudible high-frequency sine wave (e.g., 20 kHz to 24 kHz) running over an extra audio channel. While effective for detecting severed cables or disabled audio interfaces, audio-only monitoring has limitations when dealing with software freezes:

  • Audio Buffer Loops: When a DAW or operating system locks up completely, the audio interface may get trapped in an audio buffer loop, endlessly repeating the last few milliseconds of sound, including an audio pilot tone. The switcher might perceive the system as "alive" even though the sequence has stopped progressing.
  • CPU Starvation: An overloaded CPU can stall the timeline while the audio driver continues to output silence or maintain active output states.

MIDI data, by contrast, is tied directly to the sequencer timeline and application process. If the DAW engine stalls, crashes, or drops playback, the timeline halts, and the generation of MIDI events stops instantly.

How the Failover Hardware Interprets the Signal

The failover switcher—either a dedicated hardware unit or an intelligent audio interface—contains an internal watchdog timer. The process operates under simple logic:

  1. Continuous Reset: Each time the switcher receives a valid pulse from the silent MIDI pilot tone, the internal watchdog timer resets to zero.
  2. Threshold Monitoring: The hardware is programmed with a millisecond-level timeout threshold (typically between 50 ms and 250 ms).
  3. Threshold Breach: If the primary computer freezes, the MIDI stream ceases. The watchdog timer reaches its maximum limit without being reset.
  4. Instant Switchover: Once the threshold is breached, the switcher automatically flips its internal relays or solid-state switches, directing the main outputs away from the frozen primary rig to the continuously running secondary rig.

By leveraging a silent MIDI stream instead of relying strictly on audio detection or human reaction time, playback systems achieve a deterministic, fail-safe mechanism that keeps high-profile live shows running seamlessly.
