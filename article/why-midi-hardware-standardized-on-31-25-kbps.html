Why MIDI Hardware Standardized on 31.25 kbps

When the Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) specification was established in the early 1980s, its creators needed a transmission rate that balanced musical responsiveness, hardware affordability, and electrical reliability. The decision to settle on 31.25 kilobits per second (kbps) was not an arbitrary number, but a pragmatic engineering solution rooted in standard microprocessor clock speeds, binary division, low-cost component limitations, and acceptable human perception of latency.

Clean Division of Microprocessor Clock Crystals

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, synthesizers and audio gear relied on inexpensive 8-bit microprocessors, such as the Intel 8048, Intel 8051, Motorola 6800 family, and Zilog Z80. These systems operated using standard quartz crystals, frequently running at frequencies like 1 MHz, 2 MHz, 4 MHz, or 8 MHz.

Digital Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitters (UARTs) derive their serial transmission speeds by dividing the system clock using internal binary counters. Standard UART architecture typically samples incoming serial bits at 16 times the baud rate (a 16x clock) to ensure reliable edge detection.

The math works out cleanly with standard frequencies:

  • A 1 MHz clock divided by 2 produces 500 kHz.
  • 500 kHz divided by 16 (the UART oversampling rate) yields exactly 31,250 Hz, or 31.25 kbps.
  • Similarly, a 2 MHz clock divided by 64, or a 4 MHz clock divided by 128, directly yields 31,250 Hz.

Using 31.25 kbps eliminated the need for specialized, expensive crystal oscillators or dedicated baud rate generator chips. Manufacturers could simply tap into the CPU's primary master clock and use a basic binary frequency divider to drive serial communications.

Balancing Latency with Musical Timing

A primary design constraint led by Dave Smith (Sequential Circuits) and Ikutaro Kakehashi (Roland) was keeping latency below human perception. Musicians can generally detect timing delays larger than 5 to 10 milliseconds.

MIDI transmits data asynchronously using 10 bits per byte (1 start bit, 8 data bits, and 1 stop bit). At 31.25 kbps, transmitting one bit takes 32 microseconds, meaning a single 10-bit byte takes 320 microseconds (0.32 ms).

A standard MIDI "Note On" message consists of three bytes:

  1. Status byte (Note On and Channel)
  2. Data byte 1 (Note number)
  3. Data byte 2 (Velocity)

Transmitting this 3-byte message takes approximately 0.96 milliseconds. Even when triggering chords across several instruments via standard daisy-chaining, the delay remains well within an acceptable operational window for real-time live performance.

Inexpensive Optoisolators and Cable Runs

Higher speeds, such as the 500 kbps or 1 Mbps rates common in computing networks of the era, were technically possible but economically impractical for consumer instruments.

MIDI was designed to prevent ground loops—a common source of hum and noise in analog audio rigs—by requiring electrical optical isolation on every input. In 1982, high-speed optocouplers were expensive. Running serial data at 31.25 kbps permitted manufacturers to use cheap, readily available, relatively slow optoisolators (such as the Sharp PC900 or HP 6N138) without corrupting digital pulses.

Furthermore, lower speeds allowed musicians to run standard, unshielded 5-pin DIN cables up to 50 feet (15 meters) on stage without suffering severe signal degradation, radio frequency interference, or data reflections that plague higher-frequency serial lines.
