How Arduino Reads MIDI Using Optocoupler Shields

This article explains how the Arduino platform receives and processes standard Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) messages using dedicated optocoupler shield boards. It covers the transition of MIDI signals from a 5mA current-loop physical layer through galvanic optical isolation, and finally into standard TTL serial data read by the Arduino's hardware UART.

The MIDI Physical Layer Standard

MIDI communication relies on a 5mA current-loop standard rather than standard voltage-level logic. The protocol transmits asynchronous serial data at a fixed baud rate of 31,250 baud. In a MIDI cable, data is represented by the presence or absence of current: current flowing represents a logical "0" (space), and no current represents a logical "1" (mark). Because MIDI equipment often shares stages, studios, and separate power supplies, direct electrical connections can introduce ground loops, hum, or damaging voltage spikes.

The Function of the Optocoupler

To adhere to the MIDI specification and protect the microcontroller, dedicated MIDI shield boards use an optocoupler (such as the 6N138, PC900, or H11L1). An optocoupler is an integrated component containing an infrared light-emitting diode (LED) physically separated from a photodetector (such as a photodiode or phototransistor) by a transparent insulating barrier.

When an external MIDI device transmits a message, the current pulses flow across pins 4 and 5 of the 5-pin DIN or TRS jack. This current enters the shield and illuminates the optocoupler's internal LED. A reverse-biased protection diode (such as a 1N4148) is placed in parallel across the input pins to protect the optocoupler LED against accidental reverse polarity.

Because the incoming signal only powers the internal LED, there is no electrical connection between the sending device and the receiving Arduino. This galvanic isolation eliminates ground loops and protects the Arduino from external electrical faults.

Signal Reconstruction to TTL Logic

The internal photodetector on the output side of the optocoupler receives the light pulses and switches on and off accordingly. The output side is powered entirely by the Arduino's local power supply (typically +5V or +3.3V) and referenced to the Arduino's ground.

A pull-up resistor is connected between the optocoupler's output pin and the Arduino's positive voltage rail:

  • When the internal LED turns on (representing a logical "0" in current-loop terms), the phototransistor conducts, pulling the output pin to ground (LOW).
  • When the internal LED turns off (representing a logical "1"), the phototransistor ceases conduction, and the pull-up resistor pulls the output pin up to the logic level (HIGH).

This switching mechanism effectively translates the isolated current-loop pulses into standardized Transistor-Transistor Logic (TTL) voltage levels compatible with digital inputs.

UART Processing on the Arduino

The conditioned TTL signal exits the optocoupler circuit and is routed directly to the Arduino's hardware serial receive pin (RX, or Pin 0 on boards like the Arduino Uno).

The Arduino's Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter (UART) monitors this pin. By setting the serial port to 31,250 baud (via Serial.begin(31250)), the UART synchronizes with the incoming data frames:

  1. It detects the standard active-low start bit.
  2. It samples the next 8 data bits at precise timing intervals.
  3. It detects the stop bit to confirm the end of a byte.

Once the hardware UART decodes the incoming bitstream, it pushes the completed byte into the Arduino's serial receive buffer. From there, user code or dedicated MIDI software libraries read the raw bytes using Serial.read() to interpret MIDI command bytes (such as Note On, Note Off, or Control Change) and their associated data values.
