How MIDI Pedalboards Trigger 32-Foot Pipe Bass Ranks

This article explains the electro-mechanical process through which an automated organ pedalboard uses MIDI contact switches to trigger deep 32-foot pipe bass ranks. By translating physical foot movements into digital control data, modern organ systems bridge digital performance interfaces with massive acoustic pipe chests. Readers will learn how switch actuation, MIDI signal processing, and low-voltage electrical relays work together to open heavy wind valves and produce fundamental frequencies as low as 16 Hertz.

1. Pedal Actuation and MIDI Contact Switches

When an organist depresses a wooden pedal, physical motion is instantly detected by an electronic contact assembly mounted beneath or behind the pedal keys. Modern pedalboards typically employ one of three switch types:

  • Magnetic Reed Switches: A permanent magnet affixed to the underside of the moving pedal passes near a sealed glass reed switch, pulling the flexible metal contacts together.
  • Hall-Effect Sensors: A solid-state alternative where the pedal magnet alters an electrical current across a semiconductor, offering non-contact operation with zero mechanical wear.
  • Mechanical Spring Contacts: Traditional physical contacts made of gold or silver-plated wire that physically touch a busbar to close an electrical circuit.

Closing the switch sends a momentary direct current (DC) signal to an onboard MIDI encoder board.

2. Digital Encoding and Transmission

The encoder microcontroller reads the closed circuit, identifies the specific key (pedal notes typically span 30 to 32 notes, mapped from C2 to F4 or G4 in general MIDI nomenclature), and generates standard serial MIDI data.

This data packet consists of a Note On command, the specific note number, and a velocity value (typically a fixed value of 64 or 127 in organ control systems). When the foot leaves the pedal, the switch opens, generating a corresponding Note Off command. This digital stream is transmitted instantly via a standard 5-pin DIN cable, an optical cable, or a serial control network to the organ’s central transmission system or relay driver.

3. Relay Decoding and Stop Registration

Before a 32-foot pipe can sound, the MIDI message passes through the organ’s registration logic—either in a dedicated solid-state relay (SSR) computer or an automated console controller.

The system verifies whether a 32-foot stop (such as a 32' Contra Violone, Subbass, or Contra Bombarde) is currently engaged. If the stop is drawn, the control system routes the MIDI note to the corresponding electrical driver board dedicated to that specific pipe chest. Solid-state output drivers (such as MOSFETs or Darlington transistors) switch a higher-current 12V to 24V DC circuit on and off in exact synchronization with the incoming MIDI data.

4. Electro-Pneumatic Pallet Operation

A 32-foot pipe requires an enormous volume of low-pressure wind, meaning the valve (or "pallet") allowing wind into the pipe toe must be large and heavily weighted. Direct human physical linkage cannot easily pull open such large pallets; instead, the system utilizes electro-pneumatic action:

  1. Chest Magnet: The 12V–24V DC pulse from the driver energizes an electro-magnet inside the windchest.
  2. Armature Lift: The energized magnet lifts a small iron disc (armature), venting air from a miniature internal pneumatic bellows.
  3. Valve Exhaust: The collapsing primary pneumatic exhausts a secondary, larger pneumatic motor that holds the main pallet shut.
  4. Pallet Release: The main pallet pulls open, dumping air directly from the pressurized windchest into the foot of the 32-foot pipe.

5. Acoustic Wave Generation

With the windchest valve open, pressurized air streams through the pipe's flue or reed assembly. For a 32-foot low C, the pipe column vibrates at a fundamental pitch of roughly 16.35 Hz—a frequency at the absolute threshold of human hearing that is perceived more as physical floor vibration and acoustic pressure than a discrete tone.

The moment the organ pedal is released, the contact switch breaks, the MIDI encoder transmits a Note Off command, the relay cuts the current to the chest magnet, the pneumatic motor re-inflates, and the heavy pallet valve shuts, terminating wind flow to the pipe.
