Why Pitch-to-MIDI Guitars Lag on Low E Strings

Pitch-to-MIDI guitar converters frequently struggle with noticeable tracking latency when translating low E string notes compared to high E string notes. This performance gap is not a design flaw of any single manufacturer, but rather an unavoidable consequence of acoustics and digital signal processing. Because pitch detection algorithms depend on analyzing complete sound waves to determine pitch accurately, the physically longer period of low-frequency sound waves inherently delays note detection on lower strings.

The Physics of String Frequencies and Wave Periods

The primary cause of tracking latency is the physical time it takes for a vibrating string to complete a sound wave cycle. In standard guitar tuning (\(A = 440\text{ Hz}\)):

  • The open high E string vibrates at roughly 329.6 Hz. Its single cycle period (\(T = 1 / f\)) lasts approximately 3.03 milliseconds.
  • The open low E string vibrates at roughly 82.4 Hz. Its single cycle period lasts approximately 12.14 milliseconds.

Pitch-to-MIDI converters do not know what note is being played until the hardware analyzes the audio input. Because the low E string's wave cycle takes four times longer to complete than the high E string's, the converter must wait four times as long just to receive the raw acoustic data.

How Pitch-Detection Algorithms Work

Most pitch-to-MIDI converters use algorithms based on zero-crossing detection, autocorrelation, or Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) to determine the fundamental frequency of an audio signal.

To confirm a pitch without triggering false triggers or misidentifying octave overtones, an algorithm typically needs to analyze between 1.5 and 2 complete waveform cycles:

  • High E: Analyzing 1.5 cycles takes roughly 4.5 milliseconds.
  • Low E: Analyzing 1.5 cycles takes roughly 18.2 milliseconds.

When you add the converter's internal digital processing delay, MIDI transmission time (approximately 1 millisecond), and the target sound engine's buffer latency, low E tracking frequently exceeds 25 to 30 milliseconds—crossing the threshold of perceptible latency for musicians (typically around 10 to 15 milliseconds). Conversely, high E tracking easily stays within imperceptible limits.

Harmonic Complexity and Transient Interference

When a guitar pick strikes a string, the initial attack produces a chaotic, inharmonic burst of noise before settling into a stable fundamental frequency. Thick, wound strings—like the low E—produce high-amplitude upper harmonics that are significantly louder than the fundamental frequency during the first few milliseconds of the note.

Converters must apply low-pass filtering and specialized pitch-prediction logic to reject these initial transient overtones and prevent "octave jump" tracking errors. This extra computational step and filtering process adds further delay specifically to the lowest strings on the instrument.
