How Wind Synth Bite Sensors Control MIDI Pitch Bend

Wind synthesizers bridge traditional acoustic playing techniques with digital sound generation by using bite sensors in the mouthpiece to translate jaw pressure into real-time MIDI data. This article explains the electromechanical process behind bite sensors, how physical pressure translates into continuous digital values, and how these values are formatted into high-resolution MIDI Pitch Bend messages to emulate natural vibrato and expressive pitch manipulation.

The Mouthpiece Sensor Mechanism

The mouthpiece of an Electronic Wind Instrument (EWI) or wind controller contains a dedicated transducer designed to detect embouchure changes. The most common technologies include:

  • Force-Sensing Resistors (FSRs): Materials that decrease their electrical resistance as physical pressure increases.
  • Piezoelectric Elements: Crystals or ceramics that generate an electrical charge when mechanically deformed.
  • Optical or Capacitive Sensors: Systems that track physical deflection of a flexible mouthpiece reed or silicone cover without requiring direct mechanical contact.

When a performer applies or releases pressure with their teeth or lips, the sensor generates a variable analog voltage directly proportional to the force exerted.

Analog-to-Digital Conversion and Processing

The continuous analog voltage generated by the sensor is routed to the instrument’s internal microcontroller. An Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) samples this voltage at high speeds to prevent noticeable latency.

The onboard firmware processes the raw digital signal through user-defined parameters, including:

  • Dead Zones: A customizable threshold around the player's neutral resting bite, ensuring that standard mouth placement does not trigger accidental pitch shifts.
  • Scaling and Curves: Linear, logarithmic, or exponential response curves that match the physical resistance of the mouthpiece to the player's natural jaw physiology.
  • Filtering: Low-pass filtering or smoothing algorithms that remove electrical noise while preserving fast embouchure movements like vibrato.

Mapping to MIDI Pitch Bend Messages

Once the signal is processed, the microcontroller formats the data into standard MIDI Pitch Bend messages.

Unlike standard 7-bit Continuous Controllers (CC), which offer only 128 discrete steps, MIDI Pitch Bend uses a 14-bit data structure composed of two 7-bit bytes (Least Significant Byte and Most Significant Byte). This yields 16,384 distinct values (0 to 16,383), where 8,192 represents the center, unbent pitch. This high resolution eliminates audible "zipper noise" or stepping artifacts during smooth pitch fluctuations.

Depending on the instrument’s configuration, the bite sensor maps to this 14-bit scale in one of two primary ways:

  1. Bi-directional Embouchure (Acoustic Style): The neutral bite pressure rests at value 8,192. Increasing bite pressure pushes the value toward 16,383 (pitch up), while relaxing the jaw drops the value toward 0 (pitch down). This replicates the behavior of a traditional saxophone or clarinet embouchure.
  2. Uni-directional Modulation: Zero pressure sits at the center value of 8,192, and biting only bends the pitch upward toward 16,383. Downward bends are handled by a separate thumb sensor or dedicated pitch ribbon.

Synthesis and Sound Engine Response

When the connected synthesizer receives these MIDI Pitch Bend commands, its internal pitch-bend range settings determine how far the pitch actually moves. If the receiving patch is set to a range of two semitones, moving the bite sensor from its resting state to full pressure shifts the sound up by a whole step. This tight integration of high-resolution data conversion allows performers to execute subtle microtonal inflections, wide portamento slides, and realistic jaw vibrato that responds identically to acoustic wind performance.
