How Expression Pedals Convert Movement to MIDI CC 11

Expression pedals serve as vital performance tools, allowing musicians to modulate dynamics, timbre, and volume in real time using their feet. This article explains the exact electromechanical process an expression pedal uses to translate physical foot movement—from the angular rotation of the treadle down to internal mechanical linkages—into continuous control voltage, which a connected MIDI interface or keyboard converts into standardized 7-bit MIDI Continuous Controller 11 (CC 11) data packets.

1. Angular Treadle Rotation (Heel-to-Toe Motion)

The primary mechanical input begins at the treadle (the pedal platform). The user applies downward pressure with their foot, causing the treadle to pivot around a fixed fulcrum axle. This movement is purely rotational, usually spanning an arc between 15 and 30 degrees of angular displacement:

  • Heel-Down: Represents the minimum physical travel point (fully open position).
  • Toe-Down: Represents the maximum physical travel point (fully depressed position).

2. Internal Mechanical Linkage Systems

To convert this angular foot pivot into measurable electrical resistance, expression pedals rely on an internal linkage mechanism to drive a variable resistor. The most common mechanisms include:

  • Rack-and-Pinion Gear: A toothed plastic or metal arm (the rack) is mounted to the underside of the moving treadle. As the treadle tilts, the rack moves linearly up or down, engaging and rotating a toothed gear (the pinion) affixed to the shaft of a rotary potentiometer.
  • String and Cam (Pulley): Common in Ernie Ball–style designs, a durable Kevlar cable is anchored to the chassis and wrapped around a spring-loaded pulley wheel connected to a potentiometer. Rocking the pedal winds or unwinds the string, rotating the shaft smoothly.
  • Direct Lever Linkage: Simple mechanical arms that push or pull either a rotary potentiometer shaft or a straight slide-potentiometer wiper directly.
  • Magnetic/Optical (Non-Mechanical): Some modern pedals replace physical wipers with a magnet or optical shutter passing over a Hall-effect sensor or phototransistor, eliminating physical wear while still tracking the angular displacement.

3. Resistance Variation and Voltage Division

The movement of the linkage turns the shaft of a potentiometer (typically a 10kΩ to 100kΩ linear taper resistor). The pedal connects to a MIDI controller via a 1/4-inch TRS (Tip-Ring-Sleeve) cable:

  • The host MIDI device sends a reference voltage (usually +3.3V or +5V) along either the Tip or Ring, with the Sleeve serving as ground.
  • The physical sweep moves an internal conductive wiper along the resistive carbon or cermet track.
  • This forms a variable voltage divider, outputting a continuous analog voltage ranging linearly from 0V (heel-down) to the maximum supply voltage (toe-down).

4. Analog-to-Digital Conversion to MIDI CC 11

Once the variable analog voltage returns to the host MIDI interface or keyboard:

  • ADC Sampling: An onboard Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) reads the returning control voltage, sampling it at high speeds and converting the continuous voltage into a discrete numeric value (often 10-bit or 12-bit resolution internally, between 0 and 1023 or 4095).
  • MIDI Scaling: The device’s microcontroller firmware scales this digitized range down to the standard 7-bit MIDI protocol range of 0 to 127.
  • Message Generation: When assigned to Expression, the firmware bundles this value into a standard 3-byte MIDI Control Change message: [Status Byte: 0xBn] [Controller Number: 0x0B (11)] [Data Value: 0–127].

Through this chain of events, the continuous downward and upward rocking of the foot is translated directly into smooth, incremental MIDI CC 11 values for dynamic expression in software instruments and hardware synthesizers.
