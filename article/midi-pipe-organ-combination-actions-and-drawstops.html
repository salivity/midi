MIDI Pipe Organ Combination Actions and Drawstops

A pipe organ combination action is an automated registration system that allows an organist to recall complex stop selections instantly using preset buttons known as pistons. When modern pipe organ consoles incorporate MIDI, the combination action can interface with digital control systems, external synthesizers, and virtual pipe organ software. This article explores how a MIDI-enabled combination action operates and the step-by-step electronic process used to physically move electro-mechanical drawstops on a pipe organ console.

Understanding the Combination Action

In pipe organ performance, manually pulling and pushing dozens of individual stop controls (drawstops or stop tabs) while playing can be impractical. A combination action solves this by storing "memories" or presets. When an organist presses a thumb or toe piston, the combination action recalls the saved configuration and updates the organ’s registration.

In a MIDI-equipped console, registration states are encoded into standard MIDI messages—typically utilizing Program Change, Control Change (CC), or System Exclusive (SysEx) data. This allows the physical console to communicate with solid-state relay systems, remote pipe chambers, or virtual software like Hauptwerk, ensuring that physical stops and digital registers stay perfectly synchronized.

The Anatomy of an Electro-Mechanical Drawstop

Traditional mechanical organs required manual movement of wooden sliders. Modern electric-action consoles replace this mechanical link with electro-mechanical drawstops (often called solenoids).

A typical electro-mechanical drawstop unit consists of:

  • The Stop Knob and Shaft: The physical interface pulled or pushed by the organist.
  • Dual Solenoids (or a Bi-directional Motor): Two electromagnetic coils—one dedicated to pulling the stop "ON" (out) and one for pushing the stop "OFF" (in).
  • Position Sensors: Optical sensors, Hall-effect sensors, or magnetic reed switches that detect whether the stop is currently on or off, providing feedback to the control system.

How MIDI Triggers Physical Drawstops

When a preset is activated or an incoming MIDI message requests a stop change, a precise chain of electrical events occurs to physically actuate the drawstop:

  1. Message Reception and Processing: The organ's central control unit (a microprocessor-based solid-state system) receives a trigger. This can originate from a physical piston pressed by the player or an incoming MIDI message over a 5-pin DIN or USB interface.

  2. State Evaluation: The processor compares the requested registration against the current physical state of the drawstops, reading inputs from the drawstop position sensors. If a drawstop is already in the target position, the system ignores it to conserve power and reduce noise.

  3. Driver Board Activation: Microprocessors operate at low logic voltages (typically 3.3V or 5V), whereas electro-mechanical drawstop solenoids generally require higher direct current (commonly 12V to 24V DC at 1 to 2 amperes). To bridge this gap, the processor sends a signal to driver boards equipped with switching transistors or MOSFETs (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistors).

  4. Pulsing the Solenoid: The driver switches power to the appropriate coil (the "ON" or "OFF" solenoid) inside the drawstop mechanism. To prevent the electromagnetic coils from overheating or burning out, the current is delivered as a brief pulse—typically lasting between 50 and 100 milliseconds—rather than a continuous current.

  5. Mechanical Movement and Latching: The momentary magnetic field generated by the coil drives an internal armature forward or backward, snapping the drawstop knob to its new position. Many modern units employ small permanent magnets to hold the knob securely in place once the electrical pulse ends, eliminating mechanical slack without drawing standby power.

  6. Feedback Verification: Once the movement is complete, the internal sensor confirms the new physical position. The system logs the change and, if configured for bidirectional communication, sends an outgoing MIDI message confirming that the physical registration has updated.
