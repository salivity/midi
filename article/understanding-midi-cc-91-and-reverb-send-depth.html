Understanding MIDI CC 91 and Reverb Send Depth

MIDI CC 91 is the standardized MIDI continuous controller designated for Effects 1 Depth, which functions universally across audio production hardware and software as the Reverb Send Level. This article explores the specific operational parameters altered by MIDI CC 91 when interfacing with global sound processors, distinguishing between the track-level send amplitude it controls and the master architectural settings of the global effects processor itself.

Standard Definition and Primary Function

Under the official MIDI Manufacturers Association (MMA) and General MIDI (GM, GM2, GS, and XG) standards, Control Change 91 (CC 91) is assigned to Effects 1 Depth (Reverb Send).

When applied to an individual MIDI channel, CC 91 transmits a value from 0 to 127. This value governs the auxiliary send level feeding that channel’s audio into a master or global reverberation unit:

  • Value 0: Turns the reverb send off entirely, rendering the channel’s output completely dry.
  • Value 64: Sets the send level to standard unity or default half-power, balancing direct sound with spatial reflections.
  • Value 127: Routes maximum signal amplitude to the global processor, yielding an intensely wet, distant sound profile.

Send Level vs. Internal Processor Parameters

A common point of confusion is whether CC 91 changes the acoustic properties of the reverb processor itself. In typical implementations, it does not. Global sound processor parameters such as decay time, room size, early reflection delay, and high-frequency damping remain unaltered when CC 91 messages are received.

Instead, CC 91 governs the input gain directed to the processor:

  1. Ratio of Direct to Reverberant Sound: By increasing or decreasing the signal level entering the effect bus, CC 91 alters the wet/dry balance for that specific timbre. This psychoacoustically positions the sound closer to or farther from the listener in the virtual soundstage.
  2. Dynamic Energy into the Reverb Algorithm: In dynamic DSP algorithms (such as non-linear or threshold-dependent reverbs), a higher CC 91 value increases the driving energy feeding the algorithm, changing how the simulated acoustic space saturates or responds without adjusting the master parameters.

Implementation in Modern Sound Engines

Depending on how a synthesizer, sampler, or digital audio workstation (DAW) is architected, CC 91 affects processor depth in two distinct configurations:

1. Multi-Timbral and Global Auxiliary Engines

In multi-timbral setups (such as GM sound modules or multi-out samplers), a single global reverb processor serves all 16 MIDI channels. Here, CC 91 acts purely as an auxiliary send fader. Each channel sends a discrete proportion of its audio into the shared space, preserving a unified acoustic environment while allowing depth separation between instruments.

2. Insert-Based or Self-Contained Engines

In standalone virtual instruments (VSTs) where the reverb module is applied as a local insert effect rather than an auxiliary send, developers often remap CC 91 to control the Wet/Dry Mix percentage. While functionally similar to a send level, this implementation directly balances the output blend knob rather than routing through an internal audio bus.

Channel-Wide vs. Global CC Behavior

Because CC 91 is a channel-specific controller, transmitting CC 91 on MIDI Channel 1 adjusts only that channel's depth within the global processor. To adjust global processor parameters across the entire engine—such as changing master reverb decay time or room algorithms—systems rely on System Exclusive (SysEx) messages, Non-Registered Parameter Numbers (NRPNs), or dedicated host automation rather than CC 91.
