Why V/Oct Converters Fail with Hz/V Synths

Standard Volts per Octave (V/Oct) MIDI-to-CV converters fail to track properly on Hertz per Volt (Hz/V) synthesizers because the two systems rely on fundamentally incompatible mathematical standards to determine pitch. While V/Oct sends linear voltage increases that the synthesizer internally translates exponentially, Hz/V requires an exponential voltage curve sent directly from the controller because the synthesizer’s oscillator responds linearly. Connecting a standard V/Oct output directly to an Hz/V input results in severe pitch tracking errors, causing musical intervals to compress dramatically as you play higher up the keyboard.

In the V/Oct standard—popularized by Moog, Roland, ARP, and Eurorack—pitch is controlled logarithmically. Human pitch perception is exponential; each octave represents a doubling of sound frequency (for example, A2 is 110 Hz, A3 is 220 Hz, and A4 is 440 Hz). Under V/Oct, the controller outputs an equal voltage step for every semitone (1/12th of a volt) and adds precisely 1.0 volt for every octave increase. To make this linear voltage step match human hearing, synthesizers built for V/Oct feature an internal exponential converter circuit that translates the steady voltage steps into the exponentially doubling frequencies required by the oscillator.

The Hz/V standard—most famously used by vintage Korg (such as the MS-20) and Yamaha synthesizers—approaches pitch control differently by eliminating the internal exponential converter. Instead, the oscillator core is strictly linear, meaning the input voltage is directly proportional to the output frequency. If 1 volt produces 55 Hz, you must provide 2 volts to reach 110 Hz, 4 volts to reach 220 Hz, and 8 volts to reach 440 Hz. The pitch doubles only when the incoming control voltage doubles.

When you send a V/Oct signal into an Hz/V input, you are applying equal additive voltage steps to an oscillator that expects geometrically doubling voltage steps. For instance, playing an octave up on a standard V/Oct converter simply increases the output by +1V. On a 1V-per-octave scale starting at 1V (e.g., 1V = 100 Hz), adding 1V raises the frequency to 200 Hz, successfully doubling the pitch for that single octave. However, the next octave on a V/Oct converter only increases the signal to 3V, which only yields 300 Hz on the Hz/V synth—far short of the 400 Hz needed for the next octave.

As a result of this mathematical mismatch, notes played via a direct V/Oct connection become progressively flatter and squashed into microtonal clusters as you ascend the keyboard. Proper pitch tracking between modern MIDI gear and vintage Hz/V instruments requires a dedicated converter capable of logarithmic-to-linear conversion, either through hardware-level scaling or software-configurable CV output curves.
