V-Trig vs S-Trig: Moog Trigger Types Explained

Connecting vintage analog synthesizers to modern sequencers and MIDI controllers requires understanding how different instruments communicate note events. When integrating vintage Moog equipment with MIDI-to-CV converters, the most critical consideration is the difference between Voltage Triggers (V-Trig) and Switch Triggers (S-Trig). While both tell a synthesizer when to start and stop a note, they use fundamentally opposite electrical methods to transmit this information, making proper conversion essential to avoid inverted behavior or hardware damage.

What is a V-Trig (Voltage Trigger)?

V-Trig, commonly referred to as a standard Gate or Voltage Trigger, is the most common triggering standard across analog synthesizers, including Roland, ARP, Sequential Circuits, Oberheim, and modern Eurorack modular gear.

  • Inactive State (Key Off): 0 Volts (Ground).
  • Active State (Key On): A positive voltage is sent down the wire, typically jumping instantly to +5V, +10V, or +15V depending on the manufacturer.

When you strike a key on a standard MIDI controller, a standard MIDI-to-CV converter outputs a steady positive voltage for as long as the key is held, triggering the envelope generators. When you release the key, the voltage returns to zero, initiating the envelope's release phase.

What is an S-Trig (Switch Trigger)?

S-Trig, or Switch Trigger, is the proprietary standard developed by Bob Moog and used extensively on vintage instruments like the Minimoog Model D, Micromoog, Polymoog, and Moog modular systems.

Unlike V-Trig, an S-Trig does not send a positive voltage pulse to activate a note. Instead, it acts as an electronic switch that connects the signal line to ground:

  • Inactive State (Key Off): Open circuit. The input on the synthesizer is internally pulled up to a positive voltage (typically around +5V to +15V depending on the model).
  • Active State (Key On): Shorted to ground (0 Volts).

Vintage Moog instruments often use a specialized two-pin Cinch-Jones connector for their S-Trig inputs rather than standard 1/4-inch or 3.5mm phone jacks to prevent users from accidentally plugging in standard voltage sources.

The Core Distinctions and Incompatibilities

The primary distinction is polarity and electrical behavior:

  1. Active High vs. Active Low: V-Trig is "active high," requiring positive voltage to fire a note. S-Trig is "active low," requiring zero volts (ground connection) to fire a note.
  2. Signal Source vs. Passive Switch: A V-Trig output drives a voltage into a synth's circuit. An S-Trig output simply closes a path to ground, letting the synthesizer’s internal voltage drain away.

Plugging a standard V-Trig output directly into an S-Trig input causes several issues:

  • Inverted Gate: The synthesizer will behave backward, droning continuously when no note is played and cutting off sound whenever a key is pressed.
  • Failure to Trigger: The incoming positive voltage may conflict with the internal pull-up resistor of the Moog, causing the envelope to never fire at all.
  • Component Stress: Feeding continuous external positive voltage into an S-Trig circuit not designed to handle high current can stress vintage input components.

Converting V-Trig to S-Trig with MIDI Converters

To control a vintage Moog via MIDI, your converter must support S-Trig. Hardware approaches generally fall into two categories:

  • Configurable MIDI-to-CV Converters: Dedicated units (such as those from Kenton) feature internal software or hardware jumpers that allow you to switch the gate output type directly from V-Trig (+5V or +12V) to S-Trig (active ground pull-down).
  • Passive Conversion Cables: If a MIDI-to-CV converter only outputs standard V-Trig, you must place an external converter circuit between the converter and the synthesizer. This is typically achieved using a basic NPN transistor (such as a 2N3904) and a resistor inside a cable adapter. The incoming positive voltage from the V-Trig saturates the transistor, connecting the Moog’s S-Trig input to ground and properly triggering the instrument.
