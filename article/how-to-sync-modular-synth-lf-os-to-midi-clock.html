How to Sync Modular Synth LFOs to MIDI Clock

Synchronizing analog low-frequency oscillators (LFOs) to a digital MIDI clock requires converting serial digital timing data into analog electrical pulses. Modular synthesizer enthusiasts achieve this by routing a master MIDI clock through a dedicated MIDI-to-CV interface, which translates MIDI timing messages into analog clock or gate signals. These voltage pulses are then patched directly into the sync, reset, or tap-tempo inputs of an LFO, aligning modulation rates perfectly with external hardware, drum machines, or digital audio workstations (DAWs).

The Conversion: From Digital Packets to Voltage Pulses

MIDI clock is a digital protocol that sends 24 pulses per quarter note (PPQN) as serial data. Analog modular synthesizers (such as Eurorack or 5U formats) cannot interpret digital data directly; they require control voltage (CV).

To bridge this gap, enthusiasts connect the MIDI source (via 5-pin DIN or USB-MIDI) to a hardware MIDI-to-CV converter module. The module decodes the digital timing bytes and generates a short 5V or 10V gate or trigger pulse for each clock division. Most modern converters allow users to configure the output resolution, scaling the high-density 24 PPQN down to musical subdivisions such as 16th notes (4 PPQN), 8th notes, or quarter notes.

Patching into Analog LFOs

Once the analog clock pulses are generated at the converter’s output jack, they are patched to the LFO via standard 3.5mm or 1/4-inch patch cables. Depending on the design of the target LFO, the synchronization behaves in one of three primary ways:

  1. Hard Reset / Sync: Classic analog LFOs typically feature a "Reset" or "Sync" input. When a voltage trigger hits this jack, the LFO's waveform restarts immediately from its zero-crossing or peak. If the LFO's free-running frequency is manually tuned close to the tempo, this reset forces the waveform to maintain rhythmic phase with the master tempo.
  2. Clock-Synced and Tap-Tempo LFOs: Hybrid or microprocessor-driven analog LFOs feature internal cycle counters. Patching a clock pulse into these modules automatically sets the LFO's frequency to match the incoming pulse interval, eliminating phase drift without requiring manual rate adjustment.
  3. Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) Tracking: Some advanced analog LFOs use PLL circuits to track incoming clock frequencies. The PLL compares the incoming pulse train to the internal oscillator core, continually adjusting the core’s voltage to lock frequency and phase precisely to the incoming clock.

Managing Subdivisions with Clock Utilities

Because free-running analog LFOs can produce chaotic or stuttering waveforms if reset too rapidly, modular users frequently place clock utilities between the MIDI converter and the LFO.

By inserting a clock divider or multiplier into the signal chain, users can scale down a rapid 16th-note clock to trigger once every 1, 2, or 4 bars. This slower pulse train resets the LFO at musically relevant intervals—such as the start of a bar—allowing smooth, sweeping modulation across long passages while remaining strictly locked to the digital grid.
