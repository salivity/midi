Expanding MIDI Controller Inputs with Multiplexers

Building massive MIDI controller desks with dozens of potentiometers, faders, and rotary knobs quickly exhausts the native analog-to-digital converter (ADC) pins of standard microcontrollers. Analog multiplexer integrated circuits (ICs), such as the 74HC4051, overcome this physical limitation by functioning as digitally controlled multi-way switches that route multiple analog signals into a single ADC pin sequentially. This article explains the internal architecture of the 74HC4051, details how time-division multiplexing routes input data without noticeable latency, and provides the hardware and software principles required to scale these chips across large MIDI consoles.

The ADC Bottleneck in MIDI Controller Design

Most common microcontrollers, such as the ATmega328P, RP2040, or ESP32, offer between 4 and 16 analog input channels. Professional audio mixing desks and complex MIDI control surfaces often demand 32, 64, or even 128 continuous controls to manage audio parameters, synthesizer parameters, and volume levels.

Upgrading to microcontrollers with massive pin counts significantly drives up cost, PCB complexity, and routing difficulty. Analog multiplexers provide a cost-effective, scalable alternative by trading high processing speeds for pin availability, leveraging the fact that human interaction with physical controls occurs at speeds far slower than microsecond-level microcontroller operations.

How the 74HC4051 Multiplexer Works

The 74HC4051 is an 8-channel analog multiplexer/demultiplexer. Mechanically, it functions identically to an 8-position single-pole rotary switch, but its position is chosen electronically via logic gates and bilateral CMOS switches.

The IC features:

  • 8 Independent I/O Channels (Y0–Y7): Connected to individual potentiometers or faders.
  • 1 Common I/O Pin (Z): Connected to a single microcontroller ADC pin.
  • 3 Digital Binary Select Pins (S0, S1, S2): Controlled by microcontroller digital output pins.
  • 1 Active-Low Enable Pin (/E): Enables or disables the multiplexer switches.

The microcontroller selects which channel connects to the common pin by setting a 3-bit binary combination across pins S0, S1, and S2:

  • 000 connects Channel 0 (Y0) to Common (Z)
  • 001 connects Channel 1 (Y1) to Common (Z)
  • 010 connects Channel 2 (Y2) to Common (Z)
  • Continuing through 111, which connects Channel 7 (Y7) to Common (Z)

Because the internal switches are bidirectional, the 74HC4051 can route analog voltages smoothly in either direction with minimal signal degradation, handling standard 0V to 5V (or 0V to 3.3V) potentiometer sweeps.

Scanning Inputs Using Time-Division Multiplexing

To read all 8 inputs from a single ADC pin, the microcontroller executes a high-speed loop using time-division multiplexing:

  1. Address Selection: The microcontroller sets the binary state of S0, S1, and S2 to address the desired channel.
  2. Settling Time: A brief delay (often less than a microsecond) allows the internal resistance and capacitance of the switch, along with circuit trace capacitance, to stabilize.
  3. ADC Acquisition: The microcontroller triggers its internal ADC to sample the voltage from the common Z pin.
  4. Data Storage: The converted digital value is saved into an array tracking the previous state of that control.
  5. Next Channel: The loop increments the binary address and repeats the sequence.

Because an analog read typically takes between 10 and 100 microseconds depending on the microcontroller architecture, all eight channels can be sampled in less than one millisecond. The human ear and hand cannot perceive latency below 5 to 10 milliseconds, making the multiplexing completely invisible to the performer.

Scaling to Massive Consoles

To read 32, 64, or 128 controls, multiple 74HC4051 ICs are combined using one of two primary architectural strategies:

Parallel Address Architecture (Multi-ADC Pin)

The most common approach connects the digital address lines (S0, S1, S2) of all multiplexers together in parallel.

  • By dedicating 3 digital pins for the shared address lines, 8 separate 74HC4051 chips can be addressed simultaneously.
  • Each multiplexer's common Z pin connects to its own dedicated microcontroller ADC pin.
  • Setting the address pins to 000 allows the microcontroller to read Channel 0 of all eight ICs concurrently across 8 ADC pins.
  • This configuration reads 64 analog controls using only 11 microcontroller pins (3 digital outputs + 8 analog inputs).

Cascaded Enable Architecture (Single-ADC Pin)

When microcontroller ADC pins are severely limited, multiple multiplexers can share both the address lines and a single common analog input pin.

  • The common Z pins of all multiplexers tie together into a single ADC pin.
  • The active-low Enable (/E) pin on each IC is driven independently (often via a digital demultiplexer like the 74HC138 or a shift register).
  • Only one multiplexer is enabled at any given moment, while the others remain in a high-impedance state, preventing signal collisions.
  • This configuration allows dozens of potentiometers to route through a single analog pin on the processor.

Hardware and Signal Integrity Considerations

While multiplexing simplifies routing, high channel counts present electronic challenges that require careful board design:

  • On-State Resistance (\(R_{ON}\)): The internal switches have an internal resistance (typically around 60 to 80 ohms at 5V). Using potentiometers with values between \(10\,\text{k}\Omega\) and \(50\,\text{k}\Omega\) ensures that the internal switch resistance remains a negligible fraction of the total impedance, preventing voltage drop errors.
  • Crosstalk and Settling Capacitance: The ADC internal sampling capacitor takes time to charge. When switching rapidly from a channel at 0V to a channel at 5V, residual charge can bleed between readings. Adding a small settling delay (or taking two consecutive ADC readings and discarding the first) eliminates ghosting between adjacent channels.
  • Decoupling Capacitors: Placing a \(0.1\,\mu\text{F}\) ceramic capacitor directly across the VCC and GND pins of each 74HC4051 minimizes switching noise generated when toggling address lines.

Software Processing and MIDI Generation

Once values are digitized, the microcontroller converts raw voltages into standard MIDI Control Change (CC) messages:

  1. Jitter Filtering: Raw ADC readings naturally fluctuate by a few millivolts due to environmental electromagnetic interference. Software deadbanding (ignoring changes below a set threshold, such as 4–8 counts on a 10-bit ADC) prevents controllers from transmitting continuous streams of phantom MIDI messages.
  2. Resolution Mapping: Raw readings (typically 10-bit or 12-bit) are downscaled to standard 7-bit MIDI CC values (0–127) or mapped to 14-bit high-resolution MIDI formats.
  3. Change Detection: The microcontroller compares the newly mapped value against the last transmitted value for that channel. A MIDI message is only sent via serial UART or USB-MIDI if a deliberate state change has occurred, preserving bus bandwidth and processor time.
