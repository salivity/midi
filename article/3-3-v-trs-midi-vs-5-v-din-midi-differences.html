3.3V TRS MIDI vs 5V DIN MIDI Differences

This article provides an overview of the electrical transition from traditional 5-volt 5-pin DIN MIDI to the modern 3.3-volt TRS (Tip-Ring-Sleeve) MIDI specification. It breaks down how the change in operating voltage impacts the current loop design, the modification of internal resistor values, device backward compatibility, and the challenges associated with powering legacy accessory hardware from lower-voltage outputs.

The Underlying Current Loop Architecture

MIDI 1.0 does not transmit digital messages using raw voltage logic levels (such as 0V and 5V pulses). Instead, it relies on an opto-isolated 5-milliampere (mA) current loop. In this design, binary state "0" (active) corresponds to current flowing through the loop to illuminate an internal LED within an optocoupler at the receiving device, while binary state "1" (idle) corresponds to no current flowing.

Because the underlying mechanism is defined by current rather than voltage, transitioning from a 5V source to a 3.3V source requires preserving that target current of approximately 5 mA across the loop while accommodating lower rail voltages from modern microcontrollers.

Changes in Resistor Values

In the original 5V DIN standard, the current loop circuit relies on three separate 220-ohm resistors:

  • One 220-ohm pull-up resistor connected to the +5V supply rail at the transmitter.
  • One 220-ohm protection resistor connected to the UART TX output (open-drain or logic gate) at the transmitter.
  • One 220-ohm series resistor located in front of the optocoupler LED at the receiver.

Accounting for an optocoupler LED forward voltage drop (\(V_F\)) of roughly 1.5V to 1.7V, the original circuit had a total loop resistance of 660 ohms, resulting in approximately 5 mA of operating current: \[\frac{5.0\text{V} - 1.5\text{V}}{220\,\Omega + 220\,\Omega + 220\,\Omega} \approx 5.3\text{ mA}\]

When operating from a 3.3V rail, utilizing standard 220-ohm resistors would drop the loop current too low to reliably trigger standard optocouplers: \[\frac{3.3\text{V} - 1.5\text{V}}{660\,\Omega} \approx 2.7\text{ mA}\]

To rectify this, the MIDI Manufacturers Association (MMA) updated the hardware specification to lower the output resistor values on 3.3V transmitters. By lowering the transmitter's internal resistors (typically down to 33 ohms and 10 ohms, depending on the driver configuration), the circuit ensures that even with a legacy receiver’s 220-ohm input resistor, total resistance remains low enough to maintain the required 5 mA forward current: \[\frac{3.3\text{V} - 1.5\text{V}}{33\,\Omega + 10\,\Omega + 220\,\Omega} \approx 6.8\text{ mA}\]

Connector Pinout and Cabling Differences

The mechanical standard also changes the transmission line layout:

  • 5-Pin DIN: Pin 4 carries the voltage rail via a current-limiting resistor (source), Pin 5 carries the inverted data signal (sink), and Pin 2 serves as the ground shield connected only at the MIDI Out/Thru ports.
  • TRS MIDI (Specification Type A): Tip carries the current source (equivalent to DIN Pin 4), Ring carries the current sink/data (equivalent to DIN Pin 5), and Sleeve connects to the shield/ground (equivalent to DIN Pin 2).

While the electrical behavior mirrors the DIN specification through different mechanical contacts, 3.3V TRS outputs operate with tighter tolerances on cable capacitance and length due to lower overall driving margins.

Interoperability and Legacy Accessory Power

A major difference between the two signaling levels is compatibility with passively powered MIDI inline devices. Numerous legacy accessories—such as MIDI mergers, splitters, inline filters, and wireless adapters—draw parasitic operating power directly from the 5V line on Pin 4 of a traditional DIN port.

A 3.3V TRS MIDI output often cannot supply sufficient voltage or current to run these legacy 5V devices. While a 3.3V transmitter can reliably send data to a properly compliant 5V optical receiver, attempting to operate bus-powered MIDI utilities designed exclusively for 5V DIN ports from a 3.3V TRS jack usually causes the accessory to brown out or fail entirely.
