Why Large MIDI Chords Create a Strum Artifact

When a pianist plays a simultaneous ten-note chord using a traditional serial MIDI connection, the notes do not reach the sound module at the exact same moment. Because standard 5-pin DIN MIDI transmits data sequentially over a low-bandwidth connection, each note's data must wait in line to be sent. This creates a cumulative delay of approximately 7 to 10 milliseconds between the first and last note, which the human ear easily perceives as a slight roll, flam, or microscopic strumming effect rather than a unified impact.

The Serial Transmission Bottleneck

Traditional MIDI relies on a serial interface, meaning it sends information sequentially along a single data path, one bit at a time. Unlike a modern parallel or packet-based interface that can bundle multiple events together, serial MIDI processes musical performances as a strict chronological queue. Even if a performer's fingers strike all ten keys at the identical physical millisecond, the MIDI interface must serialize the physical action into individual electronic messages.

The Fixed Baud Rate

The standard transmission speed of 5-pin DIN MIDI is locked at 31,250 bits per second (31.25 kbaud). This baud rate was established in the early 1980s as a cost-effective, robust standard resistant to electrical noise, but it imposes a hard ceiling on data throughput:

  • Every byte of MIDI data requires 10 bits in transmission (1 start bit, 8 data bits, and 1 stop bit).
  • At 31,250 bits per second, transferring a single byte takes exactly 320 microseconds (0.32 milliseconds).

Data Requirements of a 10-Note Chord

A standard MIDI "Note On" message consists of three sequential bytes: the status byte (message type and MIDI channel), the note number (pitch), and the velocity (striking force).

  1. Standard Mode (3 Bytes per Note):
    • 1 note = 3 bytes = 30 bits = 0.96 ms.
    • 10 notes = 30 bytes = 300 bits = 9.6 ms.
  2. Running Status Mode (Optimized):
    • MIDI protocol allows consecutive messages of the same type to omit the status byte.
    • The first note uses 3 bytes (30 bits), and the subsequent 9 notes use 2 bytes each (180 bits), totaling 210 bits.
    • 210 bits at 31.25 kbaud = 6.72 ms.

Under optimal real-world conditions over a serial cable, there is an unavoidable hardware latency gap of nearly 7 to 10 milliseconds between the start of the first note and the start of the tenth note.

Human Perception and Transients

The human auditory system is exceptionally sensitive to temporal differences in sharp, percussive transients. An acoustic piano has an abrupt, instantaneous attack transient when the hammer hits the string. While humans often perceive delays below 10 milliseconds as simultaneous when sounds are sustained or soft, percussive onsets separated by as little as 2 to 5 milliseconds are easily distinguished as separate events.

When a 10-note piano attack is stretched across a 7-to-10-millisecond transmission window, the transient attacks of the individual notes stagger across the timeline. The ear resolves these individual high-frequency attacks sequentially, producing an audible flam or strum artifact rather than a single, coherent acoustic strike.
