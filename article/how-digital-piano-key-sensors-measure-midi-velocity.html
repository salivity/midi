How Digital Piano Key Sensors Measure MIDI Velocity

Weighted digital pianos determine how loudly a note should sound not by measuring strike force directly, but by calculating the elapsed time between physical contact points during a keystroke. This article explores the mechanical sensor arrays—predominantly dual- and triple-contact rubber dome switches—that track key or hammer motion. It explains how these physical switches register the travel time delta and how the piano's internal microprocessor converts that measurement into a standardized MIDI velocity value from 1 to 127.

The Primary Mechanism: Rubber Dome Contact Switches

The most common mechanical sensors in weighted digital pianos are elastomeric rubber dome switches. These are arranged in flexible silicone strips mounted directly beneath the key bed or hammer assembly on a printed circuit board (PCB).

Each key position features two or three tiny rubber domes with conductive carbon pills (or carbon-doped rubber pads) on their interior undersides. Directly beneath these domes on the PCB are complementary, non-connected pairs of interleaved copper traces. When the key or hammer mechanism depresses a dome, the conductive pill bridges the copper traces, completing an electrical circuit and sending an instantaneous logic signal to the instrument's processor.

Calculating the Time Delta

Velocity in physics is distance divided by time (\(v = \Delta d / \Delta t\)). Because the physical distance between the contact switches inside the piano action is fixed, velocity is inversely proportional to the time it takes to travel between them:

  1. Upper Sensor (Start Point): As the key is pressed down, the first switch dome is compressed, closing the first circuit. This starts an internal microsecond hardware timer.
  2. Lower Sensor (End Point): As the key reaches the bottom of its travel (the key bed), the second dome is compressed, closing the second circuit. This stops the timer.

The microprocessor records the difference in time (\(\Delta t\)). A rapid, forceful keystroke produces a very small \(\Delta t\), which the processor maps to a high MIDI velocity (closer to 127). A slow, gentle keystroke results in a larger \(\Delta t\), yielding a low MIDI velocity (closer to 1). If the time delta exceeds a preset threshold, the note registers at the minimum velocity (usually 1), while a zero or near-zero delta is filtered out to prevent erroneous triggering.

Dual-Sensor vs. Triple-Sensor Configurations

The number of mechanical switches per key directly impacts how accurately the instrument reflects acoustic piano performance:

  • Dual-Sensor Actions: These utilize two contact points (high and low). To play the same note again, the key must return almost completely to its resting position to reset the upper switch before another time delta can be calculated.
  • Triple-Sensor Actions: These introduce an intermediate contact switch positioned between the top and bottom contacts. This third sensor allows the processor to measure velocity from a partially released position. If a player lifts the key past the middle sensor and strikes downward again, the system calculates a new \(\Delta t\) between the middle and lower sensors without requiring a full key return. This mechanically replicates the double-escapement mechanism of an acoustic grand piano, permitting rapid note repetition.

Key Sensing vs. Hammer Sensing

In entry-level weighted actions, the rubber dome switches sit directly under the front or middle of the plastic key lever. In advanced fully weighted and hammer-action pianos, the sensors are placed beneath the counterweighted hammer mechanism at the back of the action.

Because the hammer is thrown freely by the key lever, sensing the hammer's movement rather than the key's movement produces a more authentic velocity calculation. It captures the true kinetic energy of the simulated hammer throw, accounting for subtleties like playing softly from the key surface versus striking from a height.

Alternative Sensor Types

While rubber dome mechanical contacts dominate the digital piano market due to their low cost and tactile reliability, alternative sensor technologies are used in select high-end hybrid instruments:

  • Optical Sensors: Infrared light-emitting diodes (LEDs) paired with phototransistors read continuous shutters attached to the keys or hammers. They calculate \(\Delta t\) by detecting the interruption of light beams, eliminating mechanical friction and physical wear.
  • Magnetic (Hall-Effect) Sensors: Magnets mounted on the moving keys generate variable voltage in stationary Hall-effect sensors on the circuit board, tracking distance and time electronically without direct mechanical contact.
