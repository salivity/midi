Why Was the MIDI Sample Dump Standard So Slow?

The MIDI Sample Dump Standard (SDS) was notoriously sluggish when transferring multi-megabyte sound libraries because it relied on an architecture engineered exclusively for real-time performance data rather than high-speed file transfers. Introduced in the late 1980s, SDS was forced to operate within the strict physical and logical boundaries of the standard MIDI specification. A combination of a tiny hardware bandwidth, severe 7-bit data packaging constraints, packet verification overhead, and the sheer mathematical scale of multi-megabyte audio files created a massive bottleneck that often stretched transfer times into hours.

The 31.25 kbaud Physical Bandwidth Limit

The primary bottleneck was the physical MIDI hardware specification itself. Standard 5-pin DIN MIDI communicates via an asynchronous serial interface locked at a fixed transmission rate of 31,250 bits per second (31.25 kbaud).

Because asynchronous serial transmission requires start and stop framing bits alongside the eight data bits, transmitting a single 8-bit byte actually requires 10 bits on the wire. This immediately lowered the absolute theoretical throughput of the hardware connection to just 3,125 bytes per second (approximately 3.05 KB/s), regardless of the host computer's or sampler's processing power.

7-Bit Data Encoding Penalties

The MIDI protocol reserves the eighth bit (the Most Significant Bit, or MSB) of every byte to differentiate between status commands (bit = 1) and data values (bit = 0). As a result, all payload data transmitted over MIDI is restricted to a 7-bit format (values 0–127).

Digital audio samples, however, are typically recorded in 12-bit, 16-bit, or 24-bit linear resolution. To transmit this data without triggering an unintended MIDI status command, SDS had to chop native binary audio words into 7-bit fragments. For example, transmitting a single 16-bit audio sample required three separate 7-bit MIDI bytes (21 bits of serial data to convey 16 bits of actual audio). This bit-packing scheme instantly inflated the total volume of raw data that needed to travel over the wire by roughly 30 percent.

Packet Framing and Handshaking Latency

SDS packages audio into System Exclusive (SysEx) messages. To prevent buffer overflows on hardware samplers with limited internal memory and slow microprocessors, sample data was split into tiny packets containing just 120 bytes of actual audio data.

Each packet carried extensive overhead:

  • SysEx start (F0) and end (F7) markers
  • Manufacturer and device identification codes
  • Packet number identifiers
  • Error-checking checksums

Additionally, transfers could operate in "closed-loop" (handshake) mode. In this mode, the receiving sampler had to verify the checksum and send an explicit Acknowledge (ACK), Wait (WAIT), or Cancel (CANCEL) packet back through the reverse MIDI cable before the transmitting device was permitted to send the next packet. The hardware latency of the sampler's CPU parsing the checksum and responding added significant dead time between every 120-byte burst.

The Scale of Multi-Megabyte Banks

When accounting for serial framing, 7-bit expansion, packet headers, and handshaking pauses, the real-world throughput of an SDS transfer generally hovered between 1.5 KB/s and 2.2 KB/s.

Under these conditions, simple mathematics highlights why multi-megabyte sound banks were impractical:

  • 1 Megabyte of audio: Approximately 8 to 11 minutes to transfer.
  • 4 Megabytes of audio: Approximately 35 to 45 minutes to transfer.
  • 16 Megabytes of audio: Over two to three hours to transfer.

Any transmission error or dropped packet during a long transfer could freeze the hardware or force a complete restart. This extreme slowness made SDS a last resort for gigging musicians and studio engineers, ultimately driving the industry toward much faster parallel solutions like SCSI and the SCSI Musical Data Interchange (SMDI) protocol in the 1990s.
