Polyphonic Audio to MIDI: AI Model Limitations

Automated polyphonic audio-to-MIDI conversion uses machine learning to transcribe complex, multi-layered audio recordings into editable musical notes. While modern deep learning algorithms have significantly advanced music transcription, current models still encounter fundamental technical hurdles. These systems frequently struggle with harmonic interference, expressive human performance nuances, multi-instrument separation, and poor generalization beyond specific instruments like the piano.

Harmonic Overlap and Pitch Disambiguation

The most significant physical limitation in polyphonic transcription is the collision of harmonic overtones. In polyphonic music, multiple notes sound simultaneously, causing their respective harmonic series to interlock. When two notes form common musical intervals—such as octaves, perfect fifths, or unisons—their overtones occupy identical frequency bins. Current neural networks often fail to determine whether a detected frequency peak belongs to the fundamental pitch of a higher note or an upper harmonic of a lower note, resulting in phantom notes or missed fundamentals.

Timbre Interference and Multi-Instrument Separation

Current polyphonic transcription models perform best in single-instrument, homogeneous settings, particularly solo piano. When presented with multi-instrumental arrangements, such as a rock band or an orchestral ensemble, the task becomes exponentially more difficult. Models struggle to perform joint source separation and pitch detection simultaneously. When instruments share frequency spectra, AI often attributes notes to the incorrect MIDI track or conflates multiple instrument layers into a single garbled stream.

Capturing Micro-Expressivity and Articulation

MIDI captures not just pitch, but performance dynamics, including velocity, continuous pitch bends, vibrato, and articulation. Machine learning systems excel at binary classification (identifying whether a note exists or not), but struggle to accurately map performance subtleties:

  • Velocity Accuracy: Estimating how hard a note was struck depends heavily on mastering, compression, and distance from the microphone, leading models to assign inaccurate velocity values.
  • Pitch Variations: Continuous pitch bends, glissandos, and vocal or string vibrato are often incorrectly transcribed as rapid successions of distinct semitones rather than smooth pitch-wheel data.
  • Onset and Offset Precision: Detecting the exact start (onset) and end (offset) of a note is difficult with non-percussive sounds, such as bowed strings, woodwinds, or pads with long attack and release envelopes.

Reverberation and Acoustic Interference

Real-world audio contains room reflections, reverberation, and environmental noise. Reverb causes frequency energy to linger long after an instrument stops producing sound. AI models frequently misinterpret decaying reverberant energy as sustained notes, leading to smeared note durations or false onsets. While algorithms can be trained on clean, isolated stems, their accuracy drops significantly when applied to live recordings or fully mastered commercial tracks.

Lack of Diverse Training Datasets

Supervised learning requires paired data: raw audio aligned with precise ground-truth MIDI files. The majority of existing public datasets, such as MAESTRO, consist predominantly of classical piano recorded on specialized acoustic-digital hybrid pianos. Generating accurately aligned polyphonic data for other instruments—such as distorted guitars, expressive vocals, or full ensembles—is labor-intensive and technically challenging. Consequently, models overfit to piano mechanics and acoustic profiles, limiting their transferability to diverse music genres.
