Why MIDI Keyboards Use Fixed Release Velocity

Most standard plastic synthesizer keybeds transmit a default MIDI release velocity of 64 because measuring key-lift speed requires additional hardware, precise mechanical tolerances, and increased manufacturing costs that most synth patches do not require. While downward strike velocity is essential for expressive playing, capturing the speed at which a key returns to its resting position demands a more complex sensor setup than the inexpensive dual-contact rubber membranes found in budget and mid-tier controllers. Consequently, manufacturers hardcode the MIDI midpoint value of 64 to ensure predictable performance across connected sound engines.

How Standard Keybeds Measure Note-On Velocity

A conventional plastic keybed relies on a pair of conductive rubber dome switches beneath each key: an upper switch and a lower switch. When a player presses a key, it strikes the upper contact first and then the lower contact. The keyboard’s micro-controller measures the time elapsed between these two contact closures. Fast traversal indicates a hard strike (high velocity), while slow traversal indicates a gentle press (low velocity).

The Challenge of Measuring Key Return Speed

In theory, the same two switches could measure release speed by timing the break from the bottom contact to the upper contact. In practice, cheap plastic spring-action keys produce poor data on the return stroke. Factors such as plastic flexing, pivot friction, mechanical bounce, and the player’s finger lifting off the key faster than the key mechanism can follow make standard two-contact measurements highly erratic.

To measure release velocity accurately, keyboards typically require a third sensor (a "triple-sensor" action) or continuous optical sensing. Triple-sensor mechanisms place an intermediate contact between the upper and lower switches, allowing the processor to track bidirectional movement with high precision. Incorporating these sensors substantially increases the component count, calibration complexity, and retail price of the instrument.

Why 64 is the Universal Default

Under the MIDI specification, velocity values range from 0 to 127. The value 64 represents the exact center of this range, functioning as a neutral default. When a keyboard lacks the hardware to measure return velocity, its firmware is programmed to transmit Note-Off events paired with a fixed release value of 64 (hexadecimal 0x40).

Transmitting 64 prevents erratic behavior in software synths and sound modules. If a controller transmitted a minimum value like 0 or a maximum value like 127 by default, it could cause patches with release-velocity mappings to cut off abruptly or ring out indefinitely.

Sound Design and Practical Utility

The decision to omit release-velocity sensing also comes down to demand. The vast majority of synthesizer patches rely entirely on the amplitude envelope’s Release stage (the "R" in ADSR), which is controlled globally by a dedicated knob or slider rather than per-note performance data.

Release velocity is primarily useful in high-end acoustic piano libraries (to simulate the damper falling back onto the string at varying speeds) and physical modeling synthesis. Because standard plastic keybeds are predominantly used for electronic lead sounds, basslines, pads, and sequencing where key-up articulation is rarely mapped, manufacturers prioritize low production costs and reliable Note-On response over bidirectional velocity sensing.
