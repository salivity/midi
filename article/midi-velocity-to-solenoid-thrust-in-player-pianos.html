MIDI Velocity to Solenoid Thrust in Player Pianos

Modern automated player piano systems, such as the Yamaha Disklavier, translate digital MIDI velocity values into expressive acoustic performances by converting numeric data into precise electromagnetic force. This process relies on a combination of velocity-curve mapping, pulse-width modulation (PWM) driver circuits, specialized solenoids, and real-time optical sensor feedback. By continuously measuring and adjusting the physical movement of individual keys and hammers, these systems bridge the gap between standard MIDI data and the complex, non-linear mechanical physics of an acoustic piano action.

1. Velocity Parsing and Dynamic Mapping

When a MIDI file or live stream sends a "Note On" message, it includes a velocity value ranging from 0 to 127 (or up to 1,023 in high-resolution MIDI formats). In digital audio, velocity typically controls volume, but in an acoustic piano, volume is purely a function of final hammer speed when striking the string.

Because each key on a piano has a different physical weight, inertia, and friction profile—from heavy bass keys to lighter treble keys—a raw MIDI velocity cannot simply apply a uniform electrical charge. The internal CPU uses instrument-specific calibration curves and lookup tables (LUTs) to calculate the precise target hammer velocity required for that specific key to produce the intended dynamic level, whether pianissimo or fortissimo.

2. Converting Velocity into Solenoid Current

The mechanical actuator beneath each key is a linear solenoid consisting of a wire coil and a movable ferromagnetic plunger. To propel the plunger upward against the piano key (or wippen), the system must generate a magnetic field.

The magnetic force (\(F\)) exerted on the plunger is proportional to the electric current flowing through the coil and the number of turns in the wire:

  • Pulse-Width Modulation (PWM): Microcontrollers drive high-speed MOSFET switches using high-frequency PWM. By altering the duty cycle, the system controls the average voltage and effective current delivered to the coil.
  • Current Profiling: Rather than supplying a single continuous jolt, the driver sends a precisely profiled pulse. A high initial current overcomes the static inertia and friction of the key mechanism, followed by an adjusted hold-and-decay phase to let the hammer travel naturally under momentum toward the string.

3. Closed-Loop Optical Feedback

An open-loop solenoid system cannot guarantee dynamic accuracy due to environmental changes, temperature shifts, action wear, and repetitive-strike heating in the coils. Systems like the Yamaha Disklavier overcome this through closed-loop servo control utilizing continuous optical sensors:

  • Hammer and Key Sensors: Optical shutter sensors monitor key position and measure the exact speed of the hammer shank just before it strikes the string.
  • Real-Time Corrections: The system compares the actual measured hammer velocity against the target velocity demanded by the MIDI input. If the hammer is moving too slowly or too quickly, the controller dynamically fine-tunes the pulse width or corrects the calibration profile in real time for subsequent strikes.

4. Plunger Trajectory and Release

To preserve natural acoustic tone, the solenoid plunger must never push the hammer directly into the string; it must replicate a human finger by accelerating the key just enough to let the hammer "escapement" mechanism release the hammer into free flight.

Immediately before the hammer strikes the string, the solenoid cuts power, allowing the plunger to drop out of the way. Damping pads and reverse-voltage suppression circuits (flyback diodes or active quenching) eliminate residual magnetic flux, preventing the plunger from bouncing or interfering with the key's return stroke during rapid musical repetitions.
