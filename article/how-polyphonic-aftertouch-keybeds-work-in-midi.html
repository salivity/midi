How Polyphonic Aftertouch Keybeds Work in MIDI

Polyphonic aftertouch keybeds generate independent pressure values for each depressed key by integrating dedicated per-key sensors, high-speed analog-to-digital scanning hardware, and individual MIDI message serialization. While traditional channel aftertouch uses a single sensor strip across the entire length of the keyboard to send a uniform modulation value, modern polyphonic aftertouch systems isolate the physical mechanics of every individual key. This article details the hardware sensors, internal scanning processes, calibration algorithms, and MIDI protocol implementations that make real-time, independent per-note pressure modulation possible.

Individual Sensor Technologies Under the Keys

To measure pressure independently, the keybed eliminates the shared global sensor ribbon and places an isolated sensing element directly beneath the travel path of every individual key. Modern instruments primarily use one of four sensor technologies:

  • Force-Sensing Resistors (FSRs): Individual conductive polymer arrays change electrical resistance when compressed. Once the key reaches the bottom of its standard travel (the key bed), further downward force directly compresses the FSR, producing a variable resistance proportional to the applied pressure.
  • Electromagnetic / Inductive Sensors: Used in advanced synthesis keybeds (such as ASM's Polytouch technology), each key features an individual inductor coil or magnetic pickup. Instead of physical compression, these sensors measure the subtle proximity shift of a metal or magnetic component on the key bottom, allowing continuous, wear-free contact sensing beyond the key bottom.
  • Capacitive Sensing: Conductive plates placed beneath each key track variations in capacitance as a moving plate on the key approaches or compresses a dielectric layer, delivering high-resolution distance and force feedback.
  • Optoelectronic Sensors: Paired infrared emitters and photo-detectors measure varying levels of reflected or interrupted light as the key is depressed past the bottom threshold.

Scanning Matrices and Analog-to-Digital Conversion

Because an 88-key keyboard with independent aftertouch requires monitoring 88 discrete analog values alongside standard velocity switches, modern keybeds rely on specialized scanning routines:

  1. Multiplexing: The sensors are wired into an analog multiplexer matrix (rows and columns) or read by dedicated multi-channel Analog-to-Digital Converters (ADCs).
  2. High-Speed Interrogation: An embedded micro-controller unit (MCU) or Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) scans the keybed at rates typically exceeding 1,000 Hz (1 millisecond per complete scan). This high refresh rate prevents audible latency between the physical finger pressure and sound engine response.
  3. Active Threshold Detection: The MCU only measures aftertouch on keys that are actively registered as "held down" via their primary travel switches. Once a key passes the initial key-on threshold, the MCU monitors the corresponding secondary sensor for additional compression or proximity changes.

DSP Filtering, Calibration, and Smoothing

Raw analog pressure readings are inherently susceptible to noise, mechanical tolerances, and uneven playing dynamics. Before sending data out over MIDI:

  • Calibration Offsets: The firmware stores calibration tables to normalize sensor variations across the keyboard, ensuring that 50 grams of extra pressure on a low C generates the same digital response as 50 grams on a high C.
  • Hysteresis and Debounce: Digital filters prevent rapid jitter around dynamic changes, smoothing the transition between static resting weight and deliberate finger pressure.
  • Curve Shaping: Players can select linear, exponential, or logarithmic response curves within the instrument’s operating system, remapping the raw analog voltage into a musically responsive control curve.

Generating the MIDI Messages

Once the filtered pressure value is calculated, the MCU packages the data into standardized MIDI packets.

MIDI 1.0: Polyphonic Key Pressure

Under classic MIDI 1.0, polyphonic aftertouch uses its own dedicated status byte:

  • Status Byte: 0xAn (where A represents Polyphonic Aftertouch, and n represents the MIDI channel 0–15).
  • Data Byte 1: Note Number (0–127), designating which specific note is being modulated.
  • Data Byte 2: Pressure Value (0–127), representing the level of force applied.

Unlike channel pressure (which sends a 2-byte message containing only status and pressure), polyphonic aftertouch requires a 3-byte message for every pressure increment on every active key. The internal processor prioritizes bandwidth, dynamically thinning redundant identical values to prevent choking standard 31.25 kbaud 5-pin DIN MIDI connections.

MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) and MIDI 2.0

In instruments using MPE, independent pressure is often routed as Channel Pressure (0xDn) distributed across multiple floating MIDI channels (typically channels 2 through 16), reserving Channel 1 as a global manager.

Under native MIDI 2.0, polyphonic aftertouch is handled by standard 32-bit Universal MIDI Packets (UMP). This protocol extends pressure resolution from the standard 7-bit (128 steps) limit of MIDI 1.0 up to a 32-bit value, allowing virtually continuous, step-free modulation for software and hardware instruments capable of receiving high-definition control data.
