Why Korg and Roland Used Different TRS MIDI Pinouts

When hardware synthesizers and sequencers became increasingly compact in the 2010s, manufacturers shifted away from bulky 5-pin DIN connectors to space-saving 3.5mm TRS (Tip, Ring, Sleeve) jacks. However, because the MIDI Manufacturers Association (MMA) did not formally standardize 3.5mm MIDI until 2018, industry giants Korg and Roland independently developed conflicting wiring schemes—subsequently labeled Type A and Type B. This divergence was driven by the absence of a shared specification, differing hardware design philosophies regarding signal polarity, and the historical mirroring ambiguities of DIN socket schematics.

The Physical Constraint and the Standards Vacuum

Traditional MIDI hardware relies on standard 5-pin DIN connectors dating back to the 1983 MIDI 1.0 specification. Although the DIN jack uses five pins, MIDI communication relies on only three conductors:

  • Pin 4: Current source (VCC, typically +5V or +3.3V pulled high through a resistor)
  • Pin 5: Current sink (Data / UART TX)
  • Pin 2: Ground / Shield (used for RF/EMI shielding, not a signal return)

When designing low-profile devices like the Korg SQ-1 sequencer, the Korg Electribe series, and Roland’s Boutique line, the physical depth and height of a 5-pin DIN jack proved too large. Both companies turned to standard stereo 3.5mm TRS jacks as a drop-in physical replacement. However, because neither company coordinated with the other or waited for a formal specification update, each engineering team devised its own mapping between the three active DIN pins and the TRS terminals.

Roland's Conventional Electrical Logic (Type B)

Roland—along with companies like Arturia and Novation in their early compact releases—adopted what became known as Type B:

  • Tip: Pin 4 (Source / VCC)
  • Ring: Pin 5 (Data / Sink)
  • Sleeve: Pin 2 (Shield / Ground)

Roland’s engineering choice followed the traditional conventions of audio and control voltage (CV) electronics. In typical studio patch environments, the "Tip" conductor is almost universally treated as the "hot," positive, or primary signal carrier, while the "Ring" carries the return, low, or inverted signal. By assigning Pin 4 (the voltage source) to the Tip and Pin 5 (the data-switching return) to the Ring, Roland’s engineers mirrored established analog routing logic onto the digital current loop.

Korg's Layout and Mechanical Considerations (Type A)

Conversely, Korg—coinciding with early modular manufacturers such as Make Noise—implemented the inverse arrangement, designated as Type A:

  • Tip: Pin 5 (Data / Sink)
  • Ring: Pin 4 (Source / VCC)
  • Sleeve: Pin 2 (Shield / Ground)

Korg’s layout was largely influenced by PCB trace efficiency and the internal geometry of standard optocoupler circuits. In an optocoupled MIDI input stage, Pin 5 connects directly to the cathode of the input optocoupler’s internal LED, while Pin 4 feeds the anode. Depending on how an engineer models the current loop, Pin 5 can be viewed as the active transmitting "data" conductor while Pin 4 merely provides a steady reference voltage. Prioritizing the active data line to the primary conductor (the Tip) was an equally defensible design choice that reduced trace crossover on crowded compact circuit boards.

Schematic Perspective Errors in Early Adoption

A secondary contributing factor was the historical ambiguity inherent to DIN-5 documentation. Schematics from the 1980s frequently caused confusion regarding whether pin numbering applied to the front mating face of the female socket or the rear solder lugs of the male plug.

Because Pin 4 and Pin 5 sit symmetrically on opposite sides of the centered ground (Pin 2), an engineer viewing a socket from behind sees a horizontal mirror image of someone viewing it from the front. Without a centralized TRS standard to enforce orientation, independent development teams interpreting these connections from scratch frequently reversed pins 4 and 5 relative to one another.

Eventual Standardization

Because MIDI operates as a directional current loop rather than a shared-voltage bus, an adapter using Type A cannot communicate with an adapter using Type B; the current cannot flow through the input optocoupler in reverse, rendering the connection dead.

Recognizing that market fragmentation was frustrating consumers and creating hardware incompatibility, the MIDI Manufacturers Association stepped in during 2018 to publish official TRS-MIDI specifications. The MMA officially recognized Type A (the Korg standard) as the universal industry standard for all future 3.5mm MIDI implementations. Consequently, modern devices produced across the industry have largely phased out Type B in favor of universal Type A compatibility.
