How Ikutaro Kakehashi Led Rivals to Adopt MIDI

In the early 1980s, the electronic music industry faced a critical fragmentation problem as manufacturers built instruments using incompatible, proprietary communication systems. Roland founder Ikutaro Kakehashi recognized that this lack of interoperability choked industry growth and frustrated musicians. Rather than protecting Roland's proprietary technology, Kakehashi spearheaded an unprecedented collaborative initiative, convincing fierce domestic competitors—including Yamaha, Korg, and Kawai—to join American innovators in co-developing and adopting the Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) as a universal, royalty-free standard.

By 1981, synthesizers relied on crude analog control voltages or exclusive digital formats, such as Roland’s Digital Communication Bus (DCB), which prevented gear from different brands from communicating. Kakehashi realized that a universal interface was necessary to unlock the full potential of electronic music. After discussions with Dave Smith and Chet Wood of Sequential Circuits, who proposed a basic standard called the Universal Synthesizer Interface, Kakehashi committed to refining the concept into a globally viable protocol. To achieve this, he knew he had to secure buy-in from Japan's dominant hardware manufacturers.

The Japanese musical instrument market was fiercely competitive, characterized by deep rivalries among Roland, Yamaha, Korg, and Kawai. Kakehashi leveraged his immense personal respect and diplomatic credibility within the Japanese engineering community to bring these rivals to the negotiation table. His primary argument was pragmatic: expanding the overall market for electronic instruments through interoperability would benefit all manufacturers far more than fighting for control of a fragmented, proprietary landscape.

A cornerstone of Kakehashi's strategy was ensuring that the standard remained open and royalty-free. He insisted that no single manufacturer—including Roland—should hold patents, licensing rights, or commercial leverage over the protocol. This commitment eliminated suspicions of corporate self-interest and established mutual trust among the rival companies. Japanese engineers were able to collaborate on equal footing with one another and with their American counterparts.

Throughout 1982, engineering teams from Roland, Yamaha, Korg, Kawai, and Sequential Circuits met regularly to debate and finalize technical specifications. The Japanese consortium, heavily guided by Kakehashi's engineering principles, pushed for vital technical improvements. They mandated opto-isolators to eliminate ground loops and electrical hum, finalized a reliable data transfer rate of 31.25 kilobaud, and adopted the sturdy, affordable 5-pin DIN connector.

The turning point occurred at the January 1983 Winter NAMM Show, where a Roland Jupiter-6 and a Sequential Circuits Prophet-600 successfully connected and communicated across a single cable. Because Kakehashi had already solidified the alliance in Japan, Yamaha and Korg immediately integrated the protocol into their upcoming production pipelines. Yamaha's release of the massively popular DX7 synth later that year, complete with MIDI functionality, cemented the protocol’s worldwide dominance. Kakehashi’s vision transformed bitter rivals into collaborators, resulting in a technical standard that has powered music production for over four decades.
