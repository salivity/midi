MIDI Clock vs Korg Analog Sync Explained

Synchronizing electronic instruments relies on keeping multiple sequencers, drum machines, and synthesizers running at identical tempos. While both standard MIDI clock and Korg analog sync achieve tempo coordination, they operate using completely different technologies, signal types, and resolution rates. Standard MIDI clock is a digital protocol operating at 24 Pulses Per Quarter Note (PPQN) embedded with explicit transport commands, whereas Korg analog sync relies on simple analog electrical voltage pulses running at a much lower resolution of 2 PPQN.

Digital Data vs. Electrical Pulses

The fundamental difference lies in how timing information is transmitted. Standard MIDI clock is a digital serial communication standard. It transmits hexadecimal byte packets—specifically the real-time byte 0xF8—over standard 5-pin DIN or USB connections. The receiving synthesizer's microchip reads this digital stream, decodes the timestamps, and calculates the target tempo.

In contrast, Korg analog sync uses raw electrical signals transmitted via standard 3.5mm TS or TRS audio cables. There is no digital messaging or data stream. The master device simply sends a short DC voltage pulse (typically a +5V spike lasting around 15 milliseconds) to advance the slave device by one step. Because it is an audio-frequency electrical pulse, analog sync can be recorded directly onto an audio track in a DAW or manipulated like CV/Gate control voltage.

Clock Resolution (PPQN)

Resolution determines how frequently timing updates are sent relative to musical time:

  • MIDI Clock (24 PPQN): MIDI synchronizes at a fixed standard of 24 Pulses Per Quarter Note. This high resolution allows receiving equipment to calculate microtiming, tempo changes, swing, and fractional subdivisions down to 96th notes smoothly.
  • Korg Analog Sync (2 PPQN): Korg standardized its modern analog sync (found on the Volca, Monologue, Minilogue, and Electribe series) at 2 Pulses Per Quarter Note. This means the device sends only one pulse every eighth note, which equates to a trigger on every alternate 16th-note sequencer step.

Because of this 12-to-1 ratio difference, sending a standard MIDI clock into a device expecting Korg sync will cause the receiving unit to run twelve times faster than intended unless the pulse rate is stepped down.

Transport and Playback Control

MIDI clock includes dedicated transport bytes to command connected hardware:

  • 0xFA (Start): Tells all connected units to reset playback to the beginning and start.
  • 0xFB (Continue): Resumes playback from the current position.
  • 0xFC (Stop): Halts playback.
  • Song Position Pointer (SPP): Informs downstream hardware of the exact measure to align with.

Korg analog sync lacks integrated playback commands. On standard mono connections, a slave device advances purely in response to receiving a voltage spike. If the pulses stop, playback stops; when pulses resume, playback continues from the next step rather than returning to step one. While some devices implement a stereo (TRS) connection where the second channel functions as a start/stop trigger gate, analog sync universally lacks the precise positioning data inherent to MIDI.

Interoperability

Because MIDI is digital communication and Korg sync is an analog voltage trigger, the two formats cannot interface passively using simple pin-to-plug adapters. Translating between them requires active hardware—such as a dedicated MIDI-to-analog converter, a synthesizer equipped with both protocols (such as a Korg Minilogue), or an audio interface outputting programmed click tracks—to bridge the gap between serial code and voltage pulses.
