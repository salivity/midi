Voice Stealing: Last-Note vs First-Note Priority

MIDI tone generators rely on voice stealing algorithms to manage polyphony limits when the number of incoming notes exceeds the hardware or software's available sound channels. This article breaks down the operational differences between Last-Note Priority and First-Note Priority algorithms, analyzing how each manages voice allocation, how they alter the performer's tactile experience, and where each approach is best applied in musical production.

The Mechanism of Voice Stealing

Every MIDI synthesizer or tone generator has a maximum polyphony—the number of individual audio voices it can generate simultaneously. When a musician plays more notes than the available polyphony allows, the system must execute a voice allocation policy to decide whether to drop an existing voice to accommodate the new input or to reject the new input altogether.

Last-Note Priority (LNP)

Under a Last-Note Priority algorithm, the most recently played note is always guaranteed to sound. When the polyphony limit is reached:

  • Voice Reallocation: The generator actively terminates ("steals") one of the currently sounding voices to free up a channel for the newly triggered note. Most implementations steal the oldest active note or the voice closest to completing its release envelope.
  • Performer Feel: LNP feels responsive and immediate. Fast runs, arpeggios, and melodic solos never suffer from silent key presses, even if a sustain pedal is held down and sustaining dozens of previous notes.
  • Drawbacks: Sustained foundation notes, such as a low bass note or an evolving pad chord, can be abruptly cut off if the player triggers too many subsequent notes above them.

First-Note Priority (FNP)

Under a First-Note Priority algorithm, older notes take absolute precedence over new notes. When the polyphony limit is reached:

  • Voice Preservation: The generator refuses to interrupt active voices. Instead, any incoming MIDI Note-On message that exceeds the voice count is dropped or ignored until an existing voice is released and its envelope fully decays.
  • Performer Feel: FNP can feel "choked" or unresponsive during rapid playing because keys played after reaching the limit produce no sound.
  • Drawbacks: If a musician plays a dense chord with a sustain pedal held, subsequent melodic attempts will be completely silent until the pedal is released or earlier notes naturally fade out.

Key Functional Differences

Feature Last-Note Priority First-Note Priority
New Note Behavior Always sounds immediately Silenced/ignored if voice limit is reached
Voice Termination Drops the oldest/quietest active voice Never interrupts active voices
Common Musical Use Lead synthesizers, expressive piano, fast solos Drone synthesizers, specific emulation of vintage hardware, pedal-point arrangements
Player Perception Fluid, dynamic, and responsive Rigid, stable, but potentially obstructive

Summary

The fundamental difference lies in whether the synthesizer favors the player's past actions or their present actions. First-Note Priority prioritizes the integrity of established harmony and sustained tones at the expense of new input. Last-Note Priority favors real-time responsiveness and forward musical motion, ensuring that the latest musical idea is always heard.
