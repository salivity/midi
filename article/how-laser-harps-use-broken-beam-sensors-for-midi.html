How Laser Harps Use Broken Beam Sensors for MIDI

A laser harp produces music by replacing physical strings with visible laser beams that trigger digital sounds when touched or interrupted. At the core of this interaction is an optoelectronic system pairing photo sensors with a microcontroller. When a performer breaks a light beam, the sudden disruption in optical intensity is detected by a photo sensor, converted into an electronic trigger, and translated within microseconds into a standardized MIDI Note On event sent to a synthesizer or digital audio workstation (DAW).

Optical Interruption and Photodetection

The instrument typically uses high-visibility lasers split into multiple beams using either a beam splitter or a high-speed scanning galvanometer. In a closed-frame or "through-beam" laser harp design, a dedicated photo sensor—usually a photodiode or phototransistor—is aligned directly opposite each laser beam path.

Under normal resting conditions, the laser continuously illuminates the sensor, creating an active photodiode current that maintains a constant voltage level across an input circuit. When a performer places a hand in the path of the beam, the light connection is physically obstructed. This interruption causes the current passing through the photosensitive element to plummet almost instantaneously.

Threshold Detection and Signal Conditioning

Photo sensors generate analog signals that fluctuate naturally based on ambient room light. To prevent false triggers and ensure reliability, the raw sensor voltage passes through a signal-conditioning stage:

  1. Comparator Circuits: An analog comparator compares the sensor’s current voltage against an adjustable reference voltage. The moment the beam is broken and the sensor voltage drops below the designated threshold, the comparator’s output flips instantly from low to high (or vice versa).
  2. Hardware Interrupts: This clean, digital square wave connects directly to the hardware interrupt (IRQ) pins of an onboard microcontroller (such as an ARM Cortex or AVR-based processor). Hardware interrupts bypass the standard linear execution of code, prompting the processor to immediately execute a dedicated interrupt service routine (ISR) the microsecond the beam is severed.

MIDI Note On Generation and Transmission

Once the interrupt is triggered, the microcontroller determines the exact identity of the interrupted beam based on the pin or scanning index that registered the drop in light. The firmware then constructs an asynchronous MIDI message consisting of three 8-bit bytes:

  • Status Byte (0x90): Specifies the "Note On" command across a designated MIDI channel.
  • Data Byte 1: Represents the pitch value (0–127), which is mapped to the specific laser beam that was interrupted.
  • Data Byte 2: Represents velocity (0–127). In fixed-velocity designs, this is preset (often at 127 or 100), while more advanced harps calculate velocity by measuring the transit speed of an incoming hand or via integrated distance sensors.

Because the optical transition and the subsequent microcontroller interrupt happen in sub-millisecond timeframes, the MIDI Note On command is transmitted over USB or standard 5-pin DIN connections with virtually zero perceivable latency, resulting in an immediate audio response. When the performer removes their hand and the sensor detects the restored laser beam, a corresponding MIDI Note Off command (0x80) is transmitted to silence the note.
