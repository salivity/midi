Why Yamaha DX7 Only Sends Velocity 100

Released in 1983 as one of the first commercial instruments to feature the newly minted MIDI specification, the original Yamaha DX7 is infamous for transmitting a maximum MIDI velocity of only 100 rather than the full 127 standard. This shortcoming was primarily caused by Yamaha’s reliance on proprietary internal scaling conventions, early firmware design oversights, and a focus on the synth's self-contained FM sound engine rather than its role as a master controller for external gear.

The Dawn of the MIDI Standard

When the DX7 was in development, the MIDI 1.0 specification was still being drafted and finalized by a coalition of synthesizer manufacturers. Because the standard was in its absolute infancy, there was little established precedent for how hardware should map physical key strikes to outgoing MIDI data. Yamaha was racing to bring the first digital FM synthesizer to mass market, and strict interoperability with third-party MIDI instruments was secondary to getting the keyboard to function properly with its own internal sound engine.

The 0–99 Yamaha Architecture

Yamaha’s engineers designed the DX7 around a digital parameter structure that almost exclusively used values from 0 to 99. Operator output levels, envelope generator rates, and parameter depths were all displayed and programmed within this two-digit range.

When programming the firmware's keybed scanning routines, the developers mapped the velocity lookup tables around this familiar system. The keyboard scanner measured the time between two mechanical key contacts to calculate strike speed, and the internal lookup table scaled that measurement to an output limit of 100 (with some units rarely reaching 102 under extreme, key-breaking force) instead of utilizing the full 7-bit MIDI range of 0 to 127.

Internal Voicing vs. External Transmission

Internally, the DX7’s FM tone generator did not strictly suffer from this choice. The synth's factory patches and sound engine were specifically voiced and tuned to react with full brightness and dynamic expression within the 0 to 100 velocity ceiling. As a standalone instrument, the keyboard felt expressive and complete.

The problem became glaringly apparent only when players began connecting the DX7 to newer external sound modules, samplers, and drum machines from other manufacturers like Roland, Akai, and Korg. Because these secondary devices expected incoming velocities up to 127 to trigger their brightest filter cutoffs, loudest samples, and hardest velocity layers, the DX7 caused external modules to sound noticeably quiet, dull, and unresponsive.

Legacy and Solutions

Musicians were forced to find workarounds for the DX7's limited output. The most popular vintage fix was installing the third-party "E!" expansion board from Grey Matter Response, which overhauled the DX7's firmware and provided true 0–127 velocity scaling. Other players relied on standalone MIDI processing hardware or software velocity curves in digital audio workstations to scale the incoming 0–100 signal up to 127. Yamaha eventually recognized the standard and resolved the issue entirely in 1987 with the release of the updated DX7 II.
