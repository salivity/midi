How Drum Modules Convert Dual Piezos to MIDI

This article explains the technical pipeline that electronic drum modules use to convert analog voltage spikes from dual-piezo head and rim sensors into distinct, accurate MIDI note messages. It covers signal generation, analog conditioning, analog-to-digital conversion, advanced crosstalk and ratio algorithms, and the final translation into MIDI velocity and note numbers.

1. Signal Generation and Mechanical Coupling

A dual-piezo drum pad features two independent piezoelectric transducers: one mounted centrally or peripherally against the drumhead, and another adhered to the shell or rim bracket. Striking either surface induces mechanical stress on the piezoelectric crystals, generating alternating current (AC) voltage spikes proportional to the impact's force.

Because the head, shell, and rim are mechanically coupled, an impact on the head causes physical vibrations to travel into the rim, and vice versa. Consequently, striking either surface causes both piezos to generate concurrent electrical signals. The module must actively determine the drummer's actual point of impact rather than simply listening for whichever sensor crosses a voltage threshold.

2. Analog Conditioning and Rectification

Before the module’s processor can read the sensor outputs, the raw AC voltages enter an analog front-end (AFE) circuit. Raw piezo outputs can swing between negative and positive extremes, sometimes exceeding 30 to 50 volts on hard strikes, which would damage microcontrollers.

The conditioning stage performs three main tasks:

  • Clamping and Protection: Diodes clip excessive positive and negative voltages to keep signals within safe operating limits (typically 0V to 3.3V or 5V).
  • Rectification: Negative voltage phases are either inverted (full-wave) or blocked (half-wave) so the signal consists entirely of positive direct-current (DC) voltage envelopes.
  • Filtering: Low-pass RC filters strip high-frequency mechanical noise and radio frequency interference, leaving a clean waveform envelope that tracks the strike’s transient attack.

3. Sampling and Scan Time (Peak Detection)

The conditioned analog signals route to an Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) linked to the module's Digital Signal Processor (DSP) or microcontroller. The ADC samples both channels simultaneously at high frequencies (typically tens of kilohertz).

When an incoming voltage breaches a predetermined baseline threshold, the module initiates an internal timer known as the "scan time" (typically between 1.5 and 4 milliseconds). Because the initial transient spike is not always the highest peak of the vibration waveform, the module monitors the incoming waveform throughout this scan window to detect the absolute maximum peak voltage, which dictates the strike's velocity.

4. Head vs. Rim Discrimination Algorithms

To prevent false triggering and assign a single, correct MIDI note, the module executes real-time discrimination algorithms during and immediately following the scan window:

  • Differential Amplitude (Ratio Analysis): The processor compares the peak amplitudes of both channels. If the head sensor amplitude significantly exceeds the rim sensor amplitude, the strike is categorized as a head hit. Conversely, if the rim sensor registers a much higher peak than the head, it is categorized as a rim hit.
  • Phase and Time-of-Flight: Vibration travels through the drum pad's physical structure at finite speeds. The sensor closest to the impact point generates a voltage rise slightly earlier (often fractions of a millisecond) than the secondary sensor. The processor tracks which channel initiated the transient first.
  • Rimshot Detection: A rimshot occurs when the stick contacts the head and the rim simultaneously. In this scenario, both the head and rim piezos produce substantial, synchronized voltage peaks. If both channels register above predefined independent thresholds within the same scan window, the module suppresses both individual head and rim voices and instead selects a dedicated rimshot assignment.

5. Masking and Retrigger Cancellation

Once an event is confirmed, the module deploys a retrigger cancellation mask. Acoustic vibrations resonate across the drumhead and shell for tens of milliseconds after a strike. To prevent these decaying oscillations from triggering false secondary notes, the module ignores any subsequent peaks on both channels for a defined duration (mask time), unless a subsequent peak exceeds a dynamically decaying threshold curve representing a deliberate, fast follow-up stroke (such as a buzz roll).

6. MIDI Message Generation

After the module identifies the strike type and calculates the final peak voltage:

  1. Velocity Mapping: The peak voltage value is mapped to a standard 7-bit MIDI velocity value (1 to 127). The module uses selectable curves (e.g., linear, logarithmic, exponential, or s-curve) to translate physical playing dynamics into realistic musical responses.
  2. Note Assignment: The module correlates the determined zone (Head, Rim, or Rimshot) with its assigned MIDI note number (for example, General MIDI Snare Head = Note 38, Rimshot = Note 40, Rim Click = Note 37).
  3. Transmission: The module constructs a standard three-byte MIDI Note-On packet (Status byte indicating channel and note-on, Data Byte 1 for Note Number, Data Byte 2 for Velocity) and transmits it via 5-pin DIN MIDI or USB-MIDI to an external sound module or digital audio workstation (DAW). After a set duration, a corresponding Note-Off command is generated to close the event.
