How E-Drum Modules Convert Piezo Pulses to MIDI Velocity

Electronic drum modules translate the physical impact of a drumstick into a digital musical performance by capturing the analog voltage spikes generated by piezoelectric sensors, filtering and measuring the resulting waveform, and algorithmically mapping that electrical energy to a standard MIDI velocity value between 1 and 127. This process relies on high-speed analog-to-digital conversion, precise peak detection, dynamic curve modeling, and crosstalk suppression to ensure that the module responds accurately to a drummer's subtle ghost notes as well as full-force strikes.

1. The Piezoelectric Voltage Spike

Inside an electronic drum pad, a small ceramic piezoelectric element sits beneath the striking surface, typically coupled to a foam transfer cone. When struck, the mechanical stress deforms the piezo crystal, causing an instantaneous accumulation of electrical charge. This produces an alternating current (AC) voltage spike. The amplitude of this electrical transient is directly proportional to the force of the strike: a soft tap produces a signal measuring a few millivolts, while an aggressive rimshot can generate an initial burst exceeding 50 to 90 volts.

2. Analog Conditioning and Rectification

Because microcontrollers and digital signal processors (DSPs) operate on low-voltage direct current (typically 0 to 3.3V or 5V), the raw piezo signal cannot be fed directly into an analog-to-digital converter (ADC).

Before conversion, the module conditions the signal through several hardware stages:

  • Clamping/Protection: Zener diodes or protection circuits clip excessive voltages to prevent module damage.
  • Rectification: Because the piezo generates a vibrating AC waveform with positive and negative swings, a full-wave or half-wave rectifier converts the signal into a unipolar (positive-only) voltage envelope.
  • Buffering and Filtering: Low-pass filters strip out high-frequency noise and mechanical resonances that could trigger false readings.

3. ADC Sampling and Peak Detection (Scan Time)

Once the conditioned signal enters the module's ADC, the processor continuously monitors the input at sample rates typically ranging from 20 kHz to 100 kHz or higher.

The module identifies a hit using a strict detection sequence:

  • Threshold Detection: The processor ignores resting electrical noise until the incoming voltage crosses a user-defined threshold. Crossing this threshold starts the evaluation clock.
  • Scan Time (Peak Window): A physical drum pad does not reach peak voltage instantly; the mechanical shockwave requires roughly 1 to 4 milliseconds to propagate through the mesh, foam, and sensor. Drum modules employ a configurable parameter known as "Scan Time" (or "Peak Time"). During this narrow window, the module samples the rising waveform repeatedly to track the absolute highest voltage peak (V_max) or integrates the area beneath the waveform curve.

4. Velocity Curve Transformation

Raw physical impact energy does not scale linearly with human perception of volume. If modules used a 1:1 linear translation from voltage to MIDI velocity, pads would feel either overly sensitive or excessively stiff.

Modules apply digital transfer functions (velocity curves) to the measured peak:

  • Linear: A direct, proportionate translation from the minimum threshold to the maximum voltage limit.
  • Logarithmic: Amplifies lower-voltage strikes to make soft notes more audible while requiring significantly harder hits to reach the maximum velocity of 127.
  • Exponential: Requires deliberate physical force to leave the lower velocity range, prioritizing fine control over ghost notes.
  • S-Curve: Compresses both the lowest and highest ends, maximizing dynamic resolution in the middle playing range.

The output of the selected mathematical curve maps onto the standard 7-bit MIDI integer scale (0 to 127), where 0 represents note-off and 1–127 represent dynamic volume and timbre intensity.

5. Retriggering and Crosstalk Suppression

A vibrating drumhead continues to oscillate after the initial strike, creating decaying secondary voltage peaks that could accidentally trigger multiple false notes.

To prevent this, the module initiates a "Mask Time" (or retrigger cancel window) immediately after registering the peak. During this time (typically 8 to 30 milliseconds), all subsequent incoming voltage pulses on that channel are ignored or heavily attenuated.

Simultaneously, the DSP evaluates inputs across all other drum pad channels. Using matrix algorithms (crosstalk cancellation), the module compares simultaneous voltage spikes across pads on the same drum rack. By identifying the input with the steepest rise time and highest amplitude, the module assigns the MIDI strike to the primary pad while discarding the weaker sympathetically transmitted vibrations on adjacent pads.
