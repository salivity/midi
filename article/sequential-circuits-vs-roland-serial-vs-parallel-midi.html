Sequential Circuits vs Roland: Serial vs Parallel MIDI

In the early 1980s, the development of the Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) was shaped by a critical technical debate between American manufacturer Sequential Circuits and Japanese titan Roland. At the center of the discussion was whether the new universal standard should utilize parallel or serial data transmission. While parallel communication offered the raw bandwidth and instantaneous data transfer favored for complex polyphonic performance, Sequential Circuits championed a low-cost serial alternative to ensure broad adoption. This article details the engineering debate between both companies, examining how concerns over transmission speed, cable design, latency, and hardware costs ultimately forged the modern MIDI specification.

The Push for Parallel Communication

Before a universal standard was established, manufacturers relied on proprietary, often analog, voltage control systems or early digital solutions. Roland had already implemented its Digital Communication Bus (DCB), a semi-parallel interface used to link devices like the Juno-60 and Jupiter-8 to sequencers.

Parallel transmission transfers multiple bits of data simultaneously across separate physical conductors. Proponents of a parallel standard argued that:

  • Bandwidth and Latency: Transmitting full 8-bit data bytes simultaneously meant minimal transmission delay. Multiple notes played as a chord could be sent virtually at the exact same moment.
  • Elimination of "Flamming": In a slow digital stream, chords played together can suffer from audible staggering (flamming), where notes trigger sequentially rather than simultaneously. Parallel connections effectively bypassed this bottleneck.
  • Processing Simplicity: Synthesizer microprocessors could feed data directly from their internal parallel buses to the external port without needing UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver-Transmitter) chips to serialize the data.

Because of these benefits, several engineers initially favored parallel buses similar to IEEE-488 or multi-pin computer printer cables.

Sequential Circuits and the Case for Serial Transmission

Dave Smith and Chet Wood of Sequential Circuits presented the preliminary concept of the Universal Synthesizer Interface (USI) at the Audio Engineering Society (AES) convention in late 1981. Sequential pushed aggressively for an asynchronous serial design, where data bits travel sequentially over a single pair of conductors.

Sequential’s technical rationale centered on practicality and cost:

  • Cable and Connector Costs: Parallel systems required bulky, shielded multi-conductor ribbon cables and expensive multi-pin connectors. Sequential argued that adding costly parallel ports would prevent smaller manufacturers and budget synthesizers from adopting the protocol.
  • Interference and Distance: Parallel lines are susceptible to crosstalk and signal degradation over longer runs, requiring rigid shielding and short cable limits. Serial data could be transmitted reliably over longer, more flexible, and cheaper cables.
  • Physical Footprint: A serial port required only a small, standard connector, keeping synthesizer rear panels uncluttered and simplifying physical production.

The Technical Compromise

Roland did not object to serial transmission in principle—having seen the manufacturing expenses and logistical limitations of their own multi-pin DCB cables—but Roland’s engineers, led by Tadao Kikumoto and company founder Ikutaro Kakehashi, raised major technical concerns about Sequential's original serial proposal.

Sequential had initially suggested a modest transfer rate of 19.2 kbaud (kilobaud) utilizing standard quarter-inch audio jacks. Roland argued that 19.2 kbaud was too slow to handle polyphonic performance data, pitch bend, and real-time control changes without noticeable latency. Additionally, quarter-inch jacks could cause brief electrical shorts when plugged in while powered on, risking damage to internal circuitry and producing ground loops that introduced audio hum.

To resolve the debate and ensure a serial protocol could realistically replace the performance advantages of a parallel bus, Roland proposed key modifications:

  1. Increased Transmission Speed: The speed was raised to 31.25 kbaud. This precise rate was selected because it divided evenly into the standard 1 MHz, 2 MHz, and 4 MHz clock rates of the microprocessors used in Japanese and American synthesizers, allowing hardware to process serial data without dedicated, expensive baud-rate generators.
  2. Opto-Isolation: To prevent ground hum and electrical feedback over serial cables, Roland mandated galvanic isolation using optocouplers on the receiver side.
  3. Connector Choice: The standard adopted a robust 5-pin DIN connector, utilizing a 5-milliamp current loop system to prevent accidental shorts and eliminate interference over moderate cable distances.

The Conclusion of the Debate

Sequential Circuits conceded to Roland's engineering revisions regarding speed, connector type, and opto-isolation, while Roland and other Japanese manufacturers (Yamaha, Korg, and Kawai) fully endorsed Sequential's serial architecture over a parallel one.

By raising the serial baud rate to 31.25 kbaud, the protocol proved fast enough to render chord latency imperceptible to human ears, neutralizing the core argument for a parallel bus. The resulting specification was unveiled to the public as the Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) in 1983, successfully balancing low manufacturing costs with the performance reliability required for professional music production.
