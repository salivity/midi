Shorting Ground to Signal on a 5-Pin MIDI Socket

An accidental solder bridge connecting the shield ground pin to a signal pin on a 5-pin DIN MIDI socket will disrupt or entirely stop data transmission, but it rarely causes catastrophic hardware destruction. Because the standard MIDI hardware specification relies on a 5-volt, 220-ohm current loop with built-in optoisolation, the exact consequences depend on whether the short occurs on a MIDI OUT, MIDI THRU, or MIDI IN port, and which specific signal pin is bridged.

Understanding the 5-Pin MIDI Pinout

In standard MIDI 1.0 hardware implementations:

  • Pin 1: Unused.
  • Pin 2: Ground / Cable Shield.
  • Pin 3: Unused.
  • Pin 4: Positive Signal (+5V current source via a 220-ohm resistor on outputs).
  • Pin 5: Data Signal (Current sink/UART data line via a 220-ohm resistor on outputs).

MIDI does not transmit data as conventional voltage levels referenced to a local ground. Instead, it operates as a 5 mA balanced current loop where Pin 4 and Pin 5 form a complete loop that turns an optocoupler LED on and off inside the receiving device.

Effects on a MIDI OUT or THRU Socket

On a MIDI OUT or THRU port, Pin 2 is directly connected to the sending device’s circuit ground to provide a return path for the cable's external shield.

  • Shorting Pin 2 to Pin 4 (Source): Pin 4 supplies the loop voltage (+5V) through an inline 220-ohm current-limiting resistor. If a solder bridge connects Pin 4 directly to Pin 2 (Ground), the +5V rail is pulled directly to ground through the 220-ohm resistor. This draws approximately 22.7 mA of current, dissipating roughly 114 mW of heat across the resistor. Most standard 1/4-watt resistors handle this load without burning out, but the port cannot supply current to the external loop. Result: Total loss of MIDI transmission.
  • Shorting Pin 2 to Pin 5 (Data): Pin 5 is the data line modulated by the device's UART or an inverter buffer, also protected by an inline 220-ohm resistor. Pulling Pin 5 permanently to ground holds the data line in a constant "active" (logic zero/space) state. Result: Complete communication failure, framing errors, or an unresponsive receiver.

Effects on a MIDI IN Socket

The MIDI specification explicitly dictates that Pin 2 must be left disconnected (floating) on a MIDI IN port. This design maintains galvanic isolation between gear, preventing hum, noise, and ground loops.

  • If Pin 2 is properly floating: If the socket follows standard specification and Pin 2 has no PCB trace connected to it, shorting Pin 4 or Pin 5 to Pin 2 has no functional effect. Pin 2 simply acts as a dead termination point, and the data loop continues operating normally.
  • If Pin 2 is tied to local ground (Non-compliant design): Some poorly designed equipment connects Pin 2 of the MIDI IN port directly to chassis or digital ground. If a solder bridge shorts Pin 4 or Pin 5 to Pin 2 in this scenario:
    • Galvanic isolation is broken. Ground currents from the transmitting gear can flow into the receiver, potentially creating audible hum or ground loops in attached audio interfaces.
    • Signal diversion: The loop current that should energize the receiving optocoupler's internal LED is diverted directly to ground, causing missing notes, corrupted bytes, or a completely non-functional input.

Remediation

Because the current-limiting resistors prevent immediate semiconductor burnout, fixing the issue is straightforward. Inspecting the socket with magnification, applying desoldering braid or a solder sucker to clear the bridge, and verifying continuity with a multimeter will immediately restore the port to normal working order.
